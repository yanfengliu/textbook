Order costs, and something else pays

Chapter 1 said, in one line, that order does not maintain itself and that a living thing is a thing that keeps paying. Chapter 4 then spent the payment on every page: pumps bailing sodium out at a third of a cell's budget, flippases dragging lipids between leaflets, a protist's contractile vacuole squirting out the pond it cannot keep from arriving. Every one of those was charged to a molecule the chapter could not explain and twice promised to hand on. Here is the account.

Start with the word itself, because it is used so loosely that it has stopped meaning anything. Energy is the capacity to cause change — to move something, to heat something, to rearrange atoms into a different pattern. It is not a substance, not a fluid and not a fuel. It comes in two kinds, and every other form named in this book is one of the two wearing a particular coat. Kinetic energy is the energy of motion, and the most important case in biology is the least visible one: heat is nothing but the random motion of molecules, so a warm object is one whose molecules are moving faster. Potential energy is energy held by position or arrangement — a weight on a shelf, a charge held apart from its opposite, an ion held at a concentration the membrane of Section 4.7 will not let it keep. And the form this chapter is about, chemical energy, is potential energy held in the arrangement of atoms and electrons in a molecule. When that arrangement changes to a more stable one, the difference is released, and that is all a chemical reaction is.

Two rules govern every transaction, and the second of them was worked out by engineers trying to build better steam engines rather than by anyone looking at a cell. The first law is that energy is conserved: it can be converted from one form to another and moved from one place to another, and it cannot be made or destroyed. Nothing alive produces energy. A mitochondrion does not make energy and neither does a power station; each takes energy in one form and hands it on in another, and the books balance exactly. An organism that spends more in a day than it takes in makes up the difference out of itself, which is what losing weight is.

Erwin Schrödinger gave a famous set of lectures in Dublin in 1943, published the next year as What Is Life?, in which he said that an organism lives by feeding on "negative entropy". He added a note admitting that the technical quantity he meant was free energy, and that he had avoided the term because it says nothing to a general audience. The lectures are what sent several of the physicists who founded molecular biology into the subject.

The second law is the awkward one, and it is the reason this section exists. Every transfer or conversion of energy leaves the universe with more entropy than it had before. Entropy is usually glossed as disorder, which is close enough to be useful and loose enough to mislead. What it actually measures is how many different arrangements of a system's parts would look the same from outside. There is exactly one way for a deck of cards to be in factory order and an unimaginable number of ways for it to be shuffled, so shuffling happens by itself and unshuffling does not. Nothing forbids the shuffle coming out in order; it is simply that overwhelmingly more of the available arrangements are untidy ones. A change happens of its own accord when it leads to a state there are more ways of being in.

Now look at a cell against that. A growing bacterium takes a soup of small molecules and assembles them into proteins, each with its own sequence, folded into one shape out of the astronomical number available; into membranes with their two faces kept different for the life of the cell; into a chromosome copied base by base. The number of arrangements has gone down, spectacularly. Every organism that has ever lived has been a local, sustained, very large decrease in entropy. It is the most natural objection to the second law that anybody raises, and it was raised seriously in the nineteenth century.

The answer is a single word, and it is in the statement of the law: the universe. The second law constrains an isolated system, and no cell is one. A cell is an open system, with matter and energy crossing its boundary continuously; it lowers its own entropy by raising its surroundings' entropy more. There is no exemption and no loophole. The books balance, and they balance with a great deal to spare. Figure 5.1 keeps the ledger where you can watch it: build the polymer, and the bar for the surroundings runs away faster than the bar for the compartment falls. Seal the boundary — make it genuinely isolated — and the building stops within moments, and then the structure already built comes apart.

What is it that gets exported? Heat, mostly, along with small disordered molecules. Every step a cell takes is a little inefficient, and the inefficiency leaves as random molecular motion which spreads out into the surroundings and is never coming back. The quantities involved are not subtle. A person taking in about 2000 kilocalories a day is taking in some 8400 kilojoules, and an adult whose weight is steady stores none of it: all 8400 leave again, and almost all of it as heat. Divide by the 86 400 seconds in a day and you get a little under 100 joules a second. You are a hundred-watt heater that talks. It is why a full lecture theatre grows warm, and why the arithmetic of a spacecraft has to include the people in it.

Heat is energy that has lost its address. A joule in a stretched spring or a charged gradient is pointed somewhere and can be aimed; the same joule as heat is shared out among molecules moving in every direction at once, and to collect it again you would need somewhere colder to pour it into.

And here is the consequence that runs through the rest of the chapter, so it is worth stating flatly. Heat cannot be spent again inside a cell. Getting work out of heat requires a temperature difference — that is what a steam engine is — and a cell is all at one temperature, to a fraction of a degree, throughout. A hot spot a few nanometres across would even out in less time than any chemistry takes. So the moment a cell's energy has turned into heat, it has left the economy for good. Nothing downstream can use it, and the only thing left to do with it is get rid of it.

Which finally explains Chapter 1's slogan. Section 1.6 said that energy flows through an ecosystem once while matter goes round and round, and drew it in Figure 1.7 without saying why the two behave so differently. This is why. A carbon atom is not degraded by being eaten: it is the same atom in the grass, in the rabbit and in the air, and it can go round for ever. Energy is degraded at every transfer, because every transfer sheds some as heat, and heat leaves. That is also where Section 1.6's rule of a tenth comes from: it is not a biological constant but an accumulation of thermodynamic tax, paid at every step, which is why a food chain of six links is not found anywhere and one of three or four is ordinary.

The second law does not forbid a cell; it prices it. A cell is an open system that lowers its own entropy by raising its surroundings' entropy more, and it pays the difference in heat — which, at one temperature throughout, can never be spent again. That is why energy passes through the living world once and matter goes round it indefinitely.

What decides which way a reaction goes

The second law is exactly right and almost useless to work with. To apply it you would have to account for the entropy of the entire universe before and after every reaction, which nobody can do. What is needed is a quantity that belongs to the system alone — to the beaker, or to the cell — and that nevertheless tells you what the universe is going to do. Josiah Willard Gibbs supplied it in the 1870s, and it is the single most useful number in biochemistry.

Free energy, written G, is the part of a system's energy that is available to do work at constant temperature and pressure. What matters is never G itself but the change in it, ΔG, over the course of a reaction:

ΔG = ΔH − TΔS

ΔH is the heat the reaction gives out or takes in, T the absolute temperature, and ΔS the change in the entropy of the system. Every term is measurable in the beaker, and yet the sign of ΔG tells you what the universe's entropy will do, because the heat term divided by the temperature is precisely the entropy the surroundings will gain. The rule is then as simple as it could be. If ΔG is negative the reaction runs by itself and is exergonic; if ΔG is positive it does not, and the reverse reaction runs instead, so the forward one is endergonic; and if ΔG is zero the reaction is at equilibrium. Notice what equilibrium means here, because Section 4.3 has already said it: not that nothing is happening, but that the two directions are happening equally fast, so nothing further changes. ΔG = 0 is the dynamic equilibrium of a membrane at which molecules cross as often as before, with no net movement either way.

Both terms matter, and a reaction can be driven by either. Many of the reactions in a cell that run downhill do so because they give out heat. But a change that takes heat in can still happen if it disorders the world enough, and the book has already met the case: the hydrophobic effect of Section 2.3. When hydrocarbon tails cluster together, the tails themselves become more ordered, not less. What wins is the water — the molecules that were caged around each isolated tail are released into the general disorder of the liquid, and there are far more arrangements available to them there. The membrane of Section 4.1 assembles because of a positive ΔS in the water, not because the lipids attract one another. Figure 5.2 lets the two terms be set separately, and the interesting thing to do with it is to find the temperature at which an entropy-driven reaction switches on, since the entropy term is the one that carries a T in front of it.

Now the mistake that costs most readers this chapter. A reaction with a large negative ΔG is often said to be spontaneous, and that word has done enormous damage, because in ordinary English it means "happening soon" and in thermodynamics it means only "not requiring a continuing input of work". The two are unrelated. Glucose and oxygen, sitting together in a bowl, have a ΔG of about −2870 kilojoules per mole waiting to be released; a spoonful of sugar left in the air will still be sugar next year. Diamond is thermodynamically unstable with respect to graphite, and diamonds do not turn grey. Thermodynamics says which way, never how fast.

That gap is not an inconvenience. It is the condition for there being any such thing as a cell. If every reaction with a negative ΔG went as soon as it could, a cell would be a puff of carbon dioxide: everything in it is combustible and it is surrounded by oxygen. What makes controlled chemistry possible is that exergonic reactions mostly do not happen until something lets them, and Sections 5.5 to 5.7 are about what a cell does with that. Free energy says what is permitted. Enzymes decide what actually occurs.

The ° in ΔG°′ means the standard state and the prime means "as a biochemist defines it": pH 7, water at its own concentration rather than one mole per litre, and magnesium present. Chemists use a different convention, which is why the value quoted for ATP hydrolysis in a chemistry text may not match the one here.

One more refinement, and it is the one that makes the whole subject work. Tables quote a standard free energy change, ΔG°′, measured with every reactant and product at one mole per litre. That is a fixed property of the reaction, and it is a condition no cell is in for any substance at all. The real ΔG depends on how far the actual mixture is from its equilibrium point:

ΔG = ΔG°′ + RT ln ([products] / [reactants])

R is the gas constant and T the absolute temperature, and the product RT at body temperature is 2.6 kilojoules per mole — the same 2.6 that Section 2.2 used as the thermal jostling available at 37 °C, because it is the same quantity. A natural logarithm turns each tenfold change in the ratio into 2.3 of those, so every factor of ten between products and reactants is worth 5.9 kilojoules per mole. That number is worth keeping. It is also, divided by the charge on a mole of ions, the 61.5 millivolts per tenfold difference that Section 4.7 used to work out a resting potential: the same constant wearing electrical clothes.

So a cell does not have to look for reactions that are favourable by their chemistry. It can make an unfavourable one run by holding the product concentration low, and the cheapest way to hold a product low is to have another enzyme waiting to take it away. This is why metabolism is arranged in pathways. A step whose ΔG°′ is slightly positive runs perfectly well in the middle of a sequence, because the step after it removes the product before it can build up. A pathway is not a queue of separate reactions. It is one reaction pulling the next.

Which leaves the chapter's bleakest sentence and its most useful one. Equilibrium is death, and not as a metaphor. A cell at equilibrium has every gradient flat, every ratio at its equilibrium value and ΔG zero everywhere, and there is by definition no work left in it: that is a precise description of a corpse, and reaching it is what dying consists of. What a living cell maintains instead is a steady state — concentrations that hold still because matter is flowing through them at a constant rate, which is Chapter 1's homeostasis seen as chemistry. A candle flame keeps its shape the same way, and for the same reason it goes out when the wax runs out.

A reaction runs by itself when ΔG is negative, and ΔG depends on the concentrations as much as on the chemistry: every tenfold ratio is worth 5.9 kilojoules per mole at body temperature. So a cell keeps reactions going not by choosing favourable ones but by never letting them reach equilibrium — and a negative ΔG says only that a reaction may happen, never that it will.

The cell spends one molecule

Almost every transaction in every cell that has ever been examined is paid for with the same molecule. A bacterium in a hot spring, an oak, a jellyfish and the cell reading this sentence all use ATP, and have done since before the three domains of Section 1.7 separated. Section 2.8 introduced it in a paragraph and sent it here; four sections of Chapter 4 spent it without explanation. This is what it is.

Structurally there is nothing exotic about it. ATP is a nucleotide — the same three parts Section 2.8 named, adenine joined to the sugar ribose — with a chain of three phosphate groups attached to the sugar instead of the single phosphate a nucleotide in RNA carries. The molecule that stores the genetic information and the molecule that pays for everything are built from the same kit, which is one of the better pieces of evidence that both are very old. The reaction a cell uses is the removal of the outermost phosphate by water:

ATP + H2O ⇌ ADP + Pi

Pi is the chemist's shorthand for a free inorganic phosphate ion, and ADP is what is left. Under standard conditions this releases 30.5 kilojoules per mole. There is a second version, used where more pull is needed, in which two phosphates come off together as a linked pair, which is then itself split — that arrangement releases rather more and is how a cell drives the building of DNA and RNA against a stubbornly unfavourable equilibrium.

Now the question the section is really for. Why does taking a phosphate off release anything? The standard answer is that ATP contains a "high-energy phosphate bond", often drawn with a squiggle; the phrase was coined by Fritz Lipmann in 1941 and it has been misleading students ever since. Section 2.5's table of functional groups used a version of it, saying that a phosphate's bonds to further phosphates carry energy the cell can spend. As shorthand that is serviceable. As an account of what happens it is wrong in a way that matters, because it invites a picture of energy stored inside a bond like air in a tyre, waiting to be let out by breaking it.

Breaking a bond always costs energy. Making one always releases it. That is not a rule about phosphates; it is what a bond is. Section 2.2 measured it: a carbon–carbon bond takes about 350 kilojoules per mole to break, an oxygen–hydrogen bond about 460, and nothing is ever got by breaking one on its own. A reaction releases free energy when the bonds made in the products are, taken together with everything else that changes, better than the bonds broken in the reactants. The energy comes out of the comparison, not out of a bond.

A better phrase, and one some textbooks now use, is phosphoryl transfer potential: a measure of how readily a compound will hand its phosphate group to something else. It puts the emphasis where it belongs — on the transfer, and on what is waiting at the other end — rather than on a bond that is supposed to be holding something.

So what is better about ADP and free phosphate than about ATP? Four things, and not one of them is a property of the bond that broke. First, the three phosphate groups of ATP each carry a negative charge and are strung together within a few tenths of a nanometre of each other; like charges repel, and the molecule is under electrostatic strain, which Section 2.8 described as being crowded unhappily together. Splitting one off relieves it. Second, the free phosphate ion that leaves spreads its charge evenly over four equivalent oxygens, an arrangement with more ways of being itself than it had while it was attached — a real and substantial stabilisation. Third, the water around the two products holds them better than it held ATP, in exactly the way Section 2.3 described water closing around a dissolved ion. And fourth, there are now two molecules where there was one, which is an entropy gain of the kind Section 5.1 described. ATP is not a compressed spring. It is a molecule whose products are more comfortable than it is.

Next, what it is actually worth, which is the number Section 4.7 used and promised this chapter would justify. The 30.5 kilojoules per mole is the standard figure, measured with ATP, ADP and phosphate all at one mole per litre. A cell holds them nowhere near that, and nowhere near each other: roughly 5 millimoles per litre of ATP, about a tenth as much ADP, and about 5 millimoles per litre of phosphate. Put those into the last section's equation. The ratio of products to reactants is (0.0005 × 0.005) ÷ 0.005, which is 5 × 10−4; its natural logarithm is −7.6; and multiplied by the 2.6 kilojoules per mole of RT that is −19.6. So

ΔG = −30.5 − 19.6 ≈ −50 kJ/mol

and that is where Section 4.7's fifty came from. The extra twenty kilojoules is not chemistry. It is the cell holding its ATP some two thousandfold further from equilibrium than the standard state — twenty kilojoules is a little over three of Section 5.2's decades — and paying continuously to keep it there. A cell's ATP is worth more than the table says for exactly the reason a charged battery is worth more than a flat one.

Which brings us to the word currency, which is used so often about ATP that it has stopped being an argument. It is a good word, and what it means is that ATP is change in the pocket and not money in the bank. Do the arithmetic. A cell holds ATP at about 5 millimoles per litre; the cells of a 70 kg adult hold something like 25 litres of water between them, so the whole body's stock of ATP is around an eighth of a mole, and since a mole of ATP weighs 507 grams that is about 60 grams. Against that stock, the 8400 kilojoules a day of Section 5.1, at 50 kilojoules a mole, is about 170 moles — some 85 kilograms of ATP made and spent in a day, which is more than its owner weighs. Divide the daily spend by the stock and the whole pool turns over roughly 1400 times a day: once a minute, and far faster than that in a working muscle.

Treat those as orders of magnitude rather than measurements — not every joule a body spends passes through ATP, and the concentrations above vary between one tissue and another. But the shape of the answer is not in doubt, and it is the point of the section. A cell does not store energy as ATP. It stores energy as fuel, in glycogen and fat, and as the gradients of Section 4.7; ATP is what it converts those into at the moment of spending, and it holds about a minute's worth. That is why a poison which stops ATP being made kills in minutes rather than days, and why a cell has no reserve to fall back on when it does.

Figure 5.3's second scene answers the obvious next question: why this molecule and not another? Compounds that carry a transferable phosphate can be ranked by how much their hydrolysis releases, and the ranking is wide.

ATP sits in the middle, and that is the whole of its qualification for the job. A compound above it can hand a phosphate to ADP and so make ATP; ATP can hand a phosphate to anything below it. A currency has to be worth less than the things that buy it and more than the things it buys, and a molecule at either end of that ladder would be useless as one. Muscle keeps a reserve exactly one rung up: creatine phosphate, which does nothing except re-phosphorylate ADP as fast as it is produced, and which carries a sprinter through the first few seconds before anything slower has started.

ATP releases free energy not because a bond is holding it but because the products are more stable: the crowded charges are relieved, the freed phosphate spreads its charge, water holds both products better, and one molecule has become two. Inside a cell it is worth about 50 kilojoules a mole rather than the table's 30.5, because the cell holds it far from equilibrium — and it is a currency, not a store, since the whole pool is spent and remade about once a minute.

Nothing is paid for with heat

A cell needs a great many things to happen that will not happen. It needs to join glutamate to ammonia, which costs 14.2 kilojoules per mole. It needs to push sodium out against the gradient of Section 4.6. It needs to drag a vesicle along a microtubule. Every one of those is endergonic, and Section 5.2 was categorical: an endergonic reaction does not run. So how is anything bought?

Take first the answer nearly everybody gives, because it is wrong in an instructive way. Hydrolyse some ATP nearby, the story goes, and the energy released drives the reaction. It cannot. Energy released from ATP into the general surroundings becomes heat, and Section 5.1 established that heat inside a cell, all of which is at one temperature, can do no work whatever. Two reactions in the same drop of cytoplasm are no help to one another. A beaker of ATP and a beaker of glutamate, mixed, will hydrolyse the ATP and leave the glutamate exactly as it was, a little warmer.

Energy coupling is the real answer, and it is a mechanical requirement rather than a figure of speech. Two reactions are coupled when they share a chemical intermediate — when the product of the first is the reactant of the second — so that they are not two reactions at all but two steps of one. Only then do their free energy changes add, and only the sum has to be negative. The shared intermediate is what makes coupling a fact about molecules rather than an accounting convenience, and providing one is the reason a protein has to be involved: something must hold both reactants in the same place and pass the intermediate from one step to the next before it can wander off.

The worked case is the glutamine the section opened with, made by an enzyme called glutamine synthetase. It does not do the reaction it appears to do. It does two:

glutamate + ATP → glutamyl phosphate + ADP

glutamyl phosphate + NH3 → glutamine + Pi

The intermediate is glutamyl phosphate, which never leaves the enzyme, and the sum of the two steps is the reaction the cell wanted: glutamate and ammonia to glutamine, with an ATP gone. Add the free energies — the +14.2 the amide bond costs, and the −30.5 the ATP releases — and the overall change is −16.3 kilojoules per mole, comfortably downhill. Notice what the phosphate did. It was not a parcel of energy delivered to the reaction. It was attached to the glutamate, producing a compound partway up Figure 5.3's ladder and therefore much readier to react than glutamate was. The cell did not add energy to anything. It made an unstable intermediate, and then let it fall.

That is the general pattern, and it has a name: phosphorylation. Almost everything ATP does, it does by putting its outer phosphate somewhere.

Onto a substrate, and the substrate becomes reactive: that is chemical work, and glutamine synthetase is the case. Onto a transport protein, and the protein changes shape: that is transport work, and Section 4.6 described it without naming it. Read that description again with this section in hand — the pump takes the phosphate from ATP onto itself, and it is the attachment and removal of that phosphate that switches the protein between its two shapes. The phosphorylated protein is the shared intermediate. The pump is not a machine that uses energy from ATP; it is a machine that is part of the ATP's own reaction.

Not every case transfers the phosphate away. A motor protein binds ATP in its own site and splits it there, and it is the sequence of shapes that binding, splitting and releasing put the protein through that produces the step. The principle is unchanged: the movement is not driven by the chemistry from a distance, it is part of the chemistry, and the two cannot be separated even in principle.

And onto a motor, which is mechanical work: Section 3.6's kinesin takes one 8 nm step along a microtubule for each molecule of ATP it splits, and the cargo it hauls goes where diffusion would never have taken it. Three kinds of work — chemical, transport, mechanical — and one currency for all three, which is why a cell needs only one. Figure 5.4 puts them side by side and lets the coupling be broken: choose an uphill job, choose a source, and choose whether the two are joined through an intermediate or merely running in the same beaker. Uncoupled, the ATP is spent and the job does not get done, and the heat readout says where it went.

The pump is worth doing properly, because it is the one case where every number is already on the page. Chapter 4 gave the gradients — sodium at 145 millimoles per litre outside and 12 inside, potassium at 140 inside and 4 outside — and a resting potential of about 70 millivolts, inside negative. One turn of the pump moves three sodium ions out and two potassium ions in, both uphill, and each ion has to be paid for twice over: once against its concentration, once against the voltage.

The potassium row is the interesting one: moving a positive charge inwards is helped by the negative interior, so the voltage pays back about three-quarters of what the concentration gradient charges, and two potassium ions together cost about a third of what one sodium ion does. And the totals are very close together. The pump uses about nine-tenths of what it is given, which is a remarkable figure for any machine and is the arithmetic behind the remark at the end of Section 4.7: make the gradients steep enough in the laboratory and the pump runs backwards and synthesises ATP. When two sides of a coupled reaction are within a few kilojoules of each other, which way it goes is decided by the conditions, not by the machinery. Chapter 7 is built entirely on a machine run in that reversed direction, in the folded inner membrane of Section 3.5.

Nothing in a cell is paid for with heat. Two reactions are coupled only when they share a chemical intermediate, and then their free energy changes add. What ATP does is put a phosphate group somewhere — on a substrate, on a pump, or in a motor's own site — making something less stable and therefore willing to do the next thing; and a coupled machine working near the limit can be made to run either way.

An enzyme changes the route, not the destination

Section 5.2 left a gap wide enough to put the rest of biology in. A reaction can be exergonic and still not happen. Why not?

Because getting from the reactants to the products is not a slide; it is a climb followed by a descent. Along the way there is an arrangement worse than either end — bonds half broken and half formed, charges where no charge wants to be, atoms pushed together that repel. That worst moment is the transition state, and the free energy needed to reach it from the reactants is the activation energy. Only a molecule that happens to have that much energy at the instant of collision gets over. Everything else bounces off unchanged.

The size of the obstacle is easy to underrate. At 37 °C the average molecule carries about 2.6 kilojoules per mole of thermal energy, the figure Section 2.2 used to decide which bonds break by themselves. A barrier of 50 or 100 kilojoules per mole is therefore climbed only by molecules far out in the tail of the distribution, and how far out decides everything. The relationship is exponential, and a single conversion is worth remembering: at body temperature, every 5.9 kilojoules per mole taken off the barrier multiplies the rate by ten — the same 5.9 that Section 5.2 got for a tenfold concentration ratio, because it is the same RT and the same logarithm.

Not every enzyme is a protein. A handful of RNA molecules catalyse reactions, and one of them matters more than all the rest: the site in a ribosome that joins one amino acid to the next is made of RNA, not protein. So the machine that makes every protein is not itself one, which is a strong hint about what came first.

An enzyme is a catalyst: it speeds a reaction without being consumed and without changing where the reaction ends up. It does so by offering a completely different route between the same two places, through a different and much lower transition state. The scale of what that is worth is hard to credit. One step in making the nucleotides of Section 2.8 is catalysed by an enzyme whose reaction, left to itself in water at ordinary temperatures, has a half-life of about 78 million years. With the enzyme it takes a matter of milliseconds. The speed-up is of the order of 1017, which by the conversion above means the enzyme has taken about 100 kilojoules per mole off the barrier — less than a third of a carbon–carbon bond. It has not done anything violent. It has found a way round.

What it does, concretely, is bind. Every enzyme has an active site: a pocket, usually small, formed by the folding of the chain rather than by a run of neighbouring residues, so that side chains from distant parts of the sequence come together to line it. Section 2.7's tertiary structure is what makes an active site, which is why a single substitution far from the site can still ruin it. The molecule that binds there is the substrate, held by many of the weak interactions of Section 2.2 acting at once — which is where both the binding and the fussiness come from, since a dozen weak contacts only all fit if the shape is right.

Held there, the substrate is worked on in several ways at once. Two substrates that would have to find each other by luck are held next to each other in the right orientation, which is most of what a collision has to achieve. The site strains and bends the substrate towards the shape of the transition state. It supplies a microenvironment the bulk solution cannot — water excluded, a local charge presented, an effective pH quite different from the cytosol's. And side chains lend a proton and take it back, or form a brief covalent bond of their own, splitting one difficult step into two easy ones. Many enzymes cannot do their chemistry with side chains alone and carry a cofactor: a zinc ion, an iron atom, a magnesium ion held in the site. An organic one that binds loosely and leaves again carrying something is a coenzyme, and most coenzymes are made from vitamins, which is what a vitamin is.

Underneath all of those is one idea, put most clearly by Linus Pauling in the 1940s: an enzyme binds the transition state more tightly than it binds the substrate. Whatever it does chemically, the effect is to stabilise the worst moment of the journey, and stabilising the top of a barrier is the same thing as lowering it. Emil Fischer's lock and key of 1894 was the first picture and is not quite right, because a rigid site could not strain anything. Daniel Koshland's correction in 1958 was induced fit: the site closes around the substrate as it binds, more glove than lock, which is how an enzyme can be selective and still distort what it has selected.

Now what an enzyme does not do, which is the commonest error in the subject and the reason Figure 5.5 draws the landscape rather than the molecules alone. An enzyme does not change ΔG: the reactants and products are the same molecules at the same energies, and only the route between them has altered. It does not change the position of equilibrium, because lowering the barrier lowers it by exactly the same amount for the reverse reaction — the transition state is the same arrangement whichever side you approach it from — so both rates rise by the same factor and the ratio at which they balance is untouched. An enzyme therefore cannot make an endergonic reaction happen; one that could would be a perpetual motion machine, and a cell that needs an uphill reaction must couple it, as Section 5.4 showed. And an enzyme is not used up and does not appear in the overall equation. It changes when you arrive, never where.

Finally, why enzymes rather than heat? A cell could speed everything up by getting hotter — the rate of a typical reaction roughly doubles for each 10 °C — and this is not a serious option for two reasons. Past about 40 °C the proteins of a human cell begin to unfold, which is Section 2.7's denaturation, so the range available is a few degrees. More fundamentally, heat is indiscriminate. It speeds every reaction in the cell, including all the ones the cell needs not to happen, and a cell full of combustible molecules in oxygen has a great many of those. An enzyme speeds one reaction out of the thousands chemically available and leaves the rest alone. What a cell buys with catalysis is not speed but choice: its metabolism is not what its chemistry could do, but the small subset it has provided an enzyme for, and changing which enzymes are present changes what the cell is.

A reaction is slow because reaching its transition state costs energy. An enzyme lowers that cost by binding the transition state more tightly than the substrate, which multiplies the rate in both directions by the same factor and leaves ΔG and the equilibrium exactly where they were. Heat would do the same job indiscriminately and destroy the proteins; what an enzyme gives a cell is the ability to choose which of its possible reactions actually occur.

How fast, and what slows it down

Catalysis is a claim about rates, so measure them. Fix the amount of enzyme, raise the substrate concentration step by step, and plot how fast product appears. The curve rises steeply at first, bends over, and flattens — and a reader who has been through Chapter 4 has seen it already. Figure 4.5 drew this curve for a glucose carrier in a red cell membrane, and said then that it was exactly the shape of an enzyme's and for exactly the same reason. That reason is now worth stating properly: a fixed number of binding sites, each of which is occupied for a fixed time per customer. Once nearly all of them are busy nearly all of the time, more substrate cannot help.

Two numbers name the curve, and they were given their form by Leonor Michaelis and Maud Menten in 1913. The maximum rate is the ceiling: what the enzyme achieves when every active site is occupied at all times. It depends on how much enzyme is present and on how fast each molecule cycles, so doubling the enzyme doubles it. The Michaelis constant is the substrate concentration at which the rate is half the maximum, and its useful property is that it does not depend on how much enzyme is present. It is a property of the enzyme itself, and roughly — the qualification is real, because it also depends on how fast the enzyme works once bound — it says how low a concentration the enzyme can work at.

Maud Menten was Canadian, one of the first women in Canada to take a medical degree, and could not get a research post at home; she did the work with Michaelis in Berlin in 1912, took a doctorate in Chicago, and spent her career in Pittsburgh. The paper they wrote together is among the most cited in biochemistry.

The third number is the turnover number: how many substrate molecules one enzyme molecule converts per second when it is saturated. The range across biology is enormous, from a few a second to a million. Carbonic anhydrase, which catalyses the reaction at the heart of Section 2.4's blood buffer, is near the top: of the order of a million molecules a second, which is why a red blood cell can load and unload carbon dioxide during the fraction of a second it spends passing through a capillary. Without it the buffer would still work, but far too slowly to breathe with.

Now use the two constants the way a cell does, and it turns into Chapter 4's argument again. Blood glucose is held near 5 millimoles per litre. Most cells take their first step on glucose with hexokinase, whose Michaelis constant is around a tenth of a millimole per litre: it is saturated at all times, so it works flat out whether its owner has eaten or not, which is what you want of a cell that must keep going regardless. The liver uses a different version, glucokinase, whose constant is around ten millimoles per litre — above the blood concentration, so it is never saturated and its rate rises and falls with the blood. The liver can therefore act on how much sugar has arrived. That is the same pair of designs, for the same reason, as the two glucose carriers Section 4.5 compared, and it is not a coincidence: a carrier and an enzyme are the same kind of machine, with a site, a cycle and a ceiling.

Two conditions shift the whole curve. Temperature raises the rate, roughly doubling it per 10 °C, until the enzyme starts to unfold; past that the rate falls off a cliff. The resulting curve is not a bell and it is worth seeing why: the rise is the gentle exponential of molecules clearing a barrier, and the fall is the loss of enzyme. That loss has two parts, and they run on different clocks. At any temperature some fraction of the enzyme is unfolded at that instant and the rest is working; that part is an equilibrium, and cooling folds it straight back. The rest is permanent — a chain left unfolded has time to tangle with its neighbours or settle into something that will not fold again — and it accumulates with every second spent hot. So how far an enzyme has fallen depends on how long it was held there, and a cell, which cannot cool itself down on demand, loses the enzyme for good. An enzyme's optimum is not a temperature it likes. It is the point at which losing enzyme begins to outrun speeding it. pH does something different. The side chains lining an active site must carry the right charge for the chemistry to work, and pH decides whether they do; far from the optimum, the protein denatures as well. So the optimum sits where the enzyme works: pepsin near pH 2 in the stomach, a lysosome's enzymes near pH 5 — which is why Section 4.6's proton pump is in a lysosome's membrane at all — and most enzymes of the cytosol near neutral. Figure 5.6 puts all of that under one set of controls: sweeping the substrate is what draws the curve in the first place, adding enzyme raises the ceiling and leaves the half-way concentration exactly where it was, and pushing the temperature past the enzyme's limit stops the lane altogether. Its temperature slider sweeps both ways, which is the only way to see the shape of a curve; that is the first part of the loss, the one cooling undoes. Press Keep the damage and the second part is switched on as well, so what unfolds while the bench is hot is still unfolded when it is cooled and the ceiling never comes back up — which is the loss Figure 5.5 models on its own.

Which brings us to inhibition, and to most of the pharmacy. An enzyme is the easiest thing in a cell to attack and the most specific target available, and the great majority of drugs and very nearly all poisons are enzyme inhibitors. There are three kinds and they are told apart by one experiment: add more substrate and see whether it helps.

KindWhere it bindsDoes more substrate help?Maximum rateMichaelis constant

Competitive inhibition is a molecule that resembles the substrate closely enough to occupy the site and not closely enough to be converted. It is a race for the site, and more substrate wins it, so the ceiling is unaltered and only the concentration needed to reach half of it rises. Statins work this way on the enzyme that sets how fast a liver cell makes its own cholesterol — and Section 4.8 has already supplied the rest of the story, because a liver cell short of cholesterol responds by putting more LDL receptors into its membrane and taking more out of the blood. The drug lowers blood cholesterol by inhibiting an enzyme that never touches the blood. Methanol poisoning is treated on the same principle in the opposite direction: ethanol competes for the enzyme that would otherwise take the first step in turning methanol into the formic acid that blinds and kills, keeps that enzyme occupied, and the methanol is excreted unchanged.

Non-competitive inhibition binds somewhere else entirely and changes the enzyme's shape, so the active site works badly or not at all. Adding substrate does nothing, because the substrate is not what it is competing with, and the ceiling falls: some fraction of the enzyme is simply out of service however much substrate arrives. This is the same mechanism as the deliberate regulation of Section 5.7, and the only difference between a non-competitive inhibitor and an allosteric one is whether the cell meant it.

Irreversible inhibition forms a covalent bond, and that enzyme molecule is finished; the cell recovers only by making more. Penicillin does this to the enzyme that cross-links the peptidoglycan wall of Section 3.3, and that mechanism explains the odd fact Chapter 3 recorded without explaining: penicillin kills only growing bacteria. An established wall is not being cross-linked, so nothing is being inhibited — but a cell that tries to grow builds a defective wall, and then bursts by the osmosis of Section 4.4. Aspirin does the same to the enzyme that makes prostaglandins, which is why one dose affects a platelet for its whole life. The nerve agents do it to the enzyme that clears the transmitter from a synapse, and the victim's nerves cannot stop firing.

An enzyme's rate rises with substrate and flattens at a ceiling, for the same reason Chapter 4's carrier does: a fixed number of sites with a cycle to complete. The maximum rate says how many sites there are; the Michaelis constant says at what concentration they are half busy, and so whether an enzyme tracks its substrate or ignores it. An inhibitor in the active site can be outcompeted with more substrate; one that binds anywhere else, or binds covalently, cannot.

A pathway that knows when to stop

A cell has some thousands of different enzymes and cannot afford to run them all at once. What it controls is not whether a reaction is chemically possible but how fast each enzyme goes, and it does so by four means working on four different timescales. The fastest of them gets most of this section, because it is what turns an enzyme from a machine into a switch.

Allostery is control of a protein by something binding at a site that is not the active site. The word means "other shape", and that is the mechanism: the protein has two conformations, one in which the active site is well formed and one in which it is not, and it flickers between them. A molecule that binds preferentially to one of the two shifts the balance. Bind to the inactive shape and you are an allosteric inhibitor; bind to the active shape and you are an allosteric activator. Nothing about the substrate has changed and nothing has been blocked. The enzyme has been told something, by a molecule that is not its business, and has changed its mind about how hard to work.

Most allosteric enzymes are built from several subunits, and that gives them a second property. Because the subunits change shape together, the first substrate to bind makes the next one easier — cooperativity — and the rate curve of Section 5.6 is no longer a curve that bends over gently but an S. The practical consequence is worth dwelling on: over a narrow range of concentration, an allosteric enzyme goes from almost off to almost fully on, where an ordinary enzyme would ramp gradually across a hundredfold range. A cell that wants a decision rather than a dial builds one of these. Haemoglobin, which is not an enzyme but works on the same principle, is why your blood can be nearly saturated with oxygen in the lungs and give up most of it in a working muscle, over a difference in pressure that would barely move a binding protein without cooperative subunits.

Now the pattern that the whole section is for. In feedback inhibition, the end product of a pathway is an allosteric inhibitor of the enzyme catalysing the pathway's first committed step. The textbook case is a bacterium making the amino acid isoleucine from threonine in five enzyme-catalysed steps. Isoleucine binds the first of those five enzymes, at a site that has nothing to do with threonine, and switches it off. When isoleucine is plentiful the pathway stops. When the cell has used the isoleucine up, the inhibition lifts and the pathway starts again. Nothing counts anything, nothing measures anything, and there is no controller: the product is its own signal.

Why the first step, and why specifically the first committed one? Because every step after the block is wasted work. Inhibit the last enzyme of five and the four intermediates before it pile up, each of them having cost the cell something to make and each now useless. Inhibit the first and nothing at all is spent. And "committed" matters because the intermediate produced by that step has nowhere else to go; inhibiting an earlier step that feeds several pathways would starve all of them at once. Figure 5.7 shows the whole thing running and lets it be taken apart.

This is Chapter 1's negative feedback, exactly, with molecules in place of nerves, and it is worth mapping onto Section 1.2's loop term by term. The regulated quantity is the concentration of isoleucine. The sensor is the allosteric site on the first enzyme, which reads that concentration by the simple expedient of being bound some of the time. The effector is the enzyme's own activity. And the response opposes the change, which is what makes the loop negative and the state steady. The set point is the part with no obvious counterpart, and the answer is elegant: there is no number written anywhere, and the set point simply is how tightly the allosteric site holds its ligand. An enzyme that binds isoleucine more tightly settles at a lower concentration of it. Evolution sets a set point by adjusting a binding site.

Break the loop and Section 1.2's prediction comes true. Mutate the allosteric site so that the product no longer binds, and the pathway runs regardless of how much isoleucine is present, pouring out an amino acid the cell has no use for. That is not only a thought experiment: feedback-resistant mutants of exactly this kind are how industrial amino acids are made, and the bacteria in those fermenters pour out lysine at a rate no wild organism would tolerate, because their loops have been deliberately cut.

Jacques Monod, Jeffries Wyman and Jean-Pierre Changeux set out the two-state model of allostery in 1965. Monod, who had a taste for the large claim and usually the results to support it, called allostery "the second secret of life" — the first being the genetic code.

The three slower controls can be given a paragraph each. Covalent modification takes seconds to minutes: a kinase transfers a phosphate group from ATP onto a particular residue of a target protein, and a phosphatase takes it off again. A phosphate is bulky and carries two negative charges, so attaching one reshapes the protein around it and switches the protein on or off — Section 5.4's phosphorylation used as a signal rather than as a payment. It is the commonest switch in biology: the human genome carries the instructions for more than five hundred different protein kinases. And it needs the phosphatase as much as the kinase, because a switch that can only be thrown once is not a switch.

Compartmentation is structural and does not change at all on the timescale of a reaction. Chapter 3's compartments are a regulation mechanism as well as an architecture: an enzyme sealed inside a lysosome, at pH 5, is switched off with respect to everything in the cytosol simply by not being able to reach it. The enzymes of one pathway are often held in the same compartment, or bound together into a complex that hands the intermediate from one active site to the next without releasing it — which spares it the diffusive journey of Section 3.2, raises the effective concentration enormously, and stops anything else taking it.

And the slowest control is making more enzyme, or destroying it. A cell that needs a pathway for hours rather than seconds does not leave its enzymes switched off; it stops making them. That control is exercised on the DNA, and it is Chapter 8's.

A cell regulates enzymes, not reactions. An allosteric site lets a molecule that is not the substrate decide how hard an enzyme works, and several subunits acting together turn the response into a switch. Feedback inhibition points the end product of a pathway at the first step that commits anything to it, so that nothing is wasted — which is Chapter 1's negative feedback loop with a binding site for a set point.

Metabolism has a shape

Nothing in a cell is done in one jump. A metabolic pathway is a sequence of reactions, each catalysed by its own enzyme, in which the product of one step is the substrate of the next, and the whole of a cell's chemistry is a few thousand of these laid over one another. They come in three shapes. A linear pathway runs from a starting material to a product, as Section 5.7's five steps do. A branched pathway has an intermediate that is a junction, and the branch points are where the regulation sits, because that is where a decision is being made. A cyclic pathway regenerates its own carrier every turn, so a small amount of it processes an unlimited amount of material — Chapter 7 is built round one.

Pathways run in two directions and the directions have names. Catabolism takes large molecules apart, releases free energy, and is where ATP is made; digestion and respiration are catabolic. Anabolism builds large molecules from small ones, requires free energy, and is where ATP is spent. Section 2.6's two reactions are the chemistry of the distinction: hydrolysis is catabolic and condensation anabolic, and its rule that joining n monomers releases n − 1 waters is an anabolic ledger.

Between the same two compounds, the catabolic and the anabolic routes are never simply each other reversed, and the reason is Section 5.2. A step that runs strongly downhill one way runs strongly uphill the other, and both routes have to be downhill to work, so at least one step of each must differ and use its own enzyme. That is not an inefficiency but the thing that makes separate control possible: because the two routes have different enzymes at their committed steps, a cell can switch one off without switching the other on, and a signal can be pointed at breakdown or at synthesis rather than at both.

Stand back far enough and the whole has a shape. Hundreds of different foods enter the system — sugars, fats, amino acids from proteins, unusual compounds a bacterium happens to be able to eat — and they funnel down into a very small number of shared intermediates. From those same few intermediates, hundreds of different products are built. A cell's chemistry is an hourglass: many routes in, a narrow waist, many routes out. That is why a cell can eat almost anything and build almost anything with a modest number of enzymes, and it is why Chapters 6 and 7 can be about a handful of molecules rather than thousands. Figure 5.8 is that map, and the useful thing to do with it is to knock an enzyme out and watch what happens on either side of the gap.

Knocking out one enzyme is the same reasoning Section 3.4 used on the secretory route, where blocking one step stranded the cargo in a different compartment each time. Here the cargo is a small molecule: the intermediate immediately before the missing enzyme accumulates, everything after it disappears, and both facts are diagnostic. That is what most inherited metabolic diseases are. In phenylketonuria the enzyme converting phenylalanine to tyrosine is missing, phenylalanine accumulates to concentrations that damage a developing brain, and the treatment is a diet low in the substrate — a defect read straight off the shape of a pathway, and one that every newborn in most countries is tested for.

One idea remains, and it underlies the whole of energy metabolism. It is Chapter 2's electronegativity, in a new job. Oxidation is the loss of electrons and reduction is the gain of them, and they never happen apart, since electrons lost by one thing are gained by another: the pair is a redox reaction. In biology the electrons usually travel as part of hydrogen atoms — an electron and a proton together — so a molecule that loses hydrogen has been oxidised and one that gains hydrogen has been reduced, which is a useful rule of thumb for reading any equation in the next two chapters.

Why does moving an electron release anything? Because an electron is not equally comfortable everywhere. Section 2.2 ranked the elements by how hard they pull on shared electrons, with oxygen pulling hardest of all the common ones and carbon and hydrogen well down the list. An electron shared between carbon and hydrogen is held loosely; the same electron on oxygen is held tightly; and letting it move from the one to the other is letting it fall towards the atom that wants it most. The energy released is the difference, and that is the entire reason a fuel is a fuel. It is also the reason behind a claim Section 2.6 made and could not yet justify: a fat yields about 37 kilojoules a gram against a carbohydrate's 17 because a fat's carbons are bonded almost entirely to hydrogen and have the whole fall ahead of them, while many of a sugar's carbons are already bonded to oxygen and are partway down.

Take the fall to the bottom and the arithmetic is startling:

C6H12O6 + 6 O2 → 6 CO2 + 6 H2O

ΔG°′ for that is −2870 kilojoules per mole, which is the figure Section 5.2 used for the sugar that sits in a bowl and does nothing. Set light to a spoonful of glucose and you get every one of those 2870 kilojoules in one release, as heat, and Section 5.1 has already explained why a cell could not use a joule of it. A cell instead takes glucose apart in some two dozen enzyme-catalysed steps, arranged so that the steps which do release a useful amount release a parcel comparable with the 50 kilojoules one ATP is worth, and can therefore be coupled to making one. Release 2870 at once and none of it is caught at all. Release it in parcels of thirty or fifty and most of it can be. That is why metabolism is long. The length is not evolutionary clutter; it is the mechanism.

The last piece is how the electrons get from one place to the other, because they are not handed straight to oxygen. They are handed to an electron carrier, which takes them from the reaction that removed them and delivers them to whatever is going to use them. The principal one is NAD+, which is pleasingly two of Section 2.8's nucleotides joined tail to tail; it accepts two electrons and a proton and becomes NADH. The other common one is FAD, built round vitamin B2, which takes two of each and becomes FADH2. A carrier is neither a fuel nor a store: a cell holds very little of it and recycles it constantly, exactly as it does ATP, and a cell whose carriers are all loaded cannot oxidise anything further until something unloads them. That constraint decides a good deal of what happens in the next two chapters.

How much is on account in a loaded carrier? Letting the two electrons on one NADH fall the whole way to oxygen releases about 220 kilojoules per mole — more than four ATP's worth in a single drop, which is precisely why no cell drops them in one go. What takes that fall in stages, and how the stages are converted into a proton gradient across the membrane of Section 4.7 and then into ATP, is Chapter 7's subject and it is the best story in the unit.

Each observation tells you something about a reaction's free energy, its barrier, or how close it is to equilibrium. Sort them. Drag a card onto a bin, or use its buttons.

  • Runs by itself
  • Runs only because it is coupled
  • Exergonic, and waiting on an enzyme
  • Has reached equilibrium
  • A spoonful of sugar left in a bowl in the air is unchanged a year later.
  • A cell joins glutamate to ammonia, a step that on its own would need 14.2 kJ of free energy per mole put in.
  • A red blood cell placed in pure water takes in water and swells until it bursts.
  • In a test tube an enzyme has been at work for an hour; the amounts of substrate and product no longer change, although labelled molecules show both conversions still happening.
  • A muscle cell holds calcium in its endoplasmic reticulum at ten thousand times the concentration in its cytosol.
  • Hydrogen peroxide in a peroxisome decomposes slowly on its own and millions of times faster when it meets catalase.
  • Glucose leaves an intestinal cell into the blood through a carrier, from a higher concentration to a lower one.
  • A sealed flask of a sugar and its isomer, left with the enzyme that interconverts them, settles at a fixed ratio; adding ten times as much enzyme does not change the ratio.

Which is where this chapter hands on. Figure 1.7 drew two arrows through an ecosystem, gold for energy and green for matter, before the book had any chemistry with which to explain them. Both are now chemistry. Chapter 6 takes carbon dioxide and water and uses sunlight to push electrons back up the ladder onto carbon, building sugar; that is where nearly all the energy in the living world comes in, and it is what Section 1.6 meant by drawing the gold arrow starting at the sun. Chapter 7 takes the sugar apart again and lets the electrons fall to oxygen, catching what it can on the way down. Between them the carbon goes round and the energy goes through.

Metabolism is a few shared intermediates with many routes in and many routes out, run by enzymes, with catabolism and anabolism never quite the same path reversed. Its energy comes from letting electrons fall towards oxygen, which is electronegativity doing work; and it is long because the fall has to be taken in parcels the size of one ATP, carried between reactions by a small, constantly recycled pool of NAD+ and FAD.

Key ideas

  • Energy is the capacity to cause change, and it is conserved: nothing alive produces energy, it only takes it in and converts it. Chemical energy is potential energy held in the arrangement of atoms and electrons.
  • The second law does not forbid a cell. A cell is an open system that lowers its own entropy by raising its surroundings' entropy more, and it pays in heat — which, at one temperature throughout, can never be spent again. That is why energy flows through the living world once while matter cycles.
  • A reaction runs by itself when ΔG is negative. ΔG is the heat term minus the temperature times the entropy term, so a change that takes heat in can still run if it disorders enough — which is what assembles a membrane.
  • "Spontaneous" says which way, never how fast. Sugar in air is exergonic by 2870 kilojoules a mole and lasts for years.
  • ΔG depends on concentrations as much as on chemistry: every tenfold ratio of products to reactants is worth 5.9 kilojoules a mole at body temperature. A cell keeps reactions running by never letting them reach equilibrium, and a cell at equilibrium is dead.
  • ATP is a nucleotide with three phosphates. Hydrolysis releases free energy because the products are more stable — crowded charges relieved, the freed phosphate's charge spread out, both products better surrounded by water, and two molecules where there was one. No energy is stored in the bond that breaks; breaking a bond always costs.
  • Inside a cell an ATP is worth about 50 kilojoules a mole rather than the standard 30.5, because the cell holds ATP far from equilibrium with ADP and phosphate. It is a currency, not a store: the whole pool is spent and remade about once a minute.
  • Nothing is paid for with heat. Two reactions are coupled only when they share a chemical intermediate, and then their free energy changes add. What ATP does is transfer a phosphate — to a substrate, to a pump, or within a motor — making something less stable and so willing to act.
  • A reaction is slow because reaching its transition state costs energy. An enzyme lowers that activation energy by binding the transition state more tightly than the substrate, using proximity, strain, a private microenvironment and its own chemistry, often with a cofactor or coenzyme.
  • An enzyme changes the rate, equally in both directions. It changes neither ΔG nor the position of equilibrium, and it cannot make an endergonic reaction go. What a cell gains from catalysis is not speed but choice.
  • Rate against substrate rises and flattens, as a carrier's does. The maximum rate says how many sites there are; the Michaelis constant says at what concentration they are half busy, and whether the enzyme tracks its substrate or ignores it.
  • A competitive inhibitor sits in the active site and is beaten by more substrate; a non-competitive one binds elsewhere and is not; an irreversible one bonds covalently and is permanent. Most drugs and nearly all poisons are one of the three.
  • Allostery lets a molecule that is not the substrate set an enzyme's rate, and cooperative subunits turn the response into a switch. Feedback inhibition points a pathway's end product at its first committed step, which is Chapter 1's negative feedback with a binding site for a set point.
  • Metabolism is an hourglass: many fuels in, a few shared intermediates, many products out, with catabolism and anabolism never simply each other reversed.
  • Oxidation is loss of electrons and reduction is gain, usually carried as hydrogen atoms. Energy comes from letting electrons fall towards oxygen, and the fall is taken in many small steps so that each can pay for one ATP, with NAD+ and FAD carrying the electrons between reactions.

Glossary

Check your understanding

A bacterium divides every twenty minutes, turning a solution of simple molecules into millions of precisely built cells. Does this violate the second law of thermodynamics?

  • Yes, but only locally, and the law is known to be approximate at the scale of a cell.
  • No, because the second law applies to energy and not to order.
  • No, because the bacteria are an open system: they lower their own entropy by releasing heat and small disordered molecules, which raises the entropy of their surroundings by more.
  • No, because the energy the bacteria take in is exactly equal to the energy they give out.

The second law constrains an isolated system, and nothing alive is one. The bacteria's own entropy falls a long way; the entropy of the medium and the air around them rises further, mostly as heat from the inefficiency of every step. The last option is the first law, which is also true and is not the question: conserving energy says nothing about which way a change goes. And the second law is not approximate at any scale — this is exactly the case it was formulated to cover.

Which statement best explains why hydrolysing ATP to ADP and phosphate releases free energy?

  • The bond between the second and third phosphates is a high-energy bond, and breaking it releases the energy stored inside it.
  • The products are more stable than the reactant: the crowded negative charges are relieved, the freed phosphate spreads its charge, water holds both products better, and one molecule has become two.
  • Phosphate bonds are weaker than other covalent bonds, so less energy is needed to break them.
  • Water is a very reactive molecule, and attacking the phosphate releases the energy held in the water.

Breaking any covalent bond costs energy — Section 2.2 put a carbon–carbon bond at about 350 kilojoules a mole — so nothing is ever released by breaking one alone. Energy comes out when the bonds made and the arrangements reached in the products are, on balance, better than what was there before. The third option is a belief a reader can genuinely hold, and it is false about the chemistry: the bond joining one phosphate to the next is an ordinary covalent bond of ordinary strength. Nothing about ATP is unusually fragile; what is unusual is how much better off its products are. The "high-energy bond" of the first option is a real phrase with a real author, and it is the picture this section exists to take away.

A reaction is exergonic, and left alone it settles at equilibrium with ten times as much product as reactant — which by Section 5.2's arithmetic is a standard free energy change of about −5.9 kilojoules per mole. Its enzyme is now added. What changes?

  • The reaction becomes more exergonic, so more product forms.
  • The equilibrium shifts towards product, because the enzyme makes the forward reaction faster than the reverse one.
  • Equilibrium is reached sooner, at the same ten-to-one ratio, because the enzyme multiplies the forward and reverse rates by the same factor.
  • Nothing measurable changes, because an enzyme does not alter ΔG.

An enzyme lowers the barrier, and the transition state is the same arrangement whichever side you climb from, so the barrier falls by the same amount in both directions and both rates rise by the same factor. The ratio at which they balance is untouched. The second option is the near-miss worth understanding: it is true that the forward rate rises, and false that it rises more than the reverse. The last option overshoots in the other direction — ΔG is indeed unchanged, and the rate is not, which is the whole of what a catalyst is for.

A biochemist mixes an enzyme, its substrate and ATP in a tube. The reaction the enzyme catalyses is endergonic by 12 kilojoules per mole. The ATP is hydrolysed but the substrate is untouched. What is the most likely explanation?

  • Not enough ATP was added; 12 kilojoules per mole needs more than one ATP per substrate molecule.
  • The ATP hydrolysis is not coupled to the reaction — no shared intermediate is formed — so its free energy is released as heat and is of no use to the substrate.
  • The enzyme is inhibited by the ADP produced.
  • ATP hydrolysis is exergonic only inside a cell, so in a tube it releases nothing.

Free energy released nearby does no work: heat at one temperature cannot be spent, in a tube as much as in a cell. Coupling means a shared chemical intermediate — the substrate phosphorylated, or the enzyme phosphorylated — so that the two reactions are steps of one. The first option misses the point twice over: one ATP is worth at least 30.5 kilojoules a mole, which is ample against 12, and the problem is not the amount. The last is wrong about the chemistry: ATP hydrolysis is exergonic anywhere, and being inside a cell makes it more so rather than making it possible.

Two inhibitors are tested on the same enzyme. Inhibitor A lowers the rate at low substrate concentrations, but at high substrate the rate reaches the usual maximum. Inhibitor B lowers the maximum rate and no amount of substrate recovers it. What are they?

  • A binds the active site irreversibly; B binds it reversibly.
  • A is competitive, occupying the active site and losing the race when substrate is plentiful; B binds somewhere else and changes the enzyme's shape, so more substrate is no help.
  • A is non-competitive and B is competitive.
  • A has a lower affinity for the enzyme than B, and affinity is what the two curves are showing.

The diagnostic question is whether more substrate wins. Only an inhibitor competing for the same site can be outcompeted, so a maximum rate that is eventually reached means competitive, and a maximum rate that falls means the inhibitor is not competing with the substrate for anything. The first option cannot be right about A: an irreversible inhibitor removes enzyme molecules permanently, which lowers the maximum rate exactly as B does. The last option is a real temptation — affinity does affect how much inhibitor is needed — but it does not produce the two different shapes, and shape is what the experiment measures.
