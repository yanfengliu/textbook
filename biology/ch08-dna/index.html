Chapter 1 said that life runs on information, and Chapter 2 built the molecule that holds it; neither said how anybody knew. This chapter is the evidence and then the machinery: how DNA was shown to be the genetic material, why its shape gave away how it is copied, how a cell copies six billion base pairs with about one mistake in a billion, and what most of those base pairs turn out to be.

The genes were in the duller molecule

Section 1.5 made a claim this book has leaned on ever since: that a cell's instructions are written in DNA, as an order of four bases, and copied by a rule that gives each base one partner. Section 2.8 built the molecule, and said that an archive has to be permanent and openable at once. Section 5.7 said that the slowest way a cell controls its enzymes, by making more of them or fewer, is exercised on the DNA, and Chapter 7 ended by asking how a cell decides which of its thousands of enzymes to build. That question needs two answers before it can be asked properly — what is written on the DNA and how it is kept, which is this chapter, and how a gene is read into a protein, which is Chapter 9 — and it is Chapter 12's. Underneath all of them is a question none of those sections asked. How did anybody know that the genes are made of DNA?

For the first half of the twentieth century, the best-informed answer was that they are not. By the 1920s it was established that genes are carried on chromosomes, the threads that condense in a dividing nucleus and are shared out equally between the two daughter cells, and chromosomes were known to be made of two things, DNA and protein. The genes had to be one or the other, and protein was much the better candidate. Section 2.7 explains why: a protein is built from twenty kinds of amino acid, folds into a shape chosen from an almost unlimited number, and does almost everything a cell does. DNA is built from four kinds of nucleotide, and the leading chemist of nucleic acids, Phoebus Levene, had concluded that the four came in equal amounts in a short unit repeated end to end — the tetranucleotide hypothesis. A molecule that repeats one four-letter word for its whole length can carry no more information than the word. On that picture DNA was a scaffold, perhaps holding the protein in place. The reasoning was good. The belief it rested on was wrong, and it took a quarter of a century and three experiments to find out.

The first was not about genes at all. Frederick Griffith was a medical officer in London studying pneumonia, and in 1928 he published an account of two forms of the bacterium that causes it, Streptococcus pneumoniae. One grows on a plate as smooth, glistening colonies, because each cell wears a capsule of polysaccharide; Section 3.3 said that a capsule is the difference between a harmless strain of pneumococcus and a lethal one, and this is the case it was describing. The smooth form, S, kills a mouse. The rough form, R, has lost its capsule, is cleared by the mouse's immune system, and does not. Griffith injected mice in four ways. With live R bacteria, the mice lived. With live S, they died. With S bacteria killed by heat, they lived. And with heat-killed S mixed with live R, neither of which could kill a mouse on its own, some of the mice died, and from their blood Griffith grew living, encapsulated S bacteria whose descendants stayed S.

The detail that makes this more than a curiosity is which capsule the living bacteria wore. Pneumococci come in about a hundred capsular types, each a chemically different polysaccharide, and Griffith's R strain had been derived from bacteria of one type while the S cells he killed were of another. Griffith knew that a rough strain could now and then revert to the capsule of the type it had come from, so the type was the whole question. The bacteria he recovered from the dead mice carried the capsule of the killed type. They were not rough bacteria that had regained a capsule of their own. They were rough bacteria that had acquired, from dead cells of a different type, the ability to make that type's capsule, and had passed the ability on to their offspring. Something in the dead cells had survived the heat, entered the living ones and changed what they inherited. Griffith called the change transformation, and did not try to identify its cause.

Oswald Avery did, at the Rockefeller Institute in New York, with Colin MacLeod and then Maclyn McCarty, and the work took the best part of a decade. By then transformation had been made to work in a test tube, and then with an extract of dead cells rather than whole ones, in Avery's own laboratory, so the task was a purification: grow S cells in quantity, kill them, extract them, strip out one class of molecule after another, and test at every step whether what remained could still turn R cells into S. What remained, in the paper they published in 1944, was a fibrous white substance that could be wound onto a glass rod, still active diluted to one part in six hundred million, and with every chemical property of DNA they could measure: the ratio of nitrogen to phosphorus that DNA has and protein does not, the absorption of ultraviolet light characteristic of a nucleic acid, and a very large molecular size.

Then came the test that did the most work, because it used Section 5.5's rule that an enzyme handles its own substrate and not its near neighbours. Enzymes that digest protein left the extract's activity untouched. So did an enzyme that digests RNA. An enzyme preparation that breaks down DNA destroyed it. Had the active substance been a protein carried along with the DNA, the enzymes that digest protein should have destroyed it; had it been RNA, the enzyme that digests RNA should have. Only destroying the DNA stopped the transformation.

Avery's paper was careful, and so were its readers. The objection pressed hardest, by Avery's colleague Alfred Mirsky among others, was that a trace of protein too small to detect — as much as one or two per cent could hide in such a preparation, Mirsky argued — could still have been the real agent, riding along with the DNA and escaping enzymes that were not themselves pure. It was a fair objection in 1944, and it was also the kind that can never be fully answered, since no purification removes the last molecule. In practice it was answered over the next few years. Rollin Hotchkiss purified active preparations until any protein left in them was a small fraction of one per cent, with no loss of activity; transformation was shown for other inherited traits than the capsule; and in 1950 Erwin Chargaff published analyses showing that the proportions of the four bases differ from one species to another. DNA was not a monotonous repeat. It varied, and a molecule that varies can carry information.

All known cellular life keeps its genes in DNA. Some viruses do not: the genes of influenza, of HIV and of the coronaviruses are RNA, the molecule Section 2.8 called the working copy. In the 1950s the RNA of tobacco mosaic virus, stripped of its protein, was shown to start an infection on its own. The genetic material is nucleic acid wherever it has been looked for; which nucleic acid depends on the organism.

The experiment usually remembered as settling the matter came in 1952, from Alfred Hershey and Martha Chase at Cold Spring Harbor, and it used a different organism and a different method. A bacteriophage is a virus that infects bacteria. The one they used, T2, is a protein coat around a single molecule of DNA; it attaches to the outside of an E. coli cell, and some twenty minutes later the cell bursts and releases about a hundred new phages. So the phage's genetic material gets into the cell. The question was which half of the phage does.

They answered it with Section 2.1's isotopes: atoms of one element with different numbers of neutrons, which behave alike in a cell. Some isotopes are unstable and give off radiation as they decay, so a few of their atoms can be found among millions of ordinary ones by counting the radiation. Protein contains sulfur, in the side chains of cysteine and methionine, and DNA contains none. DNA contains phosphorus, one atom in every nucleotide, and the phage's protein contains almost none. So one batch of phages was grown with radioactive sulfur, 35S, which labelled their protein and nothing else, and another with radioactive phosphorus, 32P, which labelled their DNA. Each batch was allowed to infect unlabelled bacteria for a few minutes; the mixture was spun in a kitchen blender to shear the phages off the cell surfaces; and a centrifuge separated the bacteria, which sank into a pellet, from the much lighter phage coats, which stayed in the liquid. Then they counted where each label had gone.

Most of the sulfur, 75 to 80 per cent of it, came off in the blender and stayed in the liquid with the empty coats. Most of the phosphorus stayed with the cells. And when those cells burst, the new generation of phages carried 30 per cent or more of the parents' phosphorus and less than 1 per cent of their sulfur. What entered the cell and reached the offspring was the DNA.

Told as the evidence it was, the experiment was less clean than that summary. Blending left a fifth or more of the protein label attached to the cells, and a fifth to a third of the DNA label came off with the coats, half of that even without blending. Hershey and Chase drew their conclusion about the protein with care and stopped short of saying that DNA had been shown to be the genetic material. Yet the experiment persuaded people faster than Avery's had. Some of that was sociology — the phage workers were a small and influential group, and by 1952 the tetranucleotide objection was gone — but most of it was logic. Avery's case rested on the purity of his enzymes, Hershey and Chase's on how well a blender separates coats from cells, and the two experiments shared no weakness. An objection that could explain away one of them could not explain away the other, and both, by entirely different routes, in a bacterium and in a virus, pointed at the same molecule.

Protein was the better guess: twenty kinds of building block against four, and a DNA believed to repeat one short unit for its whole length. Two experiments said otherwise by independent routes. Enzymes destroyed a bacterium's transforming principle only when they destroyed its DNA, and radioactive labels showed that it was a virus's DNA, not its coat, that entered the cell and reached the next generation. Neither was airtight, and they did not fail in the same place.

The helix was measured before it was built

By 1951 the question had moved on from what the genes are made of to how a molecule could do what genes do: carry an enormous amount of information, be copied exactly, and very occasionally change. Everyone expected the answer to lie in the molecule's structure, and nobody could see it. Section 3.1 gave the rule: nothing much finer than half the wavelength of the light can be resolved, and the atoms of a molecule are a tenth to a fifth of a nanometre apart. X-rays from a copper target have a wavelength of 0.154 nm, the size of an atom, which is short enough. But no lens can gather scattered X-rays back into an image of anything as small as an atom. What can be recorded is the pattern they make when they scatter off a sample in which the same arrangement repeats many times over, which is X-ray diffraction. Each repeat in the sample throws the rays into its own spots or arcs, and the pattern is an inverse map of the structure: the wider a spacing in the molecule, the nearer the centre its spots fall. A diffraction pattern does not show a molecule. It gives measurements, from which a molecule has to be deduced.

Rosalind Franklin joined King's College London in January 1951, an experienced X-ray crystallographer who had spent three years in Paris applying the method to carbons. She was given the DNA project, with a graduate student, Raymond Gosling. Her first discovery was practical. DNA drawn out into fibres takes two forms depending on how much water it holds: a drier, more ordered form she called A, and a wetter form, B, which is the one DNA takes in a watery cell. Earlier photographs, William Astbury's of the late 1930s among them, had been of mixtures of the two. By holding her fibres at a controlled humidity, Franklin obtained clean patterns of each.

The most famous of them, the B-⁠form pattern known as Photograph 51, was made by Franklin and Gosling in May 1952 from an exposure of sixty-two hours, and it can be read with no more than the principle above. Its spots lie in rows, and the rows are crossed by a great X; theory worked out the same year showed that a helix scatters X-rays into exactly that cross, so the molecule is a helix. The rows are evenly spaced, and their spacing gives the length along the axis in which the helix repeats, 3.4 nm, which is one full turn. At top and bottom are two heavy arcs, much further from the centre, from something repeated every 0.34 nm along the axis — the flat bases, stacked like coins — and 3.4 divided by 0.34 gives ten bases to each turn. How far the pattern spreads sideways gives the width, about 2 nm. And one row, the fourth from the centre, is missing: a single helical strand would not leave that gap, but two strands wound round the same axis and offset along it by three-eighths of a turn cancel each other's scattering on exactly that row, which is how Franklin and Gosling explained the gap in their own paper. Franklin's other measurements mattered as much as the photograph. She argued from the way her fibres took up water that the sugar–phosphate backbones must lie on the outside of the molecule, where water could reach their charged phosphates, with the bases inside; when Watson and Crick showed their first model late in 1951, with three chains and the backbones in the middle, she pointed out that it could not hold the water her fibres held, and it was abandoned. And her analysis of the A form, which gave the sharper pattern, showed that its crystals had a particular symmetry: the molecule looks the same after being turned end over end about an axis at right angles to its length. A molecule of two chains can have that symmetry only if the two chains run in opposite directions.

The last ingredient came from chemistry. Alongside the variation between species, Chargaff's analyses showed a regularity he could not explain: in the DNA of any one organism, the amount of adenine equals the amount of thymine, and the amount of guanine equals the amount of cytosine. These are Chargaff's rules. In yeast he found the four in the proportions 1.8 of adenine to 1.9 of thymine to 1.0 of guanine to 1.0 of cytosine, and in the bacillus that causes tuberculosis in birds 1.1 to 1.0 to 2.6 to 2.4: two DNAs about as unlike as DNAs come, and within each, adenine matching thymine and guanine matching cytosine as nearly as his methods could measure. He was cautious about what the equalities meant, and could not say whether they were more than coincidence. What varies from species to species is the proportion of the two pairs, never the equality within each.

Watson and Crick, at the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge, were building models rather than taking photographs, and early in 1953 they came into possession of the measurements they lacked. In January, Wilkins showed Watson Photograph 51, without Franklin's knowledge. In February, Max Perutz passed Crick a report the King's group had written for the Medical Research Council, which set out Franklin's measurements and the symmetry of the A form, and Crick saw what the symmetry meant for the chains. Watson, meanwhile, was fitting cardboard cut-outs of the bases against one another, and got nowhere until Jerry Donohue, a chemist who shared their office, told him that the standard textbook drawings put a hydrogen atom in the wrong place on guanine and on thymine. Each base can exist in two forms that differ only in where one hydrogen sits, structural isomers of the kind Section 2.5 described that turn into one another readily, called tautomers. In a cell each base spends almost all its time in one of them. The drawings showed the rare one, and in the rare form the pairs do not fit. A base caught in its rare form at the moment it is copied can pair with the wrong partner instead, thymine with guanine, and Watson and Crick suggested that same year that this is one way copying goes wrong.

With the right forms, the pairs fell into place at once. Each pair is one purine, a base built on two fused rings — adenine or guanine — across from one pyrimidine, a base built on one ring — thymine or cytosine. Two purines would be too wide to fit between the backbones and two pyrimidines too narrow. Of the purine–pyrimidine combinations, only adenine with thymine and guanine with cytosine make their hydrogen bonds in the right places without distorting the helix, two for the first pair and three for the second, as Section 2.8 said, and the two pairs come out almost exactly the same shape and width. So the pairing rule of Chapter 1 is a consequence of geometry, and Chargaff's rules follow from it: every adenine in one strand has a thymine across from it in the other, so a double-stranded molecule must hold exactly as many of one as of the other.

The model they published puts two strands round a common axis in a right-handed double helix 2 nm across. The sugar–phosphate backbones are on the outside and the paired bases inside, stacked 0.34 nm apart, ten pairs to each 3.4 nm turn. And the two strands run in opposite directions, which needs the numbering of the sugar to state properly. The five carbons of deoxyribose are numbered 1′ to 5′ — the primes tell them apart from the atoms of the base — and in the backbone each phosphate joins the 5′ carbon of one sugar to the 3′ carbon of the next. So every strand has two different ends, a 5′ end whose last sugar carries a phosphate on its fifth carbon and a 3′ end whose last sugar carries a hydroxyl group on its third. Section 2.8 said the chain has a direction, and this is the direction. In the helix, one strand runs from 5′ to 3′ up the axis and its partner runs from 5′ to 3′ down it: the strands are antiparallel, as the symmetry Franklin measured requires, and Section 8.4 shows that this one fact shapes the whole business of copying.

Because the two sugars of a base pair are attached on the same side of it rather than opposite each other, the backbones are not evenly spaced around the helix, and two spiral grooves of different widths run along the molecule, the major groove and the minor. The edges of the bases lie exposed at the bottom of both, and each of the four ways a pair can face presents a different pattern of atoms in the major groove, so a protein can read the sequence from outside without opening the helix. Chapter 12 is largely about proteins doing exactly that.

One reconciliation is owed. Section 1.5 described the helix as turning every 3.4 nm with about ten and a half base pairs to a turn, and those two numbers come from different measurements. Franklin's fibres give ten pairs in 3.4 nm. DNA free in solution, measured by other means around 1980, turns a little more slowly, about ten and a half pairs to a turn, which at the same 0.34 nm per pair makes one turn about 3.6 nm long. Both are right about the conditions they were measured in.

The model was published in Nature on 25 April 1953 — barely a page, with no experimental data of its own — followed in the same issue by a paper from Wilkins and two colleagues and then by one from Franklin and Gosling that included Photograph 51. Read in that order, Franklin's paper looks like confirmation of the model; it was the source of most of the measurements the model was built to fit, and Watson and Crick's acknowledgement said only that they had been stimulated by a general knowledge of the King's group's unpublished results. Her own notebooks show that by late February 1953 she had concluded that the B form was two helical chains with the phosphates outside; she had not reached the pairing. She left King's that spring for Birkbeck College, did pioneering work there on the structure of viruses, and died of ovarian cancer in April 1958, aged 37. The Nobel Prize for the structure went in 1962 to Watson, Crick and Wilkins. By the rules of the time a prize could go to someone who had died only if the death came after a nomination for that year's prize, and she had died four years before. How her part should be described is still argued over. Recent work on the letters and drafts of the time suggests that people then saw the discovery more nearly as the shared work of two groups than the later telling did, and that the measurements in the Council's report mattered to the model more than the famous photograph. What is not in dispute is the substance. The helix, its width, its pitch, the spacing of its bases, the backbones on the outside and the symmetry that makes the chains antiparallel were measured in Franklin's laboratory. The pairing was Watson's and Crick's. Theirs was the first model to fit all of it.

The double helix was measured before it was modelled. Franklin's photographs showed a helix 2 nm wide, turning every 3.4 nm, with flat bases stacked 0.34 nm apart; her other data put the backbones outside and required the two chains to run in opposite directions. Watson and Crick added the pairing — a two-ringed purine always across from a one-ringed pyrimidine, adenine with thymine and guanine with cytosine, both pairs the same width — which fitted every measurement and explained Chargaff's rules.

The structure says how it is copied

Near its end, Watson and Crick's paper made a famous understatement, a sentence beginning "It has not escaped our notice" that pointed out what the pairing implied: a way for the genetic material to be copied. Section 1.5 stated it as a rule and Section 8.2 has now given it a structure. Because each base has exactly one partner, each strand of the helix specifies the other completely. Separate the two and each can serve as a template, a strand whose sequence dictates, base by base, the sequence of a new strand built against it. Two double helices result, each identical to the original, and each made of one old strand and one new. This is semiconservative replication: half of each parent molecule is conserved in each daughter.

A structure that suggests a mechanism is not evidence that the mechanism happens, and in the mid-1950s there were two other schemes that were at least as easy to believe. In conservative copying the parent helix stays together and a wholly new one is made alongside it, so that one daughter is all old and the other all new. In dispersive copying every strand of both daughters is a patchwork of old pieces and new ones. Max Delbrück proposed the dispersive scheme in 1954 for a good reason. The two strands of a helix are wound round each other about once every ten base pairs, and pulling them apart means undoing every one of those turns: the chromosome of E. coli, 4.6 million pairs long, is wound nearly half a million times, and it seemed impossible that it could spin that many times, in a crowded cell, every time it was copied. A copying machine that cut the strands into short pieces as it went and joined them up again would avoid the problem, and would leave a patchwork. The objection was a real one, and Section 8.4 shows how a cell answers it.

The three schemes make different predictions about one measurable thing: after a round of copying, how much of each daughter molecule is old. What was needed was a way to tell old DNA from new, and in 1957 Matthew Meselson and Franklin Stahl, at the California Institute of Technology, found one in weight. They grew E. coli for about fourteen generations on a medium whose only source of nitrogen was ammonium chloride made with heavy nitrogen, 15N, whose atoms carry one more neutron than ordinary 14N. Unlike Hershey and Chase's labels, 15N is not radioactive: it is told apart from 14N by weight alone. Every base contains nitrogen, so by the end all the bacteria's DNA was heavy. Then they moved the bacteria to ordinary medium, in which every new strand would be light, and took samples as the population doubled, once, twice and more.

The difference in weight is small — heavy DNA is less than one per cent denser than light — and the way of measuring it was new. In density-gradient centrifugation the DNA is dissolved in a concentrated solution of caesium chloride, a heavy salt, and spun at about 45,000 revolutions a minute for most of a day. The salt is driven towards the bottom of the tube until a smooth gradient forms, least dense at the top and densest at the bottom, and each DNA molecule drifts up or down until it reaches the level where the solution's density equals its own. There it stops, and all the molecules of one density gather in one thin band. Light DNA from E. coli bands at about 1.71 grams per cubic centimetre and heavy DNA 0.014 higher, far enough apart to see. They saw them by ultraviolet light, which the bases absorb strongly near 260 nm and nothing else in the tube does. Section 6.3 explained that most molecules have gaps only an ultraviolet photon can span; the small rings of the bases are among them, which is also why DNA is colourless.

Conservative copying predicts two bands, one heavy and one light, and there was no heavy band: conservative copying was ruled out. But dispersive copying also predicts a single band in that position, so after one generation it survived. After two generations there were two bands, one at the hybrid density and one light, in equal amounts. Dispersive copying predicts a single band, lighter than hybrid, and there were two, so it was ruled out too. Only the semiconservative scheme predicted both results, and it went on predicting the later ones. When the hybrid DNA was heated, which separates paired strands, it came apart into one heavy part and one light one.

The experiment is often called the most beautiful in biology, and it is worth saying why, since beauty is not usually a scientific virtue. Each hypothesis made a prediction about something that could be seen directly, in a photograph of a tube, with no statistics needed to read it. The predictions parted at the first generation for one rival and at the second for the other, so two photographs were enough to eliminate both. And the band from the first sample, all heavy, and the band from bacteria never grown on heavy nitrogen, all light, marked where the two extremes lie on the same tube. Section 1.8 said that a hypothesis earns trust by surviving honest attempts to break it; this is what an honest attempt looks like when it is designed well. Meselson and Stahl were careful, too, about what they had not shown. They wrote of the two "subunits" of each molecule, because nothing in the tube proved that the subunits were single strands; that was shown later, by other means, and every organism in which the question has been asked since copies its DNA semiconservatively. Copying runs one way, and the strands do not

The enzyme was found before the mechanism was proved. In 1956 Arthur Kornberg isolated from E. coli a DNA polymerase, an enzyme that makes DNA in a test tube, and found that it needs four things. It needs a template, a single strand to copy. It needs a primer: a short stretch of strand already paired to the template and ending in a free 3′ hydroxyl group, because a polymerase can extend a strand but cannot start one. It needs magnesium ions. And it needs its building blocks in a particular form, not as plain nucleotides but as deoxynucleoside triphosphates, nucleotides carrying a chain of three phosphates each, one kind for each of the four bases. Given all four things, it adds nucleotides one at a time to the primer's 3′ end, each chosen to pair with the template base opposite, and the new strand grows along the template.

Kornberg's enzyme, now called DNA polymerase I, turned out not to be the one that copies the chromosome. In 1969 Paula De Lucia and John Cairns found a mutant of E. coli that had almost none of it and still copied its DNA and grew. The search that followed found DNA polymerase III, which does nearly all the copying; polymerase I finishes the job, as described below, and repairs.

The chemistry repays two debts. Section 2.8 said that a cell does not squeeze two plain nucleotides together, and that what it does instead was a story for this chapter; Section 5.3 described a way of spending ATP in which two phosphates come off together as a linked pair, which is then itself split, and said that is how a cell drives the building of DNA against a stubbornly unfavourable equilibrium. Here it is. The incoming nucleotide carries its three phosphates on the 5′ carbon of its sugar. The oxygen of the 3′ hydroxyl at the end of the growing strand attacks the innermost of the three; a new bond forms between that phosphate and the strand; and the outer two phosphates leave together as pyrophosphate. The link made is a phosphodiester bond — a phosphate joining the 3′ carbon of one sugar to the 5′ carbon of the next — which is the link of the backbone, and the same link that Section 2.6's condensation would make. But what leaves is not water.

The first reaction on its own can be made to run either way: a polymerase will run it backwards, taking a strand apart, if pyrophosphate is allowed to build up. The second reaction is what makes copying go one way. An enzyme, pyrophosphatase, splits each pyrophosphate into two phosphates as it appears, a reaction that releases free energy of its own and holds the concentration of pyrophosphate near zero. That is Section 5.2's rule that the concentrations decide which way a reaction runs, used to pull one reaction forward by removing its product, and it is Section 5.4's coupling, with pyrophosphate as the shared intermediate. Each letter added to a strand spends two of the links between phosphates that Section 5.3 described, the same number an ATP gives up when it is broken all the way down to AMP.

Now notice the direction this chemistry imposes. The new nucleotide is always added to a 3′ hydroxyl on the strand, and adding it always leaves a new 3′ hydroxyl at the end. So a strand grows at its 3′ end only; it is made from 5′ to 3′, while its template is read from 3′ to 5′. Every DNA polymerase that has been found works this way, and so do the enzymes that copy DNA into RNA. None of them adds to a 5′ end. Section 8.5 explains why; the rest of this section is what it costs.

Copying begins at an origin of replication, where proteins open the helix and two replication forks set off in opposite directions, each a Y-shaped region where the parent helix is being opened ahead and both strands copied behind. At each fork a helicase, a motor protein driven by ATP, runs along one strand prising the pairs apart, breaking the hydrogen bonds between them a few at a time, which is exactly what Section 2.8 said a rung of weak bonds allows. Single-strand binding proteins coat the exposed strands to stop them pairing again or folding back on themselves. And ahead of the fork, the helix not yet opened is being twisted tighter, because every ten pairs unwound behind pushes one extra turn into the DNA in front. That was Delbrück's problem. The extra turns make the helix coil on itself, as an over-twisted rope does, which is supercoiling, and left alone it would soon stop the fork. Topoisomerases remove it by cutting one strand or both, letting the DNA rotate or pass through the gap, and sealing the cut again. The molecule never has to spin. It is cut and rejoined, continually, just ahead of the fork.

Now put two facts together, both already stated. The two template strands at a fork are antiparallel (Section 8.2), and a polymerase can only extend a 3′ end. On one of the two templates the new strand's 3′ end points towards the fork, so as the fork opens more template the polymerase simply keeps going, and that strand is made in one continuous piece: the leading strand. On the other template the new strand has to run the opposite way, with its 3′ end pointing away from the fork. As the fork opens more template on that side, there is no 3′ end there to extend. A polymerase has to start again near the fork, work backwards away from it until it runs into the piece made before, and then start again. This strand, made in short pieces called Okazaki fragments and joined afterwards, is the lagging strand, named because its completion lags behind the fork. It is not a quirk of the machinery. It follows from the two facts. A polymerase able to add at either end would make both new strands continuously at every fork. Strands running the same way would only move the problem: at the fork travelling one way both new strands would grow towards it, but at the fork leaving the same origin in the other direction both would have to be made in pieces. Figure 8.3 lets you make both changes.

The fragments were found before anyone understood them. In 1968 Reiji Okazaki, Tsuneko Okazaki and their colleagues gave growing E. coli radioactive thymidine, the building block that carries thymine into DNA, for only a few seconds, so that only the DNA made in those seconds was labelled, and found the label in short pieces, about a thousand to two thousand nucleotides long. When the bacteria were left longer before their DNA was extracted, the same label turned up in long DNA: the pieces had been joined. In eukaryotes the fragments are shorter, about a hundred to two hundred nucleotides, close to the length of DNA carried by one of the protein spools of Section 8.6.

Every fragment needs a start, and a DNA polymerase cannot make one. The starts are made by primase, which lays down a short piece of RNA, about ten nucleotides long, paired to the template; like the RNA polymerase that copies genes into RNA, which Chapter 9 describes, primase can begin a chain from nothing, and a DNA polymerase then extends it. The leading strand needs a primer once, at its origin. The lagging strand needs one for every fragment: well over a thousand at each fork in E. coli, and tens of millions in a human cell every time it copies its DNA. Afterwards every primer has to be removed and replaced with DNA — in E. coli by polymerase I, which cuts the RNA away ahead of itself while it fills in DNA behind — and the last gap in the backbone, a single missing link called a nick, is sealed by DNA ligase, which makes the final phosphodiester bond with energy taken from ATP, or in E. coli from NAD+. Why a start should be made of RNA only to be thrown away is, again, a question for Section 8.5.

A nucleotide with no 3′ hydroxyl can be added to a strand, but nothing can be added after it. Several drugs work that way. Zidovudine, the first drug approved against HIV, is thymidine with its 3′ hydroxyl replaced by an azide group; the virus's own polymerase builds it into the DNA copy it is making, and the copy ends there.

Last, the time it takes, which is where the geometry of forks turns into arithmetic. E. coli has one origin, and its two forks each travel about a thousand nucleotides a second. Each fork has to copy half of the 4.6 million pairs, 2.3 million, which at a thousand a second takes 2300 seconds, about 38 minutes; the measured time is about forty. A human fork is much slower, a few dozen nucleotides a second, and a human chromosome is much longer: chromosome 1 alone is about 249 million pairs. From a single origin, with two forks at fifty nucleotides a second, it would take 249 million divided by 100, which is 2.5 million seconds, about a month. A human cell copies all of its DNA in about eight hours. It does so by using tens of thousands of origins, spread along every chromosome and fired at different times through those eight hours, so that each chromosome is copied in hundreds or thousands of stretches, each begun at its own origin, and the forks from neighbouring origins meet and join.

DNA polymerase needs a template, a primer ending in a free 3′ hydroxyl and the four nucleoside triphosphates. It adds each nucleotide to the 3′ end, taking the energy from the nucleotide's own phosphates, and splitting the pyrophosphate released makes the addition one-way. Because every DNA polymerase adds only at a 3′ end and the two templates run in opposite directions, one new strand at each fork is made continuously and the other backwards in short fragments, each begun on its own RNA primer and joined afterwards. A bacterium copies its chromosome from one origin in about forty minutes; a human cell uses tens of thousands of origins to copy more than a thousand times as much DNA in eight hours.

The archive is kept by checking it

Section 2.8's pairing rule has a mechanism behind it, hydrogen bonds between complementary bases, and on its own it is nowhere near precise enough. In water a wrong pair differs from a right one by little more than a single hydrogen bond among the many each base makes with the water around it, and if nucleotides were lined up along a template by pairing alone, something like one in a hundred would be wrong. Copying the 6.2 billion pairs of a human cell at that rate would give each daughter cell some sixty million errors. The rate at which a copying cell actually leaves errors behind is between about one in a billion and one in ten billion per base pair copied. Between one in a hundred and one in ten billion lie three separate checks, each catching most of what the one before it let through.

The first is the polymerase's choice. Section 5.5 said that an enzyme's active site closes around its substrate, and that the fit is what lets it handle one substrate and not its near neighbours. A polymerase's active site closes around a pair: the incoming nucleotide and the template base it faces. A correct pair, a purine across from a pyrimidine with its hydrogen bonds in the right places, has the width and shape of the closed pocket, and the enzyme closes on it and joins it to the strand. A wrong pair has a different shape, the enzyme closes on it badly, and the joining step is so much slower that the wrong nucleotide usually drifts away before it can be added. The polymerase is judging the geometry of the pair more than the strength of its bonds, and Section 8.2 showed that geometry is what adenine–thymine and guanine–cytosine share and no other pairing has. The choice alone brings the error rate to about one in a hundred thousand.

The second check is the polymerase checking itself. The polymerases that copy chromosomes carry a second active site, close to the first, which cuts nucleotides off the 3′ end of a strand one at a time. A wrong nucleotide at the end of the growing strand is badly paired, and a badly paired end does two things: the polymerase is slow to add anything to it, and it tends to come away from the template and slip into the second site, where it is cut off. The polymerase then tries again. This is proofreading, and it improves the error rate about a hundredfold, to around one in ten million.

The third check comes after the polymerase has gone. Mismatch repair proteins scan newly copied DNA for the slight distortion a wrong pair makes in the helix. Finding one is not the difficult part. The difficult part is knowing which of the two bases is the mistake, because the system has to cut out a stretch of the new strand and rebuild it, and cutting out the old one instead would write the error in for good. E. coli solves this with a mark. An enzyme adds a methyl group to the adenine of every GATC in its DNA, but it reaches newly made strands only after a delay, so for a while the old strand is marked and the new one is not, and the repair system cuts the unmarked one. Eukaryotes use a different signal, which appears to be the nicks and ends that a newly made strand carries until they are sealed, the gaps between Okazaki fragments among them. Mismatch repair improves the rate a further hundred times or so; measured in E. coli, the gain lies between about twenty and four hundred times.

The bottom line of that table is a rate per base pair per round of copying, and it is the most surprising number in this chapter: a machine joining nucleotides at dozens a second, at tens of thousands of forks at once, gets about one in a billion wrong, and in E. coli, where the rate has been measured most directly, about one in four and a half billion. Multiply it by what is being copied and the same process gives very different-sounding answers. When a human cell copies its 6.2 billion pairs at one in ten billion, each of its two daughter cells receives about 0.6 new errors on average, fewer than one; at one in a billion, about six. An E. coli cell copying 4.6 million pairs leaves about one new error in every thousand divisions. And each human child carries some sixty or seventy new mutations that neither parent carried, because between one generation and the next lie many rounds of division in the lines of cells that make eggs and sperm, and some of those mutations come from damage rather than from copying at all. One in ten billion, fewer than one per daughter cell, and seventy per child are all true, of three different things. Quoting any one of them without saying what it is per is how the same machinery comes to be described both as almost perfect and as a steady source of change.

Now the question Section 8.4 left open: why does every DNA polymerase grow a strand only at its 3′ end? Proofreading depends on it. When a strand grows from 5′ to 3′, the energy for each new link arrives with the incoming nucleotide, on its own three phosphates. Cutting a wrong nucleotide off the end leaves an ordinary 3′ hydroxyl, exactly as it was before the mistake, and the next nucleotide brings its own energy with it. Imagine instead a polymerase that grew strands from 3′ to 5′. The energy for each link would then have to be carried on the end of the strand itself, as three phosphates on its last nucleotide, for each incoming nucleotide to attack. Cutting off a wrong nucleotide would take those phosphates with it and leave an end with nothing to pay for the next link, so every correction would stop the strand dead. A polymerase that can take back its mistakes can only grow in one direction, and it is the direction they all grow in. The same argument explains the RNA primers. An enzyme that starts a chain from nothing has no paired end to check its first nucleotides against, so it cannot proofread, and what it makes is less accurate than the DNA around it. Making the start out of RNA marks it as provisional, so it can be recognised, removed and replaced by a polymerase that does check.

A copy that is accurate when it is made still has to last, and here Section 2.8's word "permanent" needs a qualification. In water at body temperature DNA falls apart, slowly. Section 5.2 explained how a reaction can be downhill and still slow, and these reactions are slow, but a human cell holds six billion pairs of targets, and slow on six billion targets is frequent. Tomas Lindahl, who measured the rates in the 1970s, found that the bond joining a purine to its sugar is broken by water thousands of times a day in every cell, leaving a gap in the sequence, and that cytosine undergoes deamination — it loses its amino group and becomes uracil — hundreds of times. Reactive forms of oxygen alter bases, turning guanine into a form that pairs with adenine; backbones break; and some fifty times in every cell cycle, both backbones break at nearly the same place. Lindahl's own later estimate for all of it together was ten to twenty thousand lesions a day in every cell.

Almost all of this damage is repaired, and the repair depends on the thing Section 1.5 said the second strand was for. Because the sequence is held twice, a damaged stretch of one strand can be cut out and rebuilt using the other as its template. The two main routes differ in how much they cut. In base excision repair, an enzyme that recognises one particular kind of wrong base — a uracil, an oxidised guanine — cuts it from its sugar, the empty sugar and phosphate are removed, a polymerase fills the one-nucleotide gap by pairing against the partner strand, and ligase seals it. In nucleotide excision repair, which deals with damage that distorts the helix, such as a dimer, the damaged strand is cut on both sides of the lesion and a stretch of about thirty nucleotides is removed and rebuilt. People who inherit broken copies of any one of several genes of this second route, one from each parent, have xeroderma pigmentosum. Their skin cannot remove ultraviolet damage, and without strict protection from daylight they develop skin cancers thousands of times more often than other people, often in childhood.

Section 2.8 said that DNA uses thymine where RNA uses uracil, and did not say why. Deamination is the reason. A cytosine that loses its amino group becomes uracil, and uracil pairs with adenine, not guanine; if the damage is not repaired before the next copying, a pair that was C–G becomes, a generation of copying later, T–A. If DNA used uracil as one of its four ordinary bases, an enzyme finding a uracil would have no way to tell whether it belonged there or had been a cytosine a moment before. Because DNA uses thymine instead — uracil carrying one extra methyl group — any uracil in DNA is damage by definition, and an enzyme that removes uracil from DNA can remove every one it finds. The one place the arrangement fails proves the rule. Many genomes, ours among them, mark some of their cytosines by adding a methyl group, for reasons Chapter 12 describes. When a methylated cytosine loses its amino group it becomes not uracil but thymine, an ordinary base, and all that is left to find is a thymine paired wrongly with a guanine, which is caught far less reliably. At the places where cytosines are methylated, a C–G pair changes to T–A some ten to twenty times more often than the same change happens elsewhere, and about a third of the single-letter changes known to cause inherited disease are of this kind.

A double-strand break, where both backbones are cut at nearly the same place, is the most dangerous damage of all, because there is no intact partner strand on the molecule to rebuild from. A cell has two ways to mend one. It can join the two ends directly, which is fast and often loses or adds a few letters at the join. Or, after the DNA has been copied, it can use the undamaged copy of the same stretch as a template and restore the sequence exactly, by a process called homologous recombination that Chapter 11 returns to. Section 8.7 shows why the natural ends of a chromosome have to be hidden from the first of these.

What escapes all of this — a copying error missed by every check, damage repaired wrongly or not at all — becomes a mutation: a heritable change in the sequence, copied faithfully from then on like any other letter. Most mutations are harmless. Some break a gene that matters, and the defects in the checks themselves are the clearest cases: people who inherit a faulty mismatch repair gene have Lynch syndrome and a high risk of cancer of the colon at a young age, and tumours whose copying polymerase has lost its proofreading site carry more mutations than almost any others. A very few mutations are useful, and they are the only source of the new variation that Section 1.7's natural selection has ever had to work on.

Put the checks and the repairs side by side and each mistake or injury belongs to one of them, which is the skill this section has been building.

Two metres, wound on spools

Do the arithmetic Section 1.5 makes possible. A human cell holds two sets of chromosomes, 6.2 billion base pairs in all, and each pair adds 0.34 nm to the length of the helix. Laid end to end, the DNA of one cell would stretch about 2.1 metres, and it is held in a nucleus typically five to ten micrometres across. Section 3.3 found it remarkable that the 1.6 mm of DNA in E. coli fits into a cell 2 µm long, eight hundred times its container; the DNA of a human nucleus is a few hundred thousand times as long as the nucleus is wide. Nor is it in short pieces. Each chromosome is one unbroken molecule: chromosome 1, the largest, is about 249 million pairs, a single thread of DNA 8.5 cm long and 2 nm thick. And it has to be packed so that any part of it can still be opened to be read, all of it can be copied, and at division the copies can be pulled apart without tangling.

The packing begins with histones, the small proteins that Section 3.3 said many archaea wind their DNA on and Section 3.5 said the genomes of mitochondria and chloroplasts lack. Between a fifth and a quarter of the amino acids in each of the core histones are lysine or arginine, whose side chains carry a positive charge at the pH of a cell, as Section 2.7 described. The backbone of DNA carries a negative charge on every phosphate, and it is the same backbone whatever the sequence. So histones bind DNA by charge, all along it, and can package any stretch of it, whatever its sequence, which is exactly what a packaging protein has to do. They are also among the most unchanging proteins known: histone H4 from a pea and from a cow differ at two of their 102 amino acids. Almost any change to them, it seems, is harmful enough for selection to remove it.

Eight histones, two each of four kinds, assemble into a squat cylinder, and 147 base pairs of DNA wind around it in about one and two-thirds turns of a left-handed coil. That unit is the nucleosome. Nucleosomes repeat every 160 to 240 pairs, depending on the cell, so the stretch of linker DNA that runs on to the next is anything from about 15 to 90 pairs long, and a fifth histone, H1, sits where the DNA enters and leaves. Seen in the electron microscope, as it was in 1974, a strand of nucleosomes on their linkers looks like beads on a string, each bead about 11 nm across; the structure of one bead was worked out in atomic detail in 1997. The 147 pairs on one spool are 50 nm of DNA, held in a particle about a fifth of that across. DNA with its packing proteins, as it is found in a nucleus, is chromatin, a word coined in the nineteenth century for the part of the nucleus that took up dye, long before anyone knew what it was.

The nucleosome is the one level of packing nobody disputes. The next level was drawn for decades in every textbook as a regular fibre 30 nm thick, the beads stacked into a helix of their own. Such a fibre does form in a test tube, under some conditions. Whether it exists in a living nucleus is now doubted: when cells are frozen rather than chemically fixed, or their chromatin is stained so that it can be traced in three dimensions, most studies find no regular fibre at all outside a few specialised cells, but an irregular chain of nucleosomes between 5 and 24 nm across, packed more densely in some places than in others. It is a clean case of Section 3.1's rule that a structure earns belief only by surviving more than one way of preparing the sample, and the regular fibre is what has not survived the gentler ones.

Above that scale the chain is gathered into loops tens to hundreds of thousands of pairs long, whose bases are held by ring-shaped protein complexes that appear to make the loops by reeling DNA through themselves, and in the nucleus between divisions each chromosome occupies a region of its own rather than mingling with the others. At division, the subject of Chapter 10, a related ring complex compacts each chromosome much further, into the short rods that can be seen in a light microscope: chromosome 1's 8.5 cm becomes a rod some seven to ten micrometres long, packed about ten thousand times.

Packing is not only storage. Tightly packed chromatin, heterochromatin, stays dense and darkly staining through most of the life of the cell and holds few of the genes being read; it is concentrated around the centromeres, the narrowed points where a dividing chromosome is held, and in long stretches of repeated sequence. Loosely packed euchromatin holds most of the genes in use. A polymerase cannot copy DNA wound on a histone core, so at every fork the nucleosomes ahead are taken apart and assembled again behind, the old histones shared between the two new molecules and new ones added to make up the number. How a cell uses packing to decide which of its genes can be read is Chapter 12's.

Not every genome is packed this way, and the differences are informative. Almost all bacteria lack histones. A bacterium's chromosome is kept slightly underwound, by a topoisomerase that spends ATP to twist it, which makes it coil on itself and take up less room, and small proteins bend and bridge it into Section 3.3's nucleoid. Most archaea do have histones, and wrap their DNA on them, sometimes in a continuous ramp rather than in separate beads; their histones and ours share an ancestor, the pattern Section 3.3 described for the machinery of information generally. And the mitochondria in your cells, descended from a bacterium (Section 3.5), have no histones either: their small circle of DNA is packed by a different protein.

A human cell holds about two metres of DNA in a nucleus five to ten micrometres across. Histones, rich in positively charged side chains, bind the negatively charged backbone whatever its sequence; eight of them carry 147 base pairs wound about one and two-thirds times in a nucleosome, and nucleosomes on their linkers are the level of packing nobody disputes. Above them the chain is folded into loops and territories, and at division compacted about ten thousand times over; the regular 30 nm fibre of older diagrams may not exist in living cells.

A linear chromosome cannot copy its own ends

A bacterial chromosome, Section 3.3 said, is usually a single circle. Its two forks travel round it in opposite directions and meet on the far side, and every gap a primer leaves, the last included, has DNA beyond it from which a polymerase can extend into the gap. The chromosomes of a eukaryote are lines, and a line has ends.

Follow a fork to the very end of one. The leading strand runs off the end along with its template, and is complete. The lagging strand is made, as always, in fragments started on RNA primers near the fork, and the last of them is started on or near the last stretch of template. When the primers are removed, each gap is filled by a polymerase extending the fragment that lies beyond it, on the side of the fork, into the space. The gap left by the last primer has nothing beyond it: no fragment, no 3′ end, nothing for a polymerase to extend. It cannot be filled. So the new strand is shorter than its template at that end, and every round of copying shortens it again. This is the end-replication problem, pointed out independently by Alexey Olovnikov and by James Watson in the early 1970s. Human fibroblasts growing in a dish lose about 50 base pairs from each end at every division, several primers' worth, because the ends are also trimmed deliberately to leave a single-stranded tail, as below.

A second problem comes with the first. To a cell, the end of a linear chromosome looks exactly like a double-strand break (Section 8.5), and a cell's answer to a break is to join the ends it finds. Left to itself, it would fuse its chromosomes end to end. One structure solves both problems. A telomere is the end of a linear chromosome: in humans, the six-letter sequence TTAGGG repeated over and over, for about ten thousand base pairs at birth and fewer later, bound by a set of proteins that mark it as an end and not a break. The strand whose 3′ end lies at the tip is left longer than its partner, as a single-stranded tail roughly 75 to 300 nucleotides long, and in at least some cells the tail tucks back into the double-stranded repeats to form a loop that hides the end altogether. The repeats carry no genes. They are expendable, and losing some of them at a division costs nothing, until they run out.

That turns the end-replication problem into a counter. In 1961 Leonard Hayflick and Paul Moorhead reported that normal human cells grown in a dish do not divide for ever, as the cancer cells then used in laboratories did: fibroblasts, the cells that make connective tissue, divided a limited number of times and then stopped, alive but unable to divide again. Hayflick later put the number at about fifty for cells from fetal tissue, and fewer for cells taken from adults. The limit, now called the Hayflick limit, turned out to be largely a matter of telomeres. They shorten at each division in culture; a cell stops dividing not when its telomeres are used up but when the shortest of them fall below a critical length, long before the average telomere is gone; and giving such cells the enzyme described next lets them divide far beyond the limit.

The enzyme is telomerase, found in 1985 by Carol Greider and Elizabeth Blackburn in Tetrahymena, a single-celled pond organism whose working nucleus holds an unusually large number of small chromosomes, and so of ends. It solves the end problem by bringing its own template. Telomerase is a protein together with a molecule of RNA, and a short part of the RNA's sequence is complementary to the telomere's repeat. The enzyme pairs that part of its RNA with the overhanging 3′ end of the chromosome and extends the end by copying the RNA, one repeat's worth; then it shifts along and adds another. Making DNA from an RNA template runs the usual direction of copying in reverse, and an enzyme that does it is a reverse transcriptase; the protein of telomerase is one. Once the tail has been lengthened, primase and polymerase fill in the partner strand as they would any lagging strand, and the telomere is longer than it was. Blackburn, Greider and Jack Szostak shared a Nobel Prize for the work in 2009.

Telomerase is active in the cells that give rise to eggs and sperm, which is why each generation starts with long telomeres, and in some of the stem cells that renew tissues. In most other human cells it is scarce or absent. It is active in most cancers, around 85 to 90 per cent of them, and most of the rest keep their telomeres by a route that uses recombination instead. That is no coincidence: a cell that is to divide without limit has to solve the end problem somehow, and the shortening of telomeres is one of the barriers a cancer has to break through. What telomere shortening does to the ageing of a whole body is much less clear than what it does in a dish. Short telomeres go with some of the diseases of old age, but laboratory mice have much longer telomeres than people and telomerase active in many tissues, and they still age, faster than we do, so the counter cannot be the whole of the clock. A lagging strand cannot be finished at the very end of a linear molecule, because the gap left by the last primer has nothing beyond it to extend from, so each copy is a little shorter than its template. Telomeres — a short sequence repeated more than a thousand times, capped by proteins that stop the end being treated as a break — are what is lost instead. Telomerase carries its own RNA template and adds repeats back; it is active in the cells that make eggs and sperm and in most cancers, and scarce in most other cells, which is why a normal human cell in a dish stops after a limited number of divisions — about fifty, for cells from fetal tissue.

Most of a genome is not genes

Section 1.5 gave two numbers for the human genome: about 3.1 billion base pairs, and around 20,000 genes that code for proteins. Put them together. A typical human protein is a few hundred amino acids long, and at three bases to an amino acid — Chapter 9 says why three — its coding sequence is about a thousand to fifteen hundred base pairs. Twenty thousand of those come to about 30 million pairs, one per cent of the genome. The sequence that actually codes for protein is a scattering of small islands in a very large sea, and the obvious question is what the sea is.

Part of the answer is inside the genes themselves. Most human genes are interrupted: the coding sequence is broken into pieces separated by stretches called introns, which are copied into RNA along with the rest and cut out of it before it is used, as Chapter 9 describes. Introns are often far longer than the pieces they separate, and together they make up about a quarter of the genome. Part of the sea is sequence that controls when and where a gene is used, short stretches that proteins bind to switch it on or off, which is Chapter 12's subject; part is genes for RNAs that do their work without being translated; part is pseudogenes, broken copies of genes that no longer make anything. But the largest single part is something else.

Barbara McClintock found the first transposable elements in maize in the 1940s, as genetic elements that moved from one place on a chromosome to another and changed the working of genes where they landed, and she was awarded an unshared Nobel Prize for it in 1983. There are two main kinds. DNA transposons are cut out of one place and pasted into another by an enzyme they encode themselves. Retrotransposons copy and paste: the element is copied into RNA, a reverse transcriptase of the kind telomerase contains copies the RNA back into DNA, and the new copy is inserted somewhere else while the original stays where it was. Copy and paste is how a sequence comes to fill a genome. The element called LINE-⁠1, about six thousand base pairs long when complete, makes up about 17 per cent of the human genome on its own, in some half a million copies. The Alu element, only about 300 pairs long, has over a million copies and makes up about 10 per cent — and cannot move by itself at all, but borrows the machinery LINE-⁠1 makes. Almost every one of those copies is a fossil, cut short when it was inserted or worn down by millions of years of mutation until it can no longer move; only about a hundred copies of LINE-⁠1 in a typical human genome still can.

Why should a genome be half made of such things? Because they copy themselves, and that is enough. An element that lands in a gene that matters usually harms its host, and Section 1.7's natural selection removes it, since the host leaves fewer descendants. An element that lands where nothing depends on the sequence does no harm, and nothing removes it. Over hundreds of millions of years copies accumulate wherever they are tolerated, and mutation slowly wears them away. In 1980 two pairs of authors, in papers printed one after the other, called such sequences selfish DNA: they spread because they are good at spreading, not because they do anything for the organism, and selection on the organism is not the only selection there is. A few have since been put to work by their hosts — the enzyme that cuts the genes for antibodies apart, so that their pieces can be joined in new combinations, descends from a transposable element, and a protein the placenta uses to fuse its cells together descends from a virus that settled in the genome of an ancestor — but those are exceptions, recruited from a very large supply.

This is why the size of a genome says so little about the organism that carries it. The pufferfish Takifugu has about as many genes as a human in about an eighth as much DNA. An onion has about five times as much DNA as a human, some lungfish around forty times, and the record, held by a small fern from New Caledonia, Tmesipteris oblanceolata, is about fifty times. Across animals the number of genes varies by a factor of a few; the amount of DNA varies by a factor of thousands. The difference is mostly transposable elements and other repeats. The lack of any steady relationship between the amount of DNA and the complexity of the organism is the C-value paradox, named after an old term for the amount of DNA in one set of chromosomes, and it stops being a paradox as soon as most of the DNA is known not to be genes.

How much of the rest does anything? The phrase junk DNA was in use by the 1960s and was made famous in 1972 by Susumu Ohno, who used it first for the dead copies of genes left behind when a gene is duplicated, and it has been argued over ever since. In 2012 a large project called ENCODE reported that about 80 per cent of the human genome showed some biochemical activity — it was copied into RNA in some cell, or bound by some protein, or chemically marked — and some reports announced that junk DNA was dead. Critics answered that activity is not function. Copying into RNA is noisy, and a protein that binds DNA will bind many sequences by chance; a sequence can be active and do nothing that matters. The test of function that evolution applies is whether changes to a sequence do harm, and a sequence whose changes do harm is held the same by natural selection over long periods while the rest drifts. By that test, comparing our genome with those of other mammals, somewhere around a tenth of the human genome is conserved. That fraction includes most of the sequence coding for protein and much of the sequence that controls it, and it is several times the coding sequence alone, which is why "non-coding" and "non-functional" are not the same word. Much of the remainder may do nothing that depends on its sequence. The onion is the standing reminder: if most of a human genome were essential, it would be hard to say why an onion needs five times as much.

So the honest summary is a range, as Section 7.6's was. About one per cent of the human genome codes for protein. Something like a tenth is conserved by selection and so is doing something that matters. Most of the rest shows no sign of being conserved, and much of it is the remains of elements that copied themselves in. Junk is not a settled word for all of it. It is a claim about a particular sequence, which can be tested, and for most sequences the test has not yet been made.

The genome of your mitochondria is the opposite case: 16,569 base pairs, 37 genes, and almost nothing between them, which is how the bacterial genomes they descend from are built too — E. coli's is close to nine-tenths coding sequence. Why genomes differ so much in this is still argued over. One explanation is that selection against useless DNA is strong enough to clear it only in organisms with enormous populations, such as bacteria, and too weak to clear it from animals and plants, whose populations are small by comparison.

That is the archive, then: a molecule identified by elimination, measured before it was modelled, copied by a machine that works in one direction and is checked three times, mended from its second strand, wound on spools, capped at its ends, and filled for the most part with copies of things that copy themselves. What the genes in it say, and how a cell reads a sequence of bases into a protein, is the next chapter's.

About one per cent of the human genome codes for protein. About a quarter is introns and nearly half is transposable elements, almost all of them broken copies that can no longer move, which spread because they copy themselves. Genome size follows such elements rather than an organism's complexity. Around a tenth of the genome is conserved by selection and much more is biochemically active; junk is a claim about a particular sequence, to be tested, not a name for everything that does not code for protein.

Key ideas

  • Genes were known to lie on chromosomes, made of DNA and protein, and protein was the favoured candidate: twenty kinds of amino acid against four nucleotides, and a DNA wrongly believed to repeat one short unit.
  • Griffith found that something in heat-killed virulent pneumococci could permanently transform live harmless ones. Avery, MacLeod and McCarty purified it and found it had the chemistry of DNA and was destroyed only by an enzyme that destroys DNA.
  • Hershey and Chase labelled a bacteriophage's protein with sulfur and its DNA with phosphorus: the DNA entered the cell and reached the next generation, and most of the protein stayed outside. The experiment was less clean than it is usually drawn, and its force came from failing in a different place from Avery's.
  • The double helix was measured before it was built. Franklin's X-ray photographs gave a helix 2 nm wide, a turn every 3.4 nm and bases stacked 0.34 nm apart; her other data put the backbones outside and required the chains to run in opposite directions. Watson and Crick's contribution was the pairing.
  • A purine always pairs with a pyrimidine, adenine with thymine and guanine with cytosine, and the two pairs are the same width. That explains Chargaff's rules: in double-stranded DNA there is as much A as T and as much G as C, while the proportion of the two pairs varies between species.
  • Each strand has a 5′ end and a 3′ end, and the two strands of the helix are antiparallel.
  • Because each strand specifies its partner, each serves as a template, and every new double helix keeps one old strand. Meselson and Stahl showed it with heavy nitrogen: one generation ruled out conservative copying and the second ruled out dispersive copying.
  • DNA polymerase needs a template, a primer ending in a free 3′ hydroxyl, and nucleoside triphosphates. It adds only to a 3′ end, the energy comes from the incoming nucleotide's own phosphates, and splitting the pyrophosphate released makes each addition one-way.
  • Because the templates are antiparallel and polymerases extend only 3′ ends, the leading strand is made continuously and the lagging strand backwards in Okazaki fragments, each begun on an RNA primer and later joined by ligase. Helicase opens the helix, and topoisomerases cut and reseal it to relieve the twist ahead of the fork.
  • A bacterium copies its circular chromosome from one origin in about forty minutes; a human cell uses tens of thousands of origins to copy 6.2 billion base pairs in about eight hours.
  • Pairing alone would give about one error in a hundred. The polymerase's choice of nucleotide, proofreading and mismatch repair bring it to between one in a billion and one in ten billion per base pair per round of copying. At one in ten billion that is fewer than one new error for each human daughter cell, while each child carries some sixty or seventy new mutations; the three numbers are per base pair, per cell and per child.
  • DNA polymerases grow strands only at the 3′ end because that is the only direction in which a wrong nucleotide can be cut off and the strand still extended.
  • DNA is damaged ten to twenty thousand times a day in every cell by water and reactive oxygen alone, and by ultraviolet light besides wherever sunlight reaches, and nearly all of it is repaired by cutting out the damaged strand and rebuilding it from the other. DNA uses thymine rather than uracil so that a deaminated cytosine can be recognised as damage. A break through both strands has no partner to repair from and is the most dangerous damage of all.
  • About two metres of DNA fit in a human nucleus because histones, binding the backbone by charge whatever the sequence, wind it into nucleosomes of 147 base pairs, which are folded into loops; at division a chromosome is about ten thousand times shorter than the DNA it holds. The regular 30 nm fibre of older diagrams may not exist in living cells.
  • A linear chromosome cannot copy the very end of its lagging strand, so it shortens at every division. Telomeres are the expendable repeats that are lost instead, and telomerase, a reverse transcriptase carrying its own RNA template, adds them back in the cells that make eggs and sperm and in most cancers.
  • About one per cent of the human genome codes for protein and nearly half is transposable elements, mostly broken. Genome size does not follow complexity, about a tenth of the genome is conserved by selection, and junk is a claim to be tested sequence by sequence rather than a settled label.

Check your understanding

Bacteriophages whose protein carries radioactive sulfur are allowed to infect bacteria. After a few minutes the mixture is blended and spun, so that the bacteria form a pellet and the phage coats stay in the liquid. Where is most of the radioactive sulfur, and why?

  • In the pellet, because the phage's protein enters the bacterium to direct the making of new phages.
  • In the liquid, with the empty coats, because the protein stays outside the cell while the DNA goes in.
  • Divided equally between the pellet and the liquid, because both the protein and the DNA contain sulfur.
  • In the pellet, because blending breaks the coats and their fragments stick to the outside of the bacteria.

Three-quarters or more of the sulfur came off with the coats. The idea that the protein enters the cell to direct the making of new phages is the hypothesis the experiment was built to test, and the result goes against it. An equal split because both molecules contain sulfur fails on the chemistry that made the experiment possible: DNA contains no sulfur, which is why sulfur could label the protein alone. Broken coats sticking to the bacteria is a real effect put in the wrong place — a fifth or more of the sulfur did stay with the cells, which is part of why the experiment was less clean than it is usually drawn — but most of it did not, and the new phages carried less than 1 per cent of it against 30 per cent or more of the phosphorus, which labelled the DNA in the other half of the experiment.

Which feature of the double helix came from Watson and Crick's model-building rather than from measurements made in Rosalind Franklin's laboratory?

  • That the molecule is a helix about 2 nm across.
  • That the bases are stacked 0.34 nm apart, ten to each turn.
  • That the sugar–phosphate backbones lie on the outside.
  • That the two chains run in opposite directions.
  • That adenine pairs with thymine and guanine with cytosine.

The pairing was Watson's and Crick's, worked out with cardboard bases once Donohue had corrected the forms the bases take: the two pairs came out the same width, and they explained Chargaff's rules. The helix, its width and the spacing of its bases were read from Franklin's photographs, and her work on how the fibres took up water put the backbones outside. The chains running in opposite directions is the feature most often credited wrongly: Crick drew that conclusion, but he drew it from a symmetry Franklin had measured in the A form, so the evidence for it came from her laboratory.

Bacteria grown for many generations on heavy nitrogen are moved to light nitrogen and allowed to divide once. Their DNA forms a single band halfway between heavy and light. What does this one result rule out?

  • Both conservative and dispersive copying, which proves that copying is semiconservative.
  • Conservative copying alone, which predicts two bands, one heavy and one light.
  • Semiconservative copying, which predicts one heavy band and one light band.
  • Nothing, because heavy and light DNA differ in density by too little to separate.

One band at the hybrid position is exactly what the semiconservative scheme predicts, but the dispersive scheme predicts it too: every molecule half old and half new, whether as two whole strands or as a patchwork. Reading this one result as ruling out both rivals is the commonest error, treating one generation as if it were two: the dispersive scheme is ruled out only by the second, where the DNA splits into a hybrid band and a light one. One heavy band and one light band is the conservative prediction under the wrong name. And a difference in density of less than one per cent is no obstacle: density-gradient centrifugation separates heavy DNA from light cleanly, which is what the experiment itself showed.

Why is one of the two new strands at a replication fork made in short fragments?

  • Because its template is damaged more often than the other, and is copied in pieces between the damaged places.
  • Because primase can lay primers on only one of the two templates, and each of its primers starts a fragment.
  • Because the templates are antiparallel and polymerases add only to 3′ ends, so one strand grows away from the fork.
  • Because the helicase unwinds one of the strands faster than the other, so that strand is exposed in bursts.
  • Because short fragments are easier to proofread than one long strand, so one of the two new strands is copied in pieces.

Together, antiparallel templates and a polymerase that adds only at a 3′ end are the cause: on one template the new strand grows away from the fork, so it has to be started again each time the fork opens more template. A polymerase that could add at either end would make both strands continuously; parallel strands would not, because the fork leaving the origin the other way would then have both its new strands made in pieces. Primase is not confined to one template — the lagging strand needs far more primers than the leading one, one for every fragment. Proofreading does depend on the direction strands grow in, but it works on the growing end of both strands and is not what makes one of them discontinuous. Damage and the helicase play no part: the helicase opens both templates together, as one fork.

With proofreading and mismatch repair working, copying leaves about one error per ten billion base pairs copied. A human cell copies its 6.2 billion base pairs and divides. On average, how many new copying errors does each of the two daughter cells receive?

  • About one error in every ten billion cells, since the rate is one in ten billion.
  • About 0.6, since 6.2 billion base pairs times one in ten billion is 0.62.
  • About 1.2, because both strands of each daughter's DNA have just been made.
  • About seventy, the number of new mutations each child carries.

A rate needs a denominator, and this one is per base pair per round of copying, so it has to be multiplied by the number of base pairs copied, which gives fewer than one new error for each daughter cell. Applying it to cells instead of base pairs answers a different question. Doubling the count because both strands have just been made forgets Section 8.3: copying is semiconservative, so each daughter keeps one old strand, which carries no new copying errors, and only its new strand does. Seventy is a real number of a different kind, new mutations per generation of people, which spans many cell divisions and includes damage that was never a copying error.
