What is life?

Biology begins with a question that sounds simple and is not. This chapter takes the question apart, meets the cell, and looks at how the answer was found.

The question biology asks

Hold a drop of pond water up to the light and you will see nothing. Put it under a microscope and it fills with movement: slipper-shaped paramecia rowing themselves along with thousands of tiny hairs, green euglena spiralling towards the light, diatoms in glass cases drifting past, an amoeba flowing over the slide like a spilled drop. None of them has a brain. None of them knows it is being watched. Yet no one who watches them doubts that they are alive, and no one doubts that the glass beneath them is not.

Biology is the study of living things, so it starts by asking what a living thing is. The question is harder than it sounds. Nobody has ever written a definition of life that everyone accepts. What biologists have instead is a list of properties that every organism shares and that nothing non-living shares all of, together with a growing understanding of how those properties arise from ordinary chemistry.

A list makes the borderline cases visible, and the borderline is where the science gets interesting. Viruses, dormant seeds, red blood cells and sterile mules each break one rule and keep the others. That is why biologists do not rely on a single test but on a family of properties, taken together.

What all living things share

Seven properties recur in every organism biologists have examined, from the smallest bacterium to the blue whale. They are not a definition, but together they draw the boundary.

  • OrderMade of one or more cells, each wrapped in a membrane and full of structure at every scale.
  • MetabolismTakes in energy and matter and transforms them: building up, breaking down, passing energy through. This is metabolism.
  • RegulationKeeps its interior steady while the outside changes: homeostasis.
  • Growth and developmentGrows by making more of itself according to instructions, and passes through stages on the way.
  • ResponseSenses its surroundings and reacts: a root turning towards water, a bacterium swimming towards sugar, a pupil narrowing.
  • ReproductionMakes new organisms of the same kind, handing on instructions written in DNA.
  • AdaptationPopulations change across generations, so organisms fit their environments. The fit is the mark of a history.

Of the seven, homeostasis is the one you can feel. Your core temperature is about 37 °C whether you are standing in snow or running in the sun, and it is held there by a loop. Sensors in your skin and in the hypothalamus, at the base of the brain, report the temperature. The hypothalamus compares the report with a set point. When the two disagree, it recruits effectors that push the temperature back: sweat glands and widened skin vessels shed heat, shivering muscles and narrowed vessels keep it. Because the response opposes the change that triggered it, the loop is called negative feedback, and nearly every steady quantity in your body, from blood sugar to blood pressure to the acidity of your blood, is held by a loop of the same shape.

A fever is not a failure of the loop. It is the set point moved up on purpose, by signalling molecules released during an infection. The loop then defends 39 °C as faithfully as it defended 37.

Figure 1.2 is a model, and a crude one: real thermoregulation involves hormones, behaviour and a dozen tissues, and the model runs far faster than a body does, so a cold plunge that would play out over many minutes takes seconds here. But the shape of the loop is exact, and once you can see it you will find it everywhere in the chapters that follow.

Living things are not steady because nothing changes. They are steady because they keep changing to stay the same.

Life is organised in levels

Take a frog apart and you find organs. Take the heart apart and you find tissues; take those apart and you find cells, and inside them organelles, molecules, and finally atoms that are the same atoms found in a rock. Go the other way and the frog is one member of a population, which lives with other populations in a community, which with its non-living surroundings forms an ecosystem, and all the ecosystems together make up the biosphere.

Figure 1.3 follows one thread through all twelve levels. At each step up, something appears that was not present in the parts. A heart pumps; no single heart cell does. A community has a food web; no single frog does. This is an emergent property, and it is why biology cannot be replaced by chemistry even though every living thing obeys chemistry's laws. The properties that matter to a frog live at the level of the frog.

The most important step on that ladder is the one we cannot see. Cells are small. A typical animal cell is about 20 micrometres across, one-fiftieth of a millimetre, so a thousand of them side by side would just span the width of a thumbnail. Below them lie another five powers of ten before the atoms.

A micrometre (µm) is a millionth of a metre; a nanometre (nm) is a billionth. Cells are measured in micrometres, molecules in nanometres.

Figure 1.4 also shows why the cell was discovered so late in the history of science. Nothing on the ruler below about a tenth of a millimetre can be seen without a lens, and nothing below about 200 nanometres, half the wavelength of visible light, can be seen with an ordinary light microscope. The fine structure inside the cell, the membranes and ribosomes and the folds of a mitochondrion, had to wait for the electron microscope, invented in the 1930s and turned on cells in the 1940s.

The cell is the unit of life

Every living thing is made of cells. Some are one cell; you are about 37 trillion of them. This was not obvious. Cells were first seen in 1665, when Robert Hooke looked at a sliver of cork through a microscope and named the little boxes he saw after the cells of a monastery. A few years later Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, grinding his own lenses in Delft, saw living single cells swimming in pond water and in scrapings from his own teeth. It took until 1838 and 1839 for Matthias Schleiden and Theodor Schwann to argue that all plants and all animals are built from cells, and until 1855 for Rudolf Virchow to add the claim that closed the loop: every cell comes from a cell that already existed.

Cell theory, as it is now stated, makes three claims, and they are still the foundation of biology:

  1. All living things are made of one or more cells.
  2. The cell is the smallest unit of life. Nothing smaller is alive on its own.
  3. Every cell arises from a pre-existing cell.

There are two kinds of cell. A prokaryote, which is what every bacterium and archaeon is, has no nucleus: its DNA lies in the cytoplasm, and its cell is typically one to five micrometres across. A eukaryote keeps its DNA inside a nucleus and divides the rest of the cell into membrane-bound compartments, the organelles, each with a job. Eukaryotic cells are usually ten to a hundred micrometres across, and every animal, plant and fungus is made of them.

Life runs on information

A crystal grows by copying its own arrangement. A cell grows by reading instructions. Those instructions are written in DNA, a molecule shaped like a twisted ladder: two backbones of alternating sugar and phosphate, joined by rungs, each rung a base pair. There are four bases, and they pair by a fixed rule: adenine with thymine, guanine with cytosine. The pairing rule is the whole secret. Because A always faces T and G always faces C, either strand carries enough information to rebuild the other. That is how a cell copies its DNA before it divides, and how damage on one strand can be repaired from the other. Figure 1.6 shows three turns of the molecule at its true proportions.

A gene is a stretch of DNA that specifies a product, usually a protein. Your genome is about 3.1 billion base pairs long and carries around 20,000 protein-coding genes on 23 chromosomes, and every cell of yours carries two sets, 46 in all. The bacterium Escherichia coli manages with 4.6 million base pairs and about 4,300 genes on a single circular chromosome. The striking thing is not the difference in size but the sameness of the system: the same four bases, the same pairing rule, and with only rare small variations the same code for translating DNA into protein, in every organism ever examined. It is the strongest single piece of evidence that all known life descends from a common ancestor.

Heredity is chemistry. Information is stored in the order of bases and copied by the rule that each base has one partner.

Energy flows and matter cycles

Living things are ordered, and order does not maintain itself. Left alone, a cell's molecules would drift towards disorder, the way a room does. To keep building and repairing, an organism must take in energy from outside and spend it. That is what metabolism is for. The second law of thermodynamics is not suspended by life; it is paid for, continuously.

Nearly all the energy that living things use arrives as sunlight. Producers, which are plants, algae and photosynthetic bacteria, capture it and store it in the chemical bonds of sugars they make from carbon dioxide and water. Consumers get their energy by eating producers or other consumers. Decomposers, mostly fungi and bacteria, take what is left. At every transfer most of the energy leaves as heat: only about a tenth of the energy stored in one level ends up stored in the next, which is why food chains are short and why there are far fewer foxes than rabbits, and far fewer rabbits than blades of grass.

Matter does the opposite. The carbon in a rabbit's muscle came from carbon dioxide in the air by way of grass, and it will return to the air when the rabbit breathes out, or when a fox or a fungus takes the rabbit apart. Energy passes through an ecosystem once and leaves. Matter goes around and around. Figure 1.7 draws the two paths through one small ecosystem.

Evolution explains the pattern

Why does a whale have finger bones inside its flipper? Why do humans, mice and yeast copy their DNA with the same molecular machinery? Why does every living thing share the seven properties of Section 1.2? The answer to all three is the same: they are related. Life on Earth is one family tree, and evolution is the name for how it grew.

Charles Darwin's argument, published in 1859 after Alfred Russel Wallace had reached the same idea on his own, is simple. Individuals in a population vary. Some of that variation is inherited. More offspring are produced than can survive, so individuals whose inherited traits suit their environment leave more descendants than the others. Over generations the population changes, and it changes in a particular direction: towards a better fit with its way of life. Darwin called the process natural selection, and the fit it produces is adaptation. Other processes change populations too, but natural selection is the only one that produces adaptation.

“Nothing in biology makes sense except in the light of evolution.”Theodosius Dobzhansky, title of a 1973 essay

Comparing DNA sequences lets biologists reconstruct the tree. Its deepest split parts Bacteria from the lineage that holds Archaea and Eukarya, and those three groups are the domains: Bacteria; Archaea, prokaryotes first found in hot springs and salt lakes and since found everywhere; and Eukarya. Eukaryotes arose, roughly two billion years ago, when a cell of an archaeal lineage took in a bacterium and kept it. That bacterium became the mitochondrion. Later, the ancestors of plants took in a photosynthetic cyanobacterium, which became the chloroplast. Both events are endosymbiosis, and the partners are still together: the mitochondrion in every one of your cells, the chloroplast in every leaf. All animals, plants and fungi are a few twigs on the eukaryote branch, as Figure 1.8 shows.

The unity of life, from the shared genetic code to the shared properties of Section 1.2, is what a single family tree looks like from the inside.

How biologists know

Everything above is knowledge, and knowledge has a history. For two thousand years it was obvious that life could arise from non-life: maggots from meat, mice from stored grain, microbes from broth. This was spontaneous generation, and it was not a foolish idea; it was what the evidence looked like. In 1668 Francesco Redi showed that maggots appear on meat only when flies can reach it. Microbes were harder. Boiled broth in a sealed flask stayed clear, while broth open to the air went cloudy. Was it the air's microbes, or did life need fresh air to generate itself?

Between 1859 and 1861 Louis Pasteur answered with a flask whose neck he drew out into an S-curve. Air could enter freely; dust, and the microbes riding on it, settled in the bend. Boiled broth in such a flask stayed clear for months. Snap the neck, or tilt the flask so that the broth touched the dust trapped in the bend, and it clouded within days. The experiment is a model of the method: a hypothesis that microbes come from other microbes carried in the air; a prediction that could have failed; a controlled experiment in which only one thing was changed; and a result that settled the question because there was no longer any way to explain it that spontaneous generation could survive.

Most of biology is found the way Pasteur found this (Figure 1.9): an observation raises a question; a hypothesis proposes an answer and makes a prediction; an experiment or a careful comparison tests the prediction, holding everything else constant; the result is analysed, and either the hypothesis survives for now or it is revised. Then the work is published, criticised, and repeated by other people. A hypothesis that has been tested many ways, over many years, by many people, and has explained a wide range of facts without failing, earns the name theory. Cell theory is one. The theory of evolution is another. In science the word marks the strongest kind of knowledge there is, not a guess.

Science does not prove. It tests. A claim earns trust by surviving honest attempts to break it.

Key ideas

  • Life has no single definition. It is recognised by a family of shared properties: order, metabolism, regulation, growth and development, response, reproduction, and adaptation.
  • Homeostasis is maintained by negative feedback: sensors, a set point, and effectors whose response opposes the change.
  • Life is organised in levels, and new properties emerge at each level from the arrangement of the parts.
  • The cell is the unit of life: all organisms are made of cells, and every cell comes from a cell.
  • Prokaryotes have no nucleus; eukaryotes keep their DNA in a nucleus and divide their cells into organelles.
  • DNA stores information as the order of four bases and copies it by complementary pairing, in the same way in every known organism.
  • Energy flows through ecosystems and leaves as heat; matter cycles.
  • Evolution by natural selection explains both the unity of life and its fit to the environment.
  • Biology is built from hypotheses that survive controlled tests; a theory is the most thoroughly tested kind of explanation.

Glossary

Check your understanding

Which of these is not a property shared by all living things?

  • Being made of cells
  • Keeping the interior steady
  • Moving from place to place
  • Using energy

Plants, fungi and many bacteria stay put for their whole lives. Every living thing responds to its surroundings, but responding is not the same as moving.

A virus has genes and evolves, yet most biologists do not count it as fully alive. Why?

  • It is too small to see with a light microscope.
  • It has no cells and no metabolism of its own, and it cannot reproduce without a host cell.
  • It cannot evolve.
  • It contains neither DNA nor RNA.

Size is not a criterion, viruses do evolve, and every virus carries DNA or RNA. What they lack is the machinery: a virus is instructions without a cell to run them.

“A heart pumps blood, but no single heart cell does.” This is an example of:

  • Homeostasis
  • An emergent property
  • Natural selection
  • Metabolism

Pumping appears at the level of the organ, from the arrangement and coordination of the cells. It is not a property of any part alone.

Which statement about an ecosystem is correct?

  • Both energy and matter cycle.
  • Both energy and matter pass through once.
  • Energy passes through once and leaves as heat; matter cycles.
  • Matter passes through once; energy cycles.

Sunlight enters, is stored in sugars, and is lost as heat at every transfer, so an ecosystem needs a constant supply. The carbon, nitrogen and other atoms are used again and again.

Pasteur's swan-neck flask stayed clear because:

  • No air could reach the broth.
  • Air entered, but dust and microbes settled in the bend of the neck.
  • The flask was sealed with wax.
  • The glass killed any microbes that touched it.

The point of the swan neck was to let air in while keeping dust out. Tilting the flask so the broth touched the trapped dust made it cloud, which showed that the dust, not the air, carried the microbes.
