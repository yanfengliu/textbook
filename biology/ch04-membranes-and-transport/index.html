Why a membrane builds itself

Every compartment in the last chapter had the same edge. The nuclear envelope, the endoplasmic reticulum, the Golgi stack, a lysosome, a vesicle, the two wrappings of a mitochondrion, the tonoplast around a vacuole, and the outer boundary of the cell itself are all made of one material, laid down to one design, about 7 nm thick. Chapter 3 drew that edge as a line and deferred it three times. It is not a line. It is the most consequential structure in the cell, and the strangest thing about it is that nothing makes it.

The molecule it is made of came up in Section 2.6. A phospholipid is a small molecule with two personalities: a head carrying a negatively charged phosphate group, which water is glad to hydrogen-bond to and which is therefore hydrophilic, and two hydrocarbon tails, which are as hydrophobic as anything in Section 2.5 and which water will not have at any price. A molecule built like that is amphipathic, and it cannot be comfortable. Dissolve it and the tails are exposed; keep it dry and the head is unsatisfied. There is no arrangement in which a single phospholipid molecule is at peace in water, and that is exactly why a crowd of them does something interesting.

Section 2.3 is the whole mechanism, and it needs nothing added to it. Water does not repel a hydrocarbon tail; it excludes it. A nonpolar chain sitting in water forces the water around it into a more restricted arrangement, and gathering all the tails into one place shrinks the surface where that restriction applies. So the tails are pressed together, not because they attract one another but because the water is pushing. That is the hydrophobic effect, which Chapter 2 said in passing was the force that folds a protein and assembles a membrane. This is the membrane. Tip phospholipids into water and let go, and they find the arrangement that hides the most tail: a sheet two molecules thick, tails turned inward to meet in an oily middle, charged heads facing the water on both faces. That is a lipid bilayer, and Figure 4.1 is nothing but this happening. Nothing in the figure assembles anything. The only rule it obeys is the one Section 2.3 established, and the sheet is what falls out of it.

Which arrangement a lipid takes depends on its shape. Two tails and a small head make a rough cylinder, and cylinders tile into a flat sheet. One tail and a large head make a wedge, and wedges close into a small sphere. That is why a detergent dissolves a membrane: its molecules are wedges, and they take the cylinders away into wedge-shaped company.

Change the molecule and you change the answer. A detergent, with one tail and a bulky head, cannot make a sheet; it makes a micelle, a ball perhaps 5 nm across with the tails at its centre — which is how soap lifts grease, and how a biochemist takes a membrane apart. A pure hydrocarbon, with no head at all, has nothing to face the water with and simply gathers into a droplet. Cholesterol, which is a stiff ring system with one small hydroxyl group, cannot make anything by itself, but it slots between phospholipid tails and stays there. The bilayer is not a general property of fat. It is what molecules of one particular shape do.

A bilayer cannot tolerate an edge. At the rim of a sheet the tails are exposed to water — precisely the state the whole arrangement exists to avoid — so any sheet large enough to bend will curl round and fuse with itself, and the edge disappears. A membrane is therefore always a closed bag. There is no such thing as a piece of membrane with a border; a compartment is not something a cell has to seal, but something it cannot avoid making. Shake phospholipids in water and you get liposomes: hollow spheres of bilayer with water trapped inside, made by no machinery at all. They are used to carry drugs into cells, and they are the reason some people think the first cell had a membrane before it had anything else.

A bilayer also heals. Push a glass needle through a cell and withdraw it, and the hole closes in moments, because a hole has an edge and an edge costs. A whole technique rests on this: physiologists routinely impale cells on micropipettes, and cells routinely survive it. Figure 4.1 has a needle, and watching the puncture close is the clearest demonstration in the chapter that the arrangement is not held in place but continually re-found.

And nothing covalent holds the sheet together. Every phospholipid in a membrane is bound to its neighbours only by the weak interactions of Section 2.2 — each one worth a few kilojoules per mole, comparable with the thermal jostling at body temperature. Any individual contact is being made and broken constantly. The sheet persists because there are so many of them and because leaving it is worse, in the way a crowd persists although no two people in it stay together. That is the subject of the next section, and it is why a membrane, for all that it is a barrier, is also a liquid.

Some arithmetic to fix the scale. A square micrometre of bilayer holds about five million lipid molecules, and the plasma membrane of a small animal cell holds of the order of a thousand million. About half the mass of that membrane is not lipid at all but protein, which is the next section's business. The whole sandwich is about 7 nm from one watery face to the other — the dark, pale, dark triple line an electron microscope shows, and the reason Section 3.1 said a membrane cannot be seen in a light microscope. The oily core in the middle of it, the part that does the excluding, is only three or four nanometres deep. Everything in the rest of this chapter is a consequence of that three-nanometre layer of grease.

The red blood cell was the right choice for this experiment and nearly the only one: it has no nucleus, no mitochondria and no internal membranes, so all the lipid extracted came from the surface. In any other cell most of the lipid would have come from inside, and the ratio would have meant nothing.

How was any of this known before anyone could see it? In 1925 Evert Gorter and François Grendel extracted the lipid from a counted number of red blood cells, spread it on the surface of water in a trough, and compressed it into a film one molecule thick. Its area came out at about twice the area of the cells it came from. Two layers, therefore: the first evidence that a membrane is a bilayer, and it was obtained by measuring a puddle. The honest footnote is that both their measurements were wrong — they did not extract all the lipid, and they underestimated the surface area of a red cell — and the two errors happened to run in opposite directions and nearly cancel. A right conclusion from a wrong measurement is still a wrong measurement. It was later redone properly, and the answer held.

What came next was wrong for thirty years. In 1935 Hugh Davson and James Danielli proposed that the bilayer was coated on both faces with a continuous layer of protein, like a sandwich, which fitted the electron micrographs of the day and explained why membranes were not simply oily. The model was overturned by freeze-fracture: freeze a cell, strike it with a knife, and the fracture runs along the weakest plane in the specimen, which is the oily midline between the two leaflets. That splits the membrane open like a book, and the exposed face is not smooth. It is cobbled with particles eight or nine nanometres across, which are proteins — sitting in the bilayer, spanning it, not spread over it. A sandwich has no such particles in the middle. The technique answered the question because it looked at the one plane no other method could reach.

Nothing assembles a membrane. Water excludes the tails, the bilayer is what that exclusion leaves behind, and because an exposed edge is the one thing it cannot tolerate, a membrane is always a closed bag that heals itself.

A mosaic that flows

In 1972 Seymour Singer and Garth Nicolson proposed the picture that has held ever since: the fluid mosaic model. The bilayer is a two-dimensional fluid, and the proteins are not a coating but a mosaic of pieces floating in it — some spanning it, some attached to one face, most of them free to drift about in the plane. It is worth being clear about what is being claimed, because the model's name has become so familiar that its content is easy to miss. The claim is that a membrane is a liquid in two directions and a solid in the third.

Take fluidity first, and measure it rather than asserting it. A lipid molecule in a membrane exchanges places with its neighbour some ten million times a second, and wanders, by that exchange, about two micrometres in a second — the length of a bacterium. Its diffusion coefficient is around 1 µm2 per second, which is a thousand times smaller than the figure Section 3.2 used for a small molecule in water, so a membrane is a very viscous liquid; but a molecule still crosses a cell in a few seconds. Membranes are as fluid as olive oil, and nothing in one stays where it was put.

The asymmetry is maintained, not merely inherited. Enzymes called flippases spend ATP to move particular lipids from one leaflet to the other, and the arrangement they keep up is read by other cells: when phosphatidylserine, normally confined to the inner leaflet, appears on the outer face, it is one of the signals that marks a cell as dying and to be eaten.

Now the third direction. For a lipid to swap leaflets — to flip-flop — its charged head must be dragged through the oily core, which is the very journey the core exists to prevent. In a synthetic bilayer any given molecule does it less than once a month. Set that beside ten million sideways swaps a second and the contrast is thirteen orders of magnitude. The consequence is the most useful fact in this section: the two halves of a membrane are separate populations. A cell can give its two faces different lipids and different proteins and they will stay different, because nothing crosses. Every membrane in your body is asymmetric, and knows which way round it is.

Two experiments made fluidity a measurement instead of an impression. In 1970 Larry Frye and Michael Edidin fused a mouse cell to a human cell using Sendai virus, and labelled each parent's surface proteins with a differently coloured fluorescent antibody — Section 3.1's immunofluorescence, put to a question it was made for. At the moment of fusion the hybrid cell was half red and half green. Within forty minutes the two labels were completely intermingled over more than nine cells in ten. Nothing had transported them; they had simply drifted. The second experiment is the modern instrument: bleach a small spot on a fluorescently labelled membrane with a strong laser pulse, and watch the spot light up again as unbleached molecules wander in from around it. The rate of recovery gives a diffusion coefficient directly, and the fraction that never recovers gives the proportion of molecules that are not free at all. Figure 4.2 has both, and the bleached spot is the one place in this book where a number falls out of an experiment the reader performs.

What decides how fluid a membrane is? Three things, and a cell adjusts all of them. Temperature is the obvious one: cool a membrane far enough and it sets, the tails packing into an ordered array and the whole sheet becoming a gel in which nothing moves. The temperature at which that happens depends on the tails. Long tails pack better than short ones and set sooner. Saturated tails, which are straight, pack best of all; an unsaturated tail has a kink at each double bond — Section 2.6's reason for a fat being liquid rather than solid — and kinks keep the neighbours apart, so a membrane rich in unsaturated tails stays fluid at lower temperatures. Organisms use this. A bacterium moved to a colder incubator changes the lipids it makes within an hour; a plant hardening for winter does the same; and the fat in a reindeer's leg is more unsaturated near the hoof, which is many degrees colder than the body above it.

Cholesterol is the interesting one, because what it does depends on where you start. At body temperature its rigid ring system gets in the way of the tails and restrains their movement, making the membrane a little stiffer and markedly less leaky. At low temperature it wedges between the tails and stops them packing into the ordered array, so the membrane cannot set. It is not a fluidiser or a stiffener but a buffer against change, and an animal plasma membrane may carry as much as one cholesterol molecule for every phospholipid. Plants and bacteria have no cholesterol; plants use other sterols to the same end, and Section 3.7 mentioned that a fungus uses one just different enough from ours to be a drug target.

Now the mosaic. Protein is about half the mass of a typical plasma membrane, and far more in a membrane whose job is chemistry: three-quarters of the inner mitochondrial membrane of Section 3.5 is protein, which is what all that cristae area is for. There are two ways to belong to a membrane. An integral protein is embedded in the bilayer, usually all the way through, and can be got out only by dissolving the membrane with detergent. A peripheral protein sits on one face, held by weaker attachments to the lipid heads or to an integral protein, and comes off in salt solution.

How a protein crosses is the sharpest possible illustration of Section 2.7. The part of the chain that lies in the oily core is typically a single α-⁠helix about twenty amino acids long, and every side chain on it is hydrophobic; the parts that stick out on either side are the ordinary mixture, with the charged and polar residues facing water. The bilayer does not select these proteins. It admits the stretch it cannot exclude, and the same arithmetic that folds a soluble protein with its greasy residues buried inside builds a membrane protein with its greasy residues turned outwards. A run of twenty hydrophobic residues in a gene's sequence is so reliable a sign of a membrane-spanning segment that it can be read straight off the sequence, and it is the same signature Section 3.4's signal peptide used to get into the endoplasmic reticulum in the first place.

Those proteins do most of what a membrane does. They are the transporters of Sections 4.5 to 4.7; they are enzymes, anchored where their substrate is; they are receptors, with a binding site outside and a business end inside, which is how a cell hears anything at all; and they are anchors, tying the membrane to the cytoskeleton of Section 3.6 and to the extracellular matrix of Section 3.7. That last job explains the awkward part of the Frye–Edidin result: not everything drifts. Bleach a spot and some proteins never come back, because they are tethered from beneath or penned into a corral by the mesh of filaments under the membrane. The mosaic is fluid, but parts of it are pinned.

Finally, the sugars, and the payoff for the asymmetry this section began with. Many membrane proteins and some lipids carry short branched carbohydrate chains, making a glycoprotein or a glycolipid, and together these form a fuzzy sugar coat on the cell, the glycocalyx. It is a recognition surface: the difference between blood groups A, B and O is a difference in one sugar on the end of a chain on the red cell surface, and the reason a mismatched transfusion is dangerous is that the immune system reads the sugars. The chains stand only on the outer face, never inside the cell — and Section 3.4 has already explained why. They were added in the lumen of the endoplasmic reticulum and the Golgi, the vesicle that carried them budded and then fused with the plasma membrane, and the face that was the lumen became the face that is the outside world. The cell does not have to sort its sugars to the right side. The geometry of vesicle traffic does it.

A membrane is a liquid in the plane and nearly impassable across it. Sideways movement is constant; a leaflet crossing is so rare that the two faces stay different for as long as the cell lives.

What crosses on its own

A cell that could keep nothing out would have no chemistry, and a cell that could let nothing in would have no supplies. What makes a membrane useful is that its barrier is not uniform: it stops some things absolutely and waves others through, and which is which is decided by nothing more than the chemistry of Section 2.2. This is selective permeability, and it is worth looking at the actual numbers, because the span is wider than anyone expects.

The measure is a permeability coefficient: how fast a substance crosses a given area of membrane for a given difference in concentration, in centimetres per second. The table below is for a synthetic bilayer with no protein in it at all — a membrane made of nothing but lipid, so that what is being measured is the barrier itself.

These are orders of magnitude and not precise constants; measured values differ between one lipid mixture and another. But the shape of the table is not in doubt, and the shape is the lesson. Twelve orders of magnitude separate the top row from the bottom. A bilayer is about a thousand million times more permeable to water than to a sodium ion, and a sodium ion is far smaller than a water molecule with its shell of hydrogen-bonded neighbours. Size is not what is being sorted.

Steroid hormones, which are oily, cross membranes freely, and this is why their receptors are inside the cell while the receptors for water-soluble hormones like insulin must sit on the surface. The chemistry of Section 2.6 decides where a signal is heard.

Charge is. To enter the core of a membrane, an ion has to leave behind the shell of water molecules that Section 2.3 described clustering around it, because there is no room for them in the grease and nothing there to hold them. Moving a charge from water, which is superb at stabilising it, into a hydrocarbon, which is hopeless at it, costs of the order of a hundred kilojoules per mole. Set that against the 2.6 kJ/mol of thermal energy available at body temperature, which Section 2.2 used to decide which bonds break by themselves, and the barrier is some forty times the energy there is to climb it with. An ion does not cross a bilayer. Not slowly — effectively not at all. Every ion in this chapter that gets anywhere gets there through a protein.

Water is the awkward case, and honest textbooks say so. It is polar, it hydrogen-bonds to everything, and it has no business crossing a sheet of grease; yet it outruns sodium by nine orders of magnitude, because it is uncharged, tiny, and present in enormous numbers. That is fast enough for most cells and nothing like fast enough for a kidney, and Section 4.5 says what is done about it. Glucose is the opposite awkward case: the published measurements of how fast it crosses a bare bilayer disagree by orders of magnitude, which tells you it is very slow indeed. A red blood cell lives on glucose and does not wait for it.

Whatever does cross gets across by simple diffusion, the process Section 3.2 used to explain why cells are small, now applied to a barrier instead of to open cytoplasm. It is worth being exact about what diffusion is, because it is almost always described as though it were a force, and it is not one. Nothing pushes a molecule down a gradient. Each molecule moves at random, indifferent to where the others are and to where it has been. If there are more molecules on the left than on the right, more of them will happen to wander rightwards than leftwards, purely because there are more available to wander; and so the substance spreads. There is no tendency in any individual molecule. The tendency exists only in the accounting.

Follow that through and a common misunderstanding dissolves. When a substance reaches equal concentrations on both sides, its molecules do not stop crossing. They cross exactly as often as before, and as often one way as the other, so nothing further changes. This is a dynamic equilibrium — a balance of two large opposite flows, not a truce. It also explains why this kind of transport is free. Diffusion down a concentration gradient is passive transport: the cell spends nothing, because the gradient was the cost, and it was paid somewhere else and earlier.

How fast, then? The rate at which a substance crosses a membrane rises with the difference in concentration across it, with the area available, and with temperature; and it falls as the barrier gets thicker. That is Fick's law, and every gas-exchange surface in biology is built to it. Your lungs present a surface of some seventy square metres, divided among a few hundred million alveoli, and the barrier between air and blood — one flattened cell, one basement membrane and one more flattened cell — is a few tenths of a micrometre thick. The arithmetic is Section 3.2's again, one level up: a large organism cannot rely on diffusion, so it grows an enormous thin surface and puts a pump behind it.

One complication, and the whole of Section 4.7 depends on it. For a molecule with no charge, the concentration gradient is the whole story. For an ion there are two gradients at once, because the inside of a cell is electrically negative with respect to the outside, and a charge feels that. The two can pull the same way or opposite ways, and what an ion actually does depends on the sum, which is called the electrochemical gradient. A chloride ion outside a cell is pulled in by its concentration gradient and pushed out by the negative interior; a potassium ion inside is pushed out by its concentration gradient and pulled back by the same negative interior. Figure 4.3 lets you set both and see which wins.

A bilayer sorts by charge and polarity, not by size: gases stream through, water trickles, ions are stopped by the cost of leaving their water behind. What does cross moves by diffusion, which is a statistical consequence and not a force, and which costs the cell nothing.

Water follows the solute

Chapter 3 used one word three times without ever explaining it. A bacterium needs a wall because water floods in by osmosis. A plant cell is held rigid because water enters by osmosis and the wall pushes back. A vacuole swells because the cell pumps solutes in and water follows by osmosis. Each of those is a load-bearing step in an argument, and each was borrowed against this section. Here is the mechanism.

Osmosis is the net movement of water across a selectively permeable membrane, into the side where more is dissolved. What makes it confusing is that we describe it by the solute, which is the thing that is not moving. Put pure water on one side of a membrane and a sugar solution on the other, with a membrane that passes water and stops sugar, and water crosses into the sugar. It is not attracted. Nor is the reason the one many textbooks give, that the pure side holds more water per litre: some salts pack water round their ions so tightly that their solutions hold more water per litre than pure water does, and water crosses into them all the same. And it is not that the sugar holds water in shells, as Section 2.3 described, and takes it out of circulation, because what counts is how many particles are dissolved and not how much water each one holds.

Count particles, not molecules. A mole of glucose gives a mole of particles; a mole of sodium chloride dissociates and gives nearly two. That is why the concentration that matters for osmosis is measured in osmoles rather than moles, and why a salt solution counts for about twice as much as a sugar solution of the same molarity.

The reason is the one that built the membrane in Section 4.1: arrangements. Sugar and water mixed can be arranged in far more ways than the same sugar and water kept apart, and every water molecule that crosses into the solution dilutes it and adds to that number. So more water crosses in than comes back, and the traffic stays unequal until something stops it. Chapter 5 gives this kind of counting its name, entropy.

What stops it is pressure, and this is where the idea becomes quantitative and stops being a picture of arrows. If water enters a closed space it raises the pressure there, and a raised pressure pushes water back out. Physiologists and plant scientists therefore track a single quantity that combines both effects, the water potential, written Ψ and measured in megapascals. It is the sum of two terms: the solute potential Ψs, which says how much the dissolved substances have lowered water's tendency to leave, and the pressure potential Ψp, which says what the pressure is doing. Pure water at atmospheric pressure is defined as zero. Dissolving anything in it makes Ψs negative; squeezing it makes Ψp positive.

The whole of osmosis is then one sentence: water moves from higher water potential to lower. Always, without exception, and with no need to remember which way is "towards the salt". A 0.1 mol/L solution of sucrose at 25 °C has a solute potential of about −0.25 MPa, from the product of the concentration, the gas constant and the absolute temperature; the same concentration of sodium chloride comes to about −0.5 MPa, because each formula unit gives two particles. Put the two either side of a membrane at the same pressure and water goes from the sucrose to the salt, because −0.25 is higher than −0.5.

The pressure needed to stop osmosis altogether is the solution's osmotic pressure, and it is larger than intuition suggests. Blood plasma is about 290 milliosmoles per litre, which at body temperature comes to roughly 0.75 MPa — about seven and a half atmospheres, which is the pressure some seventy metres under the sea. No cell membrane could contain that. It never has to, because the fluid outside your cells is held at the same value; the whole business of body fluid regulation is the business of never presenting a cell with a difference it cannot survive. Figure 4.4 has an osmometer with a movable piston, so the pressure that stops the flow can be found by hand.

Now put a cell in it. A solution is called hypotonic to a cell if it makes the cell gain water, hypertonic if it makes the cell lose water, and isotonic if it does neither. Notice how those are defined: tonicity is not a property of a solution but of a solution with respect to a particular cell, and it depends on which solutes that cell's membrane can stop. A solution of urea at the same osmolarity as the cytoplasm is iso-osmotic and still hypotonic, because urea crosses the membrane, equalises, and leaves the cell's own solutes doing the pulling. Cells put in it swell and burst. That distinction is not pedantry; it is the difference between a safe infusion and a lethal one.

An animal cell has no wall, so it has no defence but its volume. In a hypotonic solution it takes in water, rounds up, and beyond a few per cent of stretch the membrane gives way: a red cell in pure water bursts, which is haemolysis, and the released haemoglobin turns the water red. In a hypertonic solution it loses water and shrinks, its surface puckering into spikes — crenation. Only the isotonic case leaves it alone, which is why the saline in a drip is a 0.9 per cent solution of sodium chloride, about 154 mmol/L, and not water: counting both ions, that comes to roughly 300 milliosmoles per litre, near enough to the 290 of blood.

A walled cell behaves entirely differently, and the difference is one term in the equation. Put a plant cell in a hypotonic solution and water enters as before, but the cell cannot expand: the cellulose wall of Section 3.7 is stiff, so the volume barely changes and the pressure rises instead. That rising pressure is Ψp, and it pushes water potential back up. Water keeps arriving until the inside potential has climbed to match the outside, and then stops — with the cell full, the wall taut, and a pressure of several hundred kilopascals inside. This is turgor, and Section 3.7 was right that it is what holds up a plant that has no wood in it. Typical values run from about 0.6 to 0.8 MPa — several times the pressure in a car tyre, which is what Section 3.7 claimed — and they are reached and held with no machinery whatsoever.

The other two cases follow. In an isotonic solution no water enters, the pressure potential falls to zero, the wall goes slack and the cell is flaccid — a plant surrounded by a solution as concentrated as its own cells wilts, which surprises people who expect isotonic to be the comfortable case. In a hypertonic solution water leaves, the whole living contents shrink away from the wall, and the cell is plasmolysed. The wall keeps its shape throughout, which is why a dead plant cell still has an outline and why timber is stiff. Figure 4.4's plant mode is Figure 3.8's turgor slider seen from the other end: there you watched the cell wilt, here you can read why in megapascals.

A freshwater protist is permanently in the worst case: its cytoplasm is far more concentrated than the pond, so water arrives without stopping. The paramecia in Figure 1.1 solve it by bailing. A contractile vacuole fills with the surplus and periodically contracts to squirt it back out, and it costs ATP to run — one of the more expensive items in a protist's budget.

So: three ways of living with osmosis, and every organism uses one. Build a wall and let the pressure rise until it stops the water — plants, fungi, and the bacteria of Section 3.3, whose several atmospheres of internal pressure the peptidoglycan mesh contains. Bail continuously, as a freshwater protist does with its contractile vacuole. Or hold the outside fluid at the same concentration as the inside, which is what an animal does, and it is the reason Chapter 1's homeostasis has a whole organ devoted to it. A kidney's job is very largely the management of Ψ.

Water moves from higher water potential to lower, and water potential is the solute term plus the pressure term. A cell without a wall must keep the outside isotonic or change volume; a walled cell converts the arriving water into pressure instead, and stops taking it when the pressure has climbed enough.

Doors, and why a door saturates

Section 4.3 left a cell in an awkward position. It cannot admit an ion at all, its water supply crosses too slowly for a kidney, and glucose — the molecule most of its chemistry runs on — is one of the slowest things in the table. Every one of those problems is solved the same way, by a protein in the membrane that provides a route the lipid does not. When the route runs downhill, from high concentration to low, the process is facilitated diffusion.

The word to hold on to is diffusion. Facilitated diffusion is passive: it is driven by the gradient and by nothing else, it costs the cell no energy, it stops at equilibrium, and it will run either way depending on which side has more. The protein does not push. It opens a route past the grease, and everything after that is Section 4.3's statistics. A great many readers come away believing that transport through a protein must be active because a protein sounds like machinery, and it is worth saying flatly that it is not: what makes transport active is a source of energy, not a piece of protein, and that is Section 4.6.

There are two designs, and they are as different from each other as a doorway is from a revolving door.

A channel protein is a hole. Its wall is lined with polar or charged groups, so the passage through the middle is hydrophilic and an ion can cross without ever meeting the oily core. Nothing binds, nothing changes shape while the ion is passing, and so a channel is fast: up to a hundred million ions a second, which is close to the rate at which ions can arrive at the mouth by diffusion. Most channels are not open all the time. A gated channel opens in response to something, and there are three kinds of something. Voltage-gated channels respond to a change in the membrane potential, and are how a nerve impulse propagates. Ligand-gated channels open when a particular molecule binds, which is what happens at a synapse when a nerve releases its transmitter onto the next cell. Mechanically gated channels open when the membrane is deformed, which is how the hair cells of your inner ear turn a vibration into a signal.

Roderick MacKinnon solved the structure of a potassium channel and showed the filter directly; Peter Agre found the water channels. They shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2003, which is unusually prompt recognition for structures published only a few years before.

Fast is easy; choosy is hard, and the way a channel is choosy is the most elegant thing in this chapter. A potassium channel conducts K+ about ten thousand times better than Na+, and a sodium ion is the smaller of the two — 0.095 nm against 0.133 nm — so a simple sieve would do the opposite. The answer is that neither ion travels bare. Each is wrapped in water, and to enter the narrow part of the channel it must strip that water off. The filter of a potassium channel is lined with carbonyl oxygens held in a rigid frame at exactly the spacing the water molecules around a potassium ion would have occupied. A potassium ion sheds its shell and finds the oxygens waiting in precisely the right places, so it loses nothing by the exchange. A sodium ion is too small for those same fixed oxygens to reach, so it would have to give up its water and get nothing back. It is not blocked. It is priced out. The barrier of Section 4.3 has been turned into a selection device by paying part of it back.

A carrier protein works quite differently. It has a binding site, not a hole, and the site is exposed to one side of the membrane at a time. A molecule binds on the outside; the protein changes shape; the site is now open to the inside; the molecule leaves; the protein relaxes. There is never a continuous passage through it, which is how a carrier can be specific and how it can later be made to work uphill. The price is speed. The fastest carrier moves of the order of a thousand molecules a second, which is a hundred thousand times slower than a channel, because every passenger requires a full cycle of shape change. The glucose carrier of a red blood cell, GLUT1, is the textbook case and the one Figure 4.5 uses.

Out of that mechanism falls a signature no amount of arguing can fake, and it is the practical way to tell the two apart. Raise the concentration outside and watch the rate. Simple diffusion rises in a straight line for ever: double the difference, double the flux, with no ceiling, because nothing is being occupied. Facilitated diffusion rises, curves over, and flattens. The flattening is saturation: there is a fixed number of carriers, each takes a fixed time per passenger, and once nearly all of them are busy at all times the membrane is delivering as fast as it can. The ceiling is set by how many carriers the cell has installed, not by how much glucose is outside, which means a cell controls its uptake by controlling the number of doors. The curve is exactly the shape of an enzyme's, and for exactly the same reason: a finite number of binding sites, each with a cycle to complete.

Figure 4.5 puts the two curves on one axis, and the number worth reading off is where the carrier's curve is half way up. For GLUT1 that is at about 1 to 2 mmol/L of glucose, while blood is held near 5 mmol/L. The carrier is therefore working close to its ceiling nearly all the time, so the rate at which a red cell takes up glucose barely depends on how much glucose is in the blood — which is what you want from a cell that must keep working whether its owner has eaten or not. Other members of the same family are tuned differently: the carrier in a liver cell is half-saturated well above blood concentration, so it tracks the blood instead of ignoring it, and the liver can therefore act on how much sugar has arrived.

Which brings us back to water. A bare bilayer passes water at a respectable rate for a polar molecule and nothing like fast enough for a kidney, which filters something like 180 litres a day and must reabsorb almost all of it. The solution is a channel, the aquaporin, and it is an extraordinary one: a pore so narrow that water goes through in single file, at about three thousand million molecules a second, while refusing everything else — including, remarkably, a proton, which would otherwise hop along the file of hydrogen-bonded water molecules as though down a wire. The channel prevents that by twisting one water molecule around at its narrowest point, breaking the chain. Aquaporins are where they are needed and absent where they are not: thick in a red cell and in the kidney tubule, sparse elsewhere. The kidney's final concentration of urine is achieved by putting aquaporins into a membrane and taking them out again under hormonal control, which is to say that your body regulates its water balance by regulating the number of doors.

A protein does not make transport active; it makes it fast and selective. A channel is a hole and passes millions of ions a second; a carrier binds, changes shape and cycles, so it saturates — and saturation is the observable that gives a transporter away.

Pumping uphill

Everything so far runs downhill. A cell that could only let things run downhill would end up at equilibrium with its surroundings, which is a precise description of being dead. Chapter 1 put it in one line: order costs energy, and a living thing is a thing that keeps paying. Active transport is where a cell pays — moving a solute against its electrochemical gradient, which cannot happen by itself and therefore must be coupled to something that releases energy.

In primary active transport the energy comes straight from a chemical reaction, and almost always from the same one: the hydrolysis of ATP. Chapter 5 is about where ATP comes from and why its hydrolysis releases what it does; for this chapter it is enough that a cell keeps a supply of it, and that a protein which can hydrolyse a molecule of ATP has energy at its disposal at the moment and place it is needed. A transporter that does this is a pump.

The pump to know is the sodium–potassium pump, which sits in the plasma membrane of every animal cell you have. Per molecule of ATP it moves three sodium ions out of the cell and two potassium ions in, both against their gradients, and it does it by the carrier trick of Section 4.5 with one addition: the phosphate from the ATP is attached to the protein itself, and it is the attachment and removal of that phosphate that switches the protein between its two shapes. The cycle runs in six steps, and Figure 4.6 steps through them.

Three sodium ions bind to sites facing the cytosol, which fit sodium and not potassium. Their binding lets the protein take the phosphate from ATP. Phosphorylated, the protein flips to its other shape, in which the sites now face outside and, crucially, no longer fit sodium — so the three ions are released into the extracellular fluid whether they like it or not. In that shape a different pair of sites fits potassium, and two potassium ions from outside bind. Their binding triggers the removal of the phosphate. Unphosphorylated, the protein relaxes to its first shape, the sites turn inward and stop fitting potassium, and the two ions are released into the cytosol. The protein is back where it started and one molecule of ATP is gone.

This pump and its relatives, the calcium pump and the stomach's proton pump among them, work this way: two shapes, with the binding site facing opposite ways and having different affinities in each, and a chemical reaction that decides which shape the protein is in. Nothing is dragged through anything. The cargo is picked up where it is plentiful and put down where it is scarce, by a protein that changes its mind about what it wants to hold.

The point of the pump is not the ions it is holding at any moment but the gradients it maintains. In a typical animal cell, sodium stands at about 145 mmol/L outside and 12 mmol/L inside; potassium at about 140 mmol/L inside and 4 mmol/L outside. That is a twelvefold difference for sodium and a thirty-fivefold one for potassium, running in opposite directions, and both are held indefinitely against a membrane that leaks. The running cost is startling: the sodium–potassium pump consumes about a third of the ATP a resting animal cell makes, and in a nerve cell, which spends its working life letting these gradients collapse a little and restoring them, the figure approaches two-thirds. Something like a fifth of your resting metabolism goes on bailing out sodium. Whatever these gradients are for, they are worth that, and Section 4.7 is the answer.

There is a second reason the pump exists, and it is Section 4.4's business. A cell is full of proteins, nucleic acids and metabolites that cannot cross the membrane and that are mostly negatively charged, and those solutes exert an osmotic pull that never goes away. Left alone, an animal cell would draw in water until it burst. What the pump does is keep sodium out — continuously, at a cost — so that sodium behaves as if it were an impermeant solute on the outside, balancing the impermeant solutes inside. A cell whose pump is stopped by ouabain, or by cooling, or by running out of ATP, does not merely lose its gradients: it swells, and then it lyses. Figure 4.6 shows this, and it is the sharpest statement of the difference between a walled cell and a naked one. A plant's volume is contained passively by a wall. An animal's is defended actively, and it stops being defended within minutes of the energy supply failing.

Ouabain has a relative in the medicine cabinet. Digoxin, from the foxglove, partly blocks the same pump in heart muscle; sodium accumulates a little inside the cell; and, as the next section explains, that reduces the driving force on another transporter which normally throws calcium out in exchange for sodium coming in. More calcium stays inside, and the heart beats harder. A drug that works by poisoning the most important pump in the body has to be dosed with care, and digoxin's margin between too little and too much is one of the narrowest in medicine.

Other pumps are built to the same plan for other cargo. A calcium pump in the membrane of the endoplasmic reticulum — the smooth ER whose calcium store Section 3.4 mentioned — keeps the calcium concentration in the cytosol some ten thousand times lower than outside the cell, which is what makes a sudden calcium release usable as a signal: a signal is only worth anything against a quiet background, and the pump is what keeps the background quiet.

Proton pumps deserve their own paragraph, because Chapter 7 will be built on one. A proton pump moves hydrogen ions across a membrane, which by Section 2.4's definition is the same as moving pH. The pump in a lysosome's membrane is what holds its interior near pH 5, the acidity that Section 3.4 said its enzymes are built for; stop the pump and the enzymes go quiet. A plant or fungal cell has no sodium–potassium pump at all and uses a proton pump in its plasma membrane as its master pump instead, driving everything else from the proton gradient it creates. And the cells lining your stomach run the steepest ion gradient in the body: a pump that exchanges protons for potassium, building a lumen at about pH 1 from a cytosol at pH 7. Six pH units is a millionfold difference in hydrogen ion concentration, maintained across a membrane 7 nm thick, by a protein. The drugs that treat reflux are named after what they do to it — proton pump inhibitors.

The sodium–potassium pump is a carrier whose two shapes are chosen by a chemical reaction, so the cargo is picked up where it is plentiful and released where it is scarce. It takes about a third of a resting cell's ATP, holds a twelvefold sodium gradient and a thirty-fivefold potassium one in opposite directions, and is what keeps an animal cell from swelling.

A gradient is a battery

A third of a cell's energy is a great deal to spend on tidying ions, and no cell does it for tidiness. A gradient across a membrane is stored energy in the most literal sense: work was done to make it, and that work can be got back by letting it run down. The pump charges; everything in this section spends. Once you have seen a cell this way, a surprising amount of physiology turns out to be one machine charging a battery and several others draining it.

Start with what the gradients have already produced, which is a voltage. The membrane of a resting animal cell carries a membrane potential: the inside is electrically negative with respect to the outside, by something between 20 and 200 mV depending on the cell, and by about 70 mV in a nerve cell at rest. It is easy to assume the pump makes it, since the pump moves three positive charges out for every two it brings in. It does contribute — a pump that moves net charge is called electrogenic — but the contribution is only a few millivolts. Stop the pump and the potential is still there many minutes later, decaying only as slowly as the gradients themselves decay. Something else is making it.

That something is potassium leaving. A resting cell's membrane holds potassium leak channels that are open all the time, and potassium is some thirty-five times more concentrated inside than out, so it leaks out. Each ion that leaves takes a positive charge with it and leaves an unmatched negative charge behind, so the inside becomes negative — and a negative inside pulls potassium back. The leak does not run to completion. It stops, in effect, at the voltage at which the electrical pull exactly balances the concentration push, and the balance point can be calculated. At body temperature the voltage that balances a tenfold concentration difference for a singly charged ion is 61.5 mV, and the balance point for any ion is that figure times the logarithm of the ratio across the membrane. For potassium at 140 mmol/L inside and 4 outside that is about −95 mV. A real nerve cell rests nearer −70 mV, because a small sodium leak runs the other way and drags the figure up.

The prediction is testable with a bucket of solutions. Raise the potassium outside tenfold and the resting potential should become about 61.5 mV less negative. It does. That is also why a high blood potassium is a medical emergency: it depolarises every excitable cell at once, and it can stop a heart.

So the potential is not the pump's direct work but the potassium gradient's, spent through a channel. This is the pattern for the rest of the section. Figure 4.7 makes it a measurement: there is a voltmeter on the cell, and the reader can change the external potassium, block the leak channels, or stop the pump, and see which one moves the reading immediately and which one moves it over minutes.

How much energy is in a gradient? Take sodium, and do the arithmetic, because it is worth checking rather than believing. A mole of sodium ions falling from 145 mmol/L outside to 12 mmol/L inside releases RT ln(145/12), which at body temperature is about 6.4 kJ; and the same mole of positive charge falling through 70 mV of potential difference releases another 6.8 kJ. Together, some 13 kJ per mole of sodium admitted. Now compare the currency: hydrolysing a mole of ATP inside a living cell releases about 50 kJ — not the 30.5 kJ of the standard table, for reasons Chapter 5 gives. So one ATP is worth about four sodium ions coming in, and the pump moves three out per ATP. It is working with very little margin, which is not a design fault but a sign of an efficient machine; and if the gradients are made steep enough in the laboratory the pump runs backwards and synthesises ATP. Nothing demonstrates that a gradient is stored energy more plainly than a pump that can be made to run as a generator.

Now spend it. In secondary active transport a carrier binds two things at once: an ion that is about to fall down its electrochemical gradient, and a cargo molecule that is about to be carried up its own. Neither can move without the other, so the ion's descent pays for the cargo's climb. No ATP goes anywhere near this transporter. It is still active transport, because the cargo ends up where diffusion would never have put it, and because the gradient it spent was bought with ATP earlier, by the pump. That is what "secondary" means: not a lesser form of active transport, but active transport at one remove from the ATP.

The two arrangements have names. In symport the ion and the cargo cross in the same direction; in antiport they cross in opposite directions, the ion coming in as the cargo goes out. Animal cells mostly use the sodium gradient for this, which is what a third of their ATP is really buying; plants, fungi and bacteria mostly use a proton gradient, for the same reason and by the same logic.

Figure 4.7 works a case worth following all the way through, because it is where four sections meet. A cell lining your small intestine has to take glucose out of the gut and put it into the blood, and after a meal the gut is sugary, so simple facilitated diffusion would do. Hours later the gut is nearly empty and the blood is not, and diffusion would run backwards. The cell solves this with three different transporters on two different faces. On the face towards the gut sits a sodium–glucose symporter, which admits two sodium ions for every glucose molecule and can therefore drag glucose in even when the lumen holds a few hundredths of what the cell already contains. On the face towards the blood sit the sodium–potassium pump, throwing the sodium straight back out to keep the gradient the symporter is spending, and a plain glucose carrier, through which glucose leaves downhill into the blood. Three transport mechanisms, one direction of travel, and the whole arrangement only works because the cell's two faces carry different proteins — which they can only do because the tight junctions of Section 3.7 seal the sheet and stop the proteins wandering round the sides.

That machinery is the basis of one of the cheapest life-saving treatments there is. The cholera toxin makes the cells of the gut pour out chloride, and water follows by osmosis, and a person can lose several litres a day. What the toxin does not touch is the sodium–glucose symporter. So a drink of salt and sugar in the right proportions is absorbed when salt alone is not, and the water follows the absorbed solute back in. Oral rehydration therapy costs almost nothing, needs no needle, and is a direct application of the fact that the sugar rides in on the sodium.

The heart's version closes an earlier story. Cardiac muscle cells throw calcium out with a sodium–calcium antiporter that admits three sodium ions for each calcium ion it expels, so the sodium gradient again pays. Digoxin, from Section 4.6, partly blocks the sodium–potassium pump; sodium inside rises a little; the antiporter's driving force falls; less calcium is expelled between beats; and the cell contracts more strongly. A drug aimed at one transporter takes effect through a second one that never touched it, which is what it means to say a cell runs on gradients rather than on wires.

One last debt, from two chapters back. Section 3.3 said that the bacterial flagellum is turned by a rotary motor driven not by ATP but by protons flowing back into the cell, and left it there. This is what that sentence meant: a bacterium's pumps push protons out, the gradient is the store, and the motor is a machine that lets them fall back in and takes the work. Chapter 7 will show your own mitochondria doing the same thing in reverse — a proton gradient, charged by the electron transport chain across the cristae of Section 3.5, falling back through a pump that has been turned into a generator, making ATP as it goes. The membrane is where a cell keeps its energy. Not in a molecule, but across a film 7 nm thick.

A gradient is stored energy. The pump spends ATP to charge it; channels, symporters and antiporters spend the gradient. The membrane potential is mostly potassium leaking out, not the pump pushing, and the sodium gradient an animal cell maintains is the small change it pays for everything else.

Too big to cross

No protein in the last three sections would be any use for swallowing a bacterium. Channels and carriers handle molecules; a bacterium is a thousand times wider than the mouth of any channel, and a cholesterol-carrying particle is far too big as well, and so is a mouthful of the fluid outside the cell. For cargo on that scale the membrane does not pass the cargo through. It wraps it, and moves the wrapping.

Endocytosis is the inward version: a patch of plasma membrane buckles, deepens, closes behind the cargo and pinches off as a vesicle inside the cell. Chapter 3 met it as the entrance to the endomembrane system; here it matters as a transport mechanism, and it comes in three kinds that differ in what decides the cargo.

Phagocytosis, cell eating, is the dramatic one. A cell extends arms of membrane around a large particle — pushed out by the actin filaments of Section 3.6, which is the same mechanism as a crawling cell's leading edge — until the arms meet and fuse, enclosing the particle in a vesicle that then fuses with a lysosome and is digested, by exactly the route Section 3.4 described. For an amoeba this is dinner. In your body it is the trade of a few specialised cells: a macrophage engulfing a bacterium is doing what an amoeba does, with the same machinery, for a different reason.

Pinocytosis, cell drinking, is the unglamorous one and the busiest. Small vesicles form continuously all over the surface, taking in droplets of extracellular fluid with whatever happens to be dissolved in them. Nothing is selected. The rate is remarkable: a macrophage internalises the equivalent of its entire plasma membrane in about half an hour, which raises a question the end of this section answers.

Clathrin is a three-legged protein that assembles into a curved lattice, the way the panels of a football do. Nothing measures the vesicle or decides its size: the geometry of the lattice does, which is why coated vesicles come out so uniform.

The third kind is the selective one, and the most important. In receptor-mediated endocytosis, receptor proteins in the membrane bind one particular molecule from outside and then gather together, dragging their bound cargo with them, into a shallow dimple lined on the cytoplasmic side by a basketwork of the protein clathrin. The basket curves the membrane, the dimple becomes a pit, the pit pinches off. Because the cargo was concentrated by the receptors before the vesicle formed, this route brings in a chosen molecule hundreds of times faster than drinking the fluid would.

The case that established all this is cholesterol. Cholesterol is hydrophobic — Section 2.3 again — so it cannot travel in blood by itself; it travels packaged with protein as a low-density lipoprotein particle, an LDL. A cell that needs cholesterol puts LDL receptors in its membrane, LDL particles bind, coated pits take them in, and a lysosome breaks the particle open and releases the cholesterol for use. Michael Brown and Joseph Goldstein worked this out from patients who could not do it: in familial hypercholesterolaemia the receptor is faulty, LDL is not cleared, cholesterol accumulates in the blood, and arteries fur up decades early. About one person in 250 carries one faulty copy of the gene, and the very rare people with two have heart attacks in childhood. Brown and Goldstein shared the 1985 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for it — and the mechanism explains why the statins work, which is not by scrubbing cholesterol out of the blood but by lowering how much a liver cell makes, so that the cell puts out more LDL receptors and takes more out of the blood itself.

Exocytosis runs the other way, and Chapter 3 followed a protein all the way to it: a vesicle from the Golgi meets the plasma membrane, the two bilayers fuse, and the contents are outside. Both bilayers have to be deformed and joined, which does not happen by itself; a set of proteins holds the two membranes together and forces the fusion, and in a nerve terminal the whole event is triggered by calcium and completed in well under a millisecond.

Which brings us to the arithmetic, and to the reason this section belongs in a chapter about transport rather than in Chapter 3. Every vesicle that fuses with the plasma membrane adds its area to the cell's surface. Every vesicle that buds inward takes area away. A cell secreting hard would inflate; a macrophage drinking at its observed rate would vanish into itself within the hour. Neither happens, because the two flows are matched: membrane is not consumed but circulated, out to the surface with the secretory traffic and back in with the endocytic traffic, and a synaptic terminal recycles the membrane of a vesicle it has just released within a minute or so. The surface of a cell is not a fixed sheet of material. It is a steady state, like everything else alive.

One detail confirms the whole picture of Section 4.2. When a vesicle fuses, the face that lined its interior becomes the face that meets the outside world, and the face that met the cytosol goes on meeting the cytosol. The sugar chains added in the lumen of the endoplasmic reticulum and the Golgi therefore end up on the outside of the cell and can end up nowhere else, and the asymmetry established in Section 4.2 is not maintained by any sorting machinery at all. It is a consequence of the geometry of budding and fusing, and it has been preserved unbroken since the first cell that had an inside. Figure 4.8 lets you follow a labelled patch of membrane out and back and see it stay the same way round.

Each observation is best explained by one kind of transport. Sort them. Drag a card onto a bin, or use its buttons.

  • Simple diffusion
  • Facilitated diffusion
  • Active transport
  • Bulk transport
  • Carbon dioxide leaves a muscle cell faster and faster as the concentration inside it rises, with no sign of a ceiling.
  • Glucose enters a red blood cell faster as the outside concentration rises, but above a few millimolar the rate barely climbs further.
  • A root cell holds potassium at a hundred times the concentration of the soil water around it, and stops doing so when the root is chilled.
  • A white blood cell surrounds a bacterium with arms of its own membrane and encloses it in a vesicle.
  • Adding a hormone puts water channels into the membrane of a kidney tubule cell, and water crosses far faster in the same direction it was already going.
  • A protein in the plasma membrane hydrolyses ATP and uses the phosphate to switch between two shapes, expelling sodium and admitting potassium.
  • A steroid hormone reaches its receptor in the cytoplasm within seconds, although the cell has no transporter for it.
  • Cholesterol-carrying particles bind receptors that cluster into a coated dimple, and the loaded dimple is taken into the cell whole.

Look back along the chapter from here. A film of lipid that assembles itself, that almost nothing crosses unaided, that has proteins in it which let chosen things through, and pumps which spend energy to push things the wrong way, and a whole traffic of vesicles for what will not fit — and out of those four things come a cell's volume, its voltage, its supply of sugar, the pressure in a leaf and the pressure in a bacterium, the signal that fires a nerve and the drink that treats cholera. All of it turns on the fact that water will not have a hydrocarbon tail.

What the chapter has not explained is the ATP. It has been spent on every page — by the pumps, by the flippases, by the contractile vacuole bailing out a protist — and where it comes from has been put off to Chapter 5 twice already. That is where we go next: what energy is in a chemical sense, why some reactions run by themselves and others have to be paid for, how a cell couples the two, and what the molecule actually is that has been paying for everything here.

Cargo too large for any transporter is moved by wrapping it in membrane. Endocytosis and exocytosis must balance, because every vesicle carries surface area with it — and because a vesicle's inner face becomes the cell's outer face, the sugars end up outside without anything having to put them there.

Key ideas

  • A phospholipid is amphipathic, and water excluding its tails drives a crowd of them into a bilayer. Nothing assembles a membrane; the arrangement is what the hydrophobic effect leaves behind.
  • A bilayer cannot tolerate an exposed edge, so it closes into a bag and heals its own punctures. A membrane is about 7 nm thick, with three or four nanometres of oily core in the middle.
  • The fluid mosaic: proteins float in a two-dimensional lipid fluid. Lipids swap sideways some ten million times a second and cross to the other leaflet less than once a month, so the two faces stay different for the life of the cell.
  • Fluidity is a measurement, not an impression — from the Frye–Edidin fusion experiment and from bleaching a spot and timing its recovery. Unsaturated tails are how a cell keeps a membrane fluid in the cold; cholesterol buffers it against change in either direction.
  • A bilayer sorts by charge and polarity, not size. Gases cross freely, water slowly, and ions essentially not at all, because an ion would have to abandon the water gripping it to enter the oily core.
  • Diffusion is a statistical consequence rather than a force, it does not stop at equilibrium, and it costs the cell nothing. Its rate follows the concentration difference, the area and the thinness of the barrier.
  • Osmosis is the net movement of water across a selectively permeable membrane, and nothing attracts the water: water mixed with a solute can be arranged in far more ways than water and solute kept apart. Water moves from higher water potential to lower, and water potential is the solute term plus the pressure term.
  • An animal cell in a hypotonic solution swells and may burst; a walled cell instead builds a back-pressure, which is turgor, and stops taking water when the potentials match. In a hypertonic solution the first crenates and the second plasmolyses.
  • Facilitated diffusion is still passive. A channel is a hole and passes up to a hundred million ions a second; a carrier binds and changes shape, so it is far slower and it saturates, which is the observation that gives a transport protein away.
  • Active transport moves a solute against its electrochemical gradient and therefore has to be paid for. The sodium–potassium pump moves three sodium out and two potassium in per ATP, takes about a third of a resting animal cell's energy, and is what stops the cell swelling.
  • A gradient is stored energy. The resting membrane potential is mostly potassium leaking out down its gradient; secondary active transport lets sodium fall back in and uses the fall to drag another solute uphill.
  • Cargo too large for a transporter is wrapped in membrane: phagocytosis, pinocytosis and receptor-mediated endocytosis inward, exocytosis outward, with the two flows balanced so the cell's surface area stays put.

Glossary

Check your understanding

A sheet of phospholipid bilayer is torn, leaving a straight edge. Within moments the sheet has closed into a hollow sphere. Why?

  • The phospholipids are attracted to one another, and once the sheet is torn that attraction pulls it round into a ball.
  • At the edge the hydrocarbon tails are exposed to water, and closing up is the only way to hide them.
  • The charged heads repel one another, and the sheet curls away from itself until its two edges meet.
  • Proteins in the membrane sense the tear and draw its edges together, using ATP to do it.

A bilayer exists because water excludes the tails. An edge is the one place in a sheet where tails are still exposed, so it is the one place the arrangement has not finished solving — and a sphere is a sheet with no edge at all. Nothing is attracted to anything, and nothing is spent: this is the same process that formed the sheet in the first place, going on to completion.

A sodium ion is much smaller than a glucose molecule, and glucose is much smaller than a protein. Yet a bare bilayer is more permeable to glucose than to sodium. What is the best explanation?

  • Sodium ions are repelled by the negative charges on the phospholipid heads, so they never get close enough to the core to enter it.
  • Its charge: to enter the oily core it would have to abandon its shell of water, which costs far more energy than body temperature supplies.
  • Glucose is nonpolar, so it dissolves straight into the oily core of the membrane and out the other side.
  • The membrane has glucose carriers, so glucose crosses faster whatever the bilayer itself would allow.

What a bilayer sorts on is charge and polarity, not size. Moving a charge out of water — which stabilises it superbly — into hydrocarbon, which does not, costs of the order of a hundred kilojoules per mole, against the 2.6 kJ/mol of thermal energy available at 37 °C. Glucose is polar and crosses very slowly, but it is uncharged and has no shell to shed. Glucose carriers are real, but the question is about a bare bilayer.

A plant cell and a red blood cell are both put into pure water. What happens to each?

  • Both burst, because water enters both by osmosis, and a cell taking in water without limit bursts whatever it is.
  • The red cell swells and bursts; the plant cell takes in water until its wall's back-pressure stops it.
  • Neither changes, because pure water has no solutes in it, and without a solute there is nothing to drive osmosis.
  • The plant cell bursts, since its wall will not let it stretch, and the red cell shrinks as its contents leak away.

Water enters both, for the same reason: the inside has a lower water potential. The difference is what the arriving water does. A red cell has nothing to resist it, so the volume rises until the membrane fails. A plant cell's wall barely stretches, so the pressure rises instead; the pressure potential climbs, the cell's total water potential climbs with it, and the inflow stops when the two sides match. The cell ends up turgid, at several hundred kilopascals, and intact.

Two substances cross a membrane at rates you can measure. As the outside concentration is raised, substance A's rate keeps climbing in a straight line; substance B's rate climbs and then levels off. What does that tell you?

  • Substance B is being actively transported and substance A is not, because a rate that levels off is what paying for transport looks like.
  • Substance B crosses through a transport protein, of which the membrane has a fixed number; A crosses the lipid itself.
  • Substance A is charged and substance B is not, so only A feels the voltage that keeps pushing its rate up.
  • Substance B has reached equilibrium across the membrane, so adding more outside no longer changes how fast it crosses.

A ceiling means something is being occupied. A membrane has a fixed number of carriers and each takes a fixed time per molecule, so once they are all busy the rate cannot rise further however much more arrives. Crossing the lipid directly occupies nothing, so that rate never levels off. Note what the curve does not tell you: saturation says a protein is involved, not that energy is being spent, and facilitated diffusion saturates while remaining entirely passive. And substance B has not reached equilibrium — its rate is high and constant, not zero.

A gut cell concentrates glucose to many times the level in the gut using a symporter that admits sodium at the same time. Adding a drug that blocks the sodium–potassium pump stops the glucose accumulating, although the drug does not touch the symporter. Why?

  • The pump is what actually carries the glucose across the membrane; the symporter only opens the way for it to pass.
  • The symporter runs on sodium falling down its gradient, and the pump keeps that gradient up; once it runs down, nothing pays for the climb.
  • Blocking the pump shuts the cell's glucose carriers too, so the membrane becomes impermeable to glucose altogether.
  • The drug must also be blocking the symporter directly, whatever it was designed for, since nothing else could stop the glucose accumulating.

This is what "secondary" means. No ATP goes near the symporter; the energy comes from sodium entering down its electrochemical gradient, and the gradient was bought earlier with ATP by the pump. Stop the pump and the gradient decays over minutes as sodium leaks in, and with it the symporter's power supply. The transport was active all along — the payment was simply made upstream.
