Everything here is running on sunlight

Section 1.6 drew two arrows through an ecosystem: a green one going round and round for matter, and a gold one passing through once for energy. It drew the gold arrow starting at the sun and could not then say why. Section 5.1 supplied half the answer — energy passes once because every transfer sheds some as heat, and heat at one temperature can never be spent again. This chapter supplies the other half, which is where the arrow starts. Almost every joule in every living thing on this planet was put there by a leaf, or by an alga, or by a bacterium, doing the chemistry in this chapter.

Organisms divide by where their carbon comes from. An autotroph builds its own carbon compounds out of carbon dioxide; a heterotroph gets its carbon by eating compounds something else has already built. Those are Section 1.6's producers and consumers, named by their chemistry instead of by their place in a food chain. Most autotrophs run on light, which is photosynthesis; a minority — at hydrothermal vents, in deep rock, in the nitrogen cycle — run on the oxidation of an inorganic chemical instead, and owe the sun nothing. They are a small fraction of the living world and a large fraction of its interesting corners, and everything else is downstream of a leaf.

So: what is light, and why can it do chemistry? Light arrives in indivisible packets. A photon of a given colour carries a fixed amount of energy, set by its wavelength: the energy is Planck's constant times the speed of light divided by λ, which for a whole mole of photons comes to about 119 600 kilojoules divided by the wavelength in nanometres. That single expression gives the whole visible range, and it is worth having the numbers rather than the formula.

A mole of red photons carries about seventy times the average thermal energy in the leaf absorbing it, and a mole of violet photons nearly a hundred and twenty times. But the ratio is not, by itself, what makes light able to do something heat cannot. You could not get there by heating the leaf: to give its molecules 176 kilojoules per mole of thermal energy each you would have to take it past twenty thousand kelvin, and the energy would still arrive in the wrong form when you did. Two things about the form are what matter.

The first is that a photon is absorbed whole or not at all. Its energy is not shared out among everything nearby; it arrives in one place, in one lump, in one event. The second is where that lump goes: into a single electron. Warming a leaf by ten degrees puts energy into every bond and every molecule in it, a little each, and Section 5.5 gave the consequence — it speeds every reaction indiscriminately, including all the ones the leaf needs not to happen. A photon does the opposite. It picks one electron in one molecule and moves it, and leaves everything else exactly as it was.

There is a third difference, and it is the one that answers Section 5.1 on its own terms. That section said work needs a difference in temperature and a cell has none. Sunlight is radiation from a surface at about 5800 kelvin arriving in a leaf at about 300, and it has not cooled on the way. A leaf is not a body at one temperature after all: it has a hot reservoir, 150 million kilometres away, and that is what it is running on.

What the absorbed photon does inside the molecule is the sentence to get right, because it is usually left vague. The electron does not gain a mysterious vigour. It moves into a different orbital — one further out, in which it is held less tightly by the nuclei of the molecule. The molecule is now in an excited state, and the useful thing about that state is stated in Section 5.8's vocabulary: the molecule has moved a long way up the ladder of how readily something gives an electron away. A chlorophyll in the dark will not reduce anything. The same chlorophyll, a picosecond after it has absorbed a red photon, is one of the more powerful reducing agents in biology. Nothing has been energised in general. One electron has been put somewhere it will not stay, and the whole of the light reactions is the business of catching it on the way back down.

The excited state lasts a few billionths of a second. That is not an amount of energy but a deadline: whatever is going to be done with it must be done within that window, or the molecule falls back and the energy leaves as heat or as a redder photon. Section 6.3 is about how a leaf makes the deadline.

Now the reaction. Written the way it usually is, photosynthesis is the equation of Section 5.8 with the arrow reversed:

6 CO2 + 6 H2O → C6H12O6 + 6 O2

Section 5.8 gave the free energy change of the forward direction as −2870 kilojoules per mole. Run backwards it is +2870, and Section 5.2 is categorical about what a positive ΔG means: this does not happen by itself, ever, and something has to pay for it. The light is what pays. That is the entire logic of the chapter, and everything after this is mechanism.

Section 5.8 also said why the number is so large, and the reason was Section 2.2's electronegativity: a fuel is a fuel because its carbons are bonded mostly to hydrogen and have the whole fall towards oxygen ahead of them. Photosynthesis is that fall taken backwards. It pulls electrons away from oxygen, which pulls hardest on shared electrons of all the common elements, and puts them on carbon, which pulls hardly at all. Every difficulty in this chapter comes from that sentence.

The equation hides one thing, and it is worth digging out because the way it was dug out is a good piece of science. Six carbon dioxides go in carrying twelve oxygen atoms, and six oxygen molecules come out carrying twelve. It is natural to conclude that the plant splits carbon dioxide and releases its oxygen. Cornelis van Niel argued in the 1930s that it does not. He was working on purple sulfur bacteria, which photosynthesise using hydrogen sulfide where a plant uses water, and which deposit granules of sulfur rather than giving off oxygen. If those organisms are doing the same chemistry, then the general form of photosynthesis is not "split the carbon dioxide" but "take hydrogen from something and use it to reduce the carbon dioxide" — and the leftover is whatever the hydrogen was taken from. For the bacteria that is sulfur. For a plant it would have to be oxygen, and the oxygen would have to come from the water.

That is a prediction, and in 1941 Samuel Ruben, Merle Randall, Martin Kamen and James Hyde tested it by growing algae with water whose oxygen was the heavy isotope 18O. The label came out in the oxygen gas. Label the carbon dioxide instead and it does not. The measurement was criticised almost immediately, because carbon dioxide and water exchange oxygen atoms with one another in solution and the label could have moved before the plant touched it, and the conclusion has survived every better-controlled repetition since. So the honest equation is uglier:

6 CO2 + 12 H2O → C6H12O6 + 6 O2 + 6 H2O

Twelve waters in and six out, because water is both the source of the electrons and a product of the reaction that uses them. Get the habit of asking, of every arrow in this chapter, where the electron is and what it has just been persuaded to leave.

One last number, for scale, and it is the shakiest in the chapter. Photosynthesis on land and photosynthesis in the sea each fix of the order of a hundred gigatonnes of carbon a year — the published estimates for the land alone run from about 120 up to about 175, which is a disagreement of a third about the largest chemical flux on the planet — so the total is somewhere around two hundred and fifty gigatonnes, fixed and released again every year. That is the gold arrow arriving. The green arrow is the same carbon going round, and Chapter 7 is where it is let go.

Nearly all the energy in the living world enters through this chapter's chemistry. Light can drive it where heat cannot, because a photon is absorbed whole and its energy goes to one electron rather than being shared out, and because sunlight is not at the leaf's temperature. Making a sugar from carbon dioxide and water costs about 2870 kilojoules per mole — Section 5.8's number with its sign reversed — and the oxygen given off comes from the water, not from the carbon dioxide.

A compartment inside a compartment

Section 3.5 described a chloroplast and used it as evidence: two membranes, its own circular DNA, bacterial ribosomes, genes matching cyanobacteria. It named the parts, said that the thylakoid membrane holds the machinery that captures light and the stroma the enzymes that build sugar, and left it there. The parts turn out to be the argument, so it is worth laying them out again with the chemistry in view.

Three membranes, and three watery spaces they keep apart. The outer and inner envelope membranes wrap the organelle, with a thin intermembrane space between them. Inside the inner one is the stroma. And suspended in the stroma, not continuous with the envelope at all, is a third membrane system: flattened sealed sacs called thylakoids, whose interior is the thylakoid lumen. Most of them are stacked into piles — a pile is a granum — and the piles are joined to one another by unstacked sheets running between them, so that as far as anyone has traced it the lumen of a whole chloroplast is one connected volume rather than a few hundred separate bubbles.

That last clause is not a detail. Section 4.1 explained why a lipid bilayer closes into a bag: an exposed edge is the one thing it cannot tolerate, so a sheet seals itself into a compartment. Section 4.7 explained what a compartment is for: a difference held across a membrane is stored energy, because work was done to make it and can be got back by letting it run down. Put the two together and the thylakoid stops being a shape and becomes a machine. It can hold protons at a concentration the stroma does not have, because it is closed. A membrane with the same proteins in it, arranged as an open sheet, would carry out every reaction of Section 6.4 and produce no ATP at all, because there would be nowhere to put the protons. The seal is the store.

With that, the division of labour writes itself. The light reactions are on the thylakoid membrane, because that is where the pigments are and where the gradient can be held. The carbon reactions are in the stroma, because that is where there is room and where the enzymes and the chloroplast's own ribosomes are. Two things cross from the membrane to the stroma — ATP and NADPH — and the spent versions of both come back. Nothing else needs to move, and nothing the two halves make leaves the chloroplast — except the oxygen, which is waste — until there is sugar to export.

The two halves are not evenly spread over the membrane. Photosystem II sits mostly in the stacked regions, where the thylakoids are pressed together; photosystem I and ATP synthase sit mostly in the unstacked sheets and at the edges of the stacks. The reason for at least part of that is blunt: ATP synthase has a knob about 10 nm across on its stromal face, and there is not 10 nm of room between two appressed membranes.

Stacking itself is the argument Section 3.5 made about the cristae of a mitochondrion, run again. The light reactions happen on a membrane, so the amount of them a chloroplast can do at once is set by how much membrane it has; stacking is how you get a great deal of membrane into a small volume. A chloroplast is about 5 µm long and holds dozens of grana, with anything from a handful to a hundred thylakoids in each.

Now step outward, because a chloroplast in the dark with no carbon dioxide is an ornament, and getting carbon dioxide to it is an architectural problem. A leaf is a few tenths of a millimetre thick, and it is thin for the reason Section 3.2 gave: diffusion time grows as the square of distance, so a structure that relies on gases diffusing into it cannot be deep. Inside, the mesophyll cells are packed loosely, with air spaces running between them, so that carbon dioxide travelling as a gas can get within a micrometre or two of every chloroplast before it has to dissolve and cross a wall, a membrane and the stroma. The whole interior surface of a leaf is wet, which is what it must be for a gas to dissolve at it, and the leaf is sealed against the outside by a waxy skin with adjustable pores in it. Each pore is a stoma, made by two guard cells that bow apart to open it.

Which is where the trouble starts, and it is worth naming now because Section 6.8 exists to answer it. The air is 21 per cent oxygen and about 0.042 per cent carbon dioxide. A leaf is fishing a trace gas out of the atmosphere using a wet surface, and Section 4.3's rule for a flux across a barrier — the difference times the area, divided by the thickness — applies to both gases in the pore at once. Water vapour leaves that wet interior far faster than carbon dioxide enters it, because the difference driving it out is enormous: saturated inside, rarely so outside. The same pore does both, and a plant cannot have one without the other. Open it and the plant feeds and dries; shut it and the plant keeps its water and starves. Transpiration is the name of the loss, and a plant in ordinary conditions loses of the order of five hundred grams of water for every gram of dry matter it makes.

A chloroplast keeps three watery spaces apart with three membranes, and the one that matters is the thylakoid lumen, sealed off by a membrane suspended in the stroma. The light reactions happen on that membrane and the carbon reactions in the stroma, with ATP and NADPH crossing between them. The seal is not incidental: it is what lets a gradient be held, and without it the light reactions would run and pay for nothing. Outside, a leaf is thin, wet inside and full of air spaces, and every pore that lets carbon dioxide in lets far more water out.

Green is the part a leaf uses least

A pigment is a molecule that absorbs some visible wavelengths and not others, and its colour is the part it fails to absorb — the light that gets past it and reaches an eye. Hold on to that sentence, because the commonest thing said about leaves gets it backwards.

Chlorophyll has three parts and each does a different job. At the centre is a flat ring with a magnesium ion held in the middle of it by four nitrogen atoms — the same family of ring, with a different metal in the middle, as the haem that carries oxygen in your blood and as the cytochromes that pass electrons in every cell, which is one of the better hints that these molecules are very old. The magnesium is the handle: the proteins that hold chlorophyll grip it there, often with a histidine side chain reaching in from above or below the ring, and that grip sets each molecule at its own place and angle in the membrane. Around the ring runs an alternating sequence of single and double bonds, and that is the part that absorbs light. Trailing off it is a hydrocarbon tail twenty carbons long. The tail does no chemistry at all: it is Section 4.1's hydrophobic anchor, and it is why chlorophyll sits held in the thylakoid membrane with the ring at the surface and the tail in the oil, rather than drifting about in the stroma where it would be useless.

Why does the ring absorb visible light when almost nothing else in a cell does? Because of what the alternating bonds do to the electrons in them. Section 2.1 put an atom's electrons in shells at different distances from the nucleus, and the consequence it did not need then is that those distances are energies: an electron further out is held less tightly, and moving between two levels costs or releases exactly the difference. In an ordinary bond a shared pair belongs to two atoms and the step to the next level up is a large one. In a long alternating run the electrons are not confined to any one pair but spread across the whole system, and the more room they have the smaller that step becomes — until it is small enough for a visible photon to be exactly the right size to cross it. Most molecules in a cell have gaps only an ultraviolet photon can span, which is precisely why they are colourless. The colour a pigment absorbs is the size of that gap, measured in energy. Chlorophyll is green for the same reason a carbon–carbon bond is 350 kilojoules per mole: it is a number about electrons, and it happens to fall where it falls.

Measured in a solvent, chlorophyll a absorbs most strongly at about 430 nm, in the blue-violet, and at about 662 nm, in the red, with a deep trough between them across the green. Those two peaks move by tens of nanometres depending on the solvent and, in a living membrane, on the protein the molecule is bound to — which turns out to matter a great deal, because it lets one molecule do several jobs. The two reaction centres of Section 6.4 are both chlorophyll a; they absorb at 680 and 700 nm and behave quite differently, and the difference is entirely in what is holding them.

That is an absorption spectrum: how strongly a pigment absorbs at each wavelength, measured on the pigment. It says nothing at all about whether the absorbed light drives anything. For that you need an action spectrum: how well each wavelength drives the process, measured on the living thing. Comparing the two is the experiment, and it was first done in 1882, beautifully, with almost no apparatus. Theodor Engelmann laid a filament of a green alga along a microscope slide, used a prism to spread a spectrum along its length so that each part of the filament was lit by a different colour, and added bacteria that swim towards oxygen. The bacteria told him the answer: they crowded at the red end of the filament and at the blue, and thinned out across the green. The action spectrum was drawn by the bacteria, and it had the same shape as chlorophyll's absorption spectrum. That is what makes chlorophyll the pigment doing the work rather than merely a pigment that happens to be present.

Where the two spectra disagree is as informative as where they agree. A leaf's action spectrum is broader than chlorophyll a's absorption spectrum, with activity at wavelengths chlorophyll a barely touches. Something else is absorbing there and passing the energy on, and that is the evidence for the accessory pigments' part in the chemistry. The pigments themselves had been in bottles for decades — carotene since 1831 — so what the gap between the two curves found was not that they existed but that they were connected.

Now the question the section is named for, and it has to be done in three steps because two of the usual answers are wrong. The first wrong answer is that chlorophyll absorbs green light. It does not; green is the band it absorbs least. The second wrong answer is subtler and much more common: that a leaf reflects the green. A leaf is not a mirror. It scatters light internally between cells and air spaces, so a photon that enters is bounced about and passes the pigments many times over, and that path-lengthening means a leaf absorbs roughly three-quarters of the green falling on it, against something over nine-tenths of the blue and the red. What reaches your eye is a modest remainder — and the remainder is green because green is the band where the absorption is weakest. A leaf's colour is a leftover, not a choice. No plant is green in order to be green. Selection acted on what chlorophyll does with blue and red photons; the green that escapes is the part nobody was paid to catch.

Why the gap in chlorophyll sits where it does, leaving that particular hole, is an open question and worth flagging as one. There are arguments from the spectrum of light that reaches a few metres down in water, where this chemistry began; arguments that absorbing least at the peak of the solar spectrum is a way of not being overwhelmed; and arguments that the ring's chemistry does not leave much choice. None of them is settled.

The other pigments are not decoration. An accessory pigment absorbs where chlorophyll a is weak and hands the energy on to it. Chlorophyll b differs from a by one functional group and absorbs at about 453 and 642 nm, filling in either side; the carotenoids are orange and yellow and absorb from about 400 to 500 nm, across the blue-green where both chlorophylls are poor. Between them the leaf covers a much wider band than any one pigment could. Carotenoids are also why an autumn leaf turns yellow: they were there all summer, hidden under the chlorophyll, and when the tree breaks the chlorophyll down and reclaims its nitrogen the carotenoids are what is left. (The reds are a different story — those are made fresh, in autumn, for reasons still argued about.) Which brings us to how the pigments are arranged, and to a measurement from 1932 that is still the clearest way to see it. Robert Emerson and William Arnold gave algae very brief, very bright flashes of light — bright enough that every pigment molecule present must have absorbed a photon — and counted the oxygen. They got one oxygen molecule per flash for about every 2500 chlorophyll molecules. If a chlorophyll were an independent worker, a flash that hit all of them should have produced far more oxygen than that. It does not, and the reason is that the pigments are not independent. They are organised into groups, each serving one place where the chemistry happens, and only that place can do it.

Such a group is a photosystem. The great majority of its pigments are the antenna: a few hundred chlorophylls and carotenoids held in position by proteins, whose only job is to absorb a photon and pass the excitation inwards. They pass it by resonance — one excited molecule hands its excited state to a neighbour and drops back, without either molecule's electrons going anywhere — and it reaches the middle within a few hundred trillionths of a second, comfortably inside the deadline of Section 6.1. At the middle is the reaction centre: a particular pair of chlorophylls, in a particular protein setting, which does the one thing no other pigment in the group does. It gives the electron away.

The arithmetic behind the antenna is simple and convincing. In full sunlight a single chlorophyll absorbs a photon perhaps ten times a second. A reaction centre, once supplied, can work hundreds of times a second. One pigment per centre would leave the machinery idle almost all of the time; a few hundred keeps it busy. An antenna is a funnel, and Emerson and Arnold's 2500 chlorophylls per oxygen, divided by the eight photochemical events an oxygen molecule turns out to need, gives a few hundred pigments per centre — which is what the structures later showed.

Finally, what can happen to an excited chlorophyll, because a leaf's survival depends on which one does. There are four exits. It can pass the excitation to a neighbour, which is what the antenna does. It can hand its electron to an acceptor, which only a reaction centre can do, and which is photosynthesis. It can emit a photon, which is fluorescence. Or it can fall back and shed the energy as heat. The emitted photon is always at the red end of chlorophyll's spectrum, a little redder than its red absorption peak, whatever colour was absorbed: a blue photon's surplus over the red gap has already gone as heat within a picosecond of absorption, before any exit is taken, which is why a blue photon and a red one each buy exactly one excitation. In a leaf working comfortably, almost all of it goes down the second route; the small fraction that fluoresces is measurable from a distance, and measuring it is how photosynthesis is monitored in a field or from a satellite without touching the plant.

The trouble comes when the reaction centres are all busy and the light keeps arriving. An excited chlorophyll with nowhere to send its energy holds it, and the longer it holds it the more likely it is to settle into a longer-lived excited state that can hand its energy to an oxygen molecule — making a form of oxygen that will attack anything nearby, including the membrane the chlorophyll is sitting in. Carotenoids intercept both: they quench the long-lived chlorophyll before it reaches oxygen, and they quench the reactive oxygen if it forms anyway. On top of that a leaf in bright light deliberately detunes part of its own antenna so that it sheds energy as heat instead of passing it on. The detuning switches on within a minute and is slow to switch off, which is why a leaf moved from full sun into shade works below its best efficiency for several minutes afterwards, still throwing away light it could now use. (The lag the other way, when a shaded leaf is moved into full sun, is a different thing and belongs to Section 6.6: the enzyme that fixes carbon takes minutes to be switched on, and the stomata longer to open.)

Chlorophyll absorbs blue and red strongly and green weakly, because a ring of alternating bonds has a small enough gap for a visible photon to cross. An action spectrum measured on a living alga matches that absorption, which is how chlorophyll was shown to be the pigment doing the work, and where the two curves disagree is the evidence that other pigments pass their light on to it. A leaf looks green because green is the band it absorbs least — it still absorbs about three-quarters of it — so the colour is a consequence and not a purpose. Hundreds of antenna pigments feed one reaction centre, because a single chlorophyll is struck far too rarely to keep one busy.

Two pushes, and water pays for both

Strip away the machinery and the light reactions have one job: take electrons away from water and put them on NADP+. Everything else — the oxygen, the protons, the ATP — is a consequence of doing that. Section 5.8 measured jobs of this kind by asking how tightly each end holds its electrons, so write both ends down, along with the places in between.

From water to NADPH is a climb of 1.14 volts, and a volt has a price: moving a mole of electrons up one volt costs 96.5 kilojoules. Two electrons up 1.14 volts is therefore about 220 kilojoules per mole — and that is Section 5.8's number. That section said letting the two electrons on one NADH fall the whole way to oxygen releases about 220 kilojoules per mole, and remarked that it was more than four ATP's worth. This is the same journey in the opposite direction, and the light is what pays for it.

Why two photosystems? Put it as a design problem. A single reaction centre would have to be, in the dark, a better oxidant than +0.82 volts, so that it could pull electrons off water; and, once excited, a better reductant than −0.32 volts, so that it could give them to NADP+. Those are the two ends of a 1.14 volt span. A red photon at 680 nm is 176 kilojoules per mole, which divided by 96.5 is about 1.8 volts for one electron, so the arithmetic does not forbid it — but 1.8 against 1.14 leaves very little room, and every step of any electron chain wastes some of what it is handed. No organism that splits water does it with one photosystem. Two smaller climbs with a downhill run between them give each half a comfortable margin, and the downhill run is not waste at all: it is where the protons get moved.

The evidence for two came before the mechanism, from two measurements by Robert Emerson. The first is the red drop: past about 680 nm the efficiency of photosynthesis falls away sharply although chlorophyll still absorbs there, which is odd if there is one machine. The second is the answer to it, and it is the elegant one. Give a plant far-red light at 700 nm alone, out in the drop, and measure the oxygen. Give it 650 nm light alone and measure the oxygen. Now give it both at once: the rate is higher than the two separately added together. Two beams cannot help one another unless there are two things to drive, with different preferences, and both have to be running for either to be useful. That is the Z scheme, named for the shape the path makes when it is drawn against those potentials: up, down, up.

The same experiments settled a long argument about how many photons an oxygen molecule costs. Otto Warburg, who had measured it first and had a Nobel Prize and a formidable temper, insisted on four. Emerson kept getting eight to twelve. Eight is the theoretical minimum for the two-photosystem scheme — four electrons, two photons each — and eight to ten is what a real leaf achieves in good conditions. Warburg never accepted it.

Now follow one electron. Photosystem II's reaction centre is called P680, after the wavelength at which it itself absorbs best. An excitation arrives, P680 is excited, and within a few picoseconds it hands its electron to a neighbouring pigment and then onwards, leaving P680 as a positively charged hole. That hole is the point of the entire photosystem. The useful product of photosystem II is not the electron that left; it is the oxidant left behind, which at about +1.2 volts is strong enough to do the one thing nothing else in biology does.

It takes electrons from water. The machine that does it is the oxygen-evolving complex, a cluster of four manganese ions and a calcium ion bound on the lumen side of photosystem II, and it faces an awkward arithmetic. The reaction it has to perform is

2 H2O → O2 + 4 H+ + 4 e−

— a four-electron reaction — while a photon removes exactly one electron at a time. So the cluster counts. It holds its oxidation state between hits, becoming more oxidised with each photon P680 delivers, and only when four have accumulated does it take four electrons off two waters at once and release the oxygen. That is a claim you can test directly, and Pierre Joliot and Bessel Kok did in the late 1960s: give dark-adapted chloroplasts a train of single brief flashes and measure the oxygen after each. It does not come out evenly. Nothing appears on the first two flashes and a burst comes on the third — the third and not the fourth, because a cluster left in the dark settles one step along, and about three in four start the count at one — then another on the seventh, another on the eleventh, the peaks blurring as the population drifts out of step. The cluster is a counter, and you can watch it count in fours.

Two things leave. The four protons are released into the lumen, which is the first of the three things making that compartment acidic, and one oxygen molecule diffuses out — the oxygen in the air you are breathing, and a waste product.

Meanwhile the electron has gone into the membrane. It is picked up by plastoquinone, a small oily molecule that takes two electrons and, with them, two protons — and it takes those protons from the stroma. Loaded, it diffuses within the membrane to the cytochrome b6f complex, passes the electrons on, and releases the protons into the lumen. Protons taken from one side and dropped on the other is transport across a membrane, and this complex is the only real proton pump in the chain: it is thought to move about two protons for every electron, by a loop that sends part of the electron flow round a second time. The electron, now well down the hill, is handed to plastocyanin, a small copper protein that shuttles one electron at a time along the lumen face of the membrane to photosystem I.

Photosystem I's reaction centre is P700, and it does the second push. An excitation arrives, P700 gives its electron away, and this time the electron ends up on ferredoxin, on the stromal side, at −0.42 volts: the strongest reductant that leaves the photosystem. P700+, at +0.45 volts, is refilled by plastocyanin arriving with the electron photosystem II sent. An enzyme called ferredoxin–NADP+ reductase then collects two of those electrons, takes a proton from the stroma, and makes NADPH. That proton is the third thing making the lumen acidic relative to the stroma — not by adding to the lumen but by taking from the other side, which does the same job.

Two sentences on the carrier itself, because it is Section 5.8's with a small addition. NADP+ is NAD+ with one extra phosphate group on the ribose at the far end from the business end. The phosphate holds no energy and takes no part in the chemistry. It is a label, and it is read by the proteins: enzymes that take electrons off fuel use the unphosphorylated pool, enzymes that put electrons into things being built use the phosphorylated one. That lets a cell hold one pool mostly oxidised and the other mostly reduced at the same time — the first ready to accept, the second ready to give. A cell running both accounts through one pool could not do both jobs at once, and would have to choose, minute by minute, between taking things apart and building them.

Everything described so far is linear electron flow: water in, oxygen and NADPH and a proton gradient out. But at ferredoxin the path forks. The electron can go to NADP+, or it can be sent back to the cytochrome complex, from there to plastocyanin, and from there to P700 again. That loop is cyclic electron flow, and what it produces is instructive precisely because of what it does not. Protons are still pumped by the cytochrome complex, so ATP is still made. No electron reaches NADP+, so no NADPH is made. No electron is taken from water, so no oxygen appears and no water is consumed. It is photosystem I running on its own as an ATP machine. Section 6.6 will say why a plant needs one, and Figure 6.2 lets you run the path both ways and watch each output stop.

The light reactions move electrons from water to NADP+, a climb of 1.14 volts that costs about 220 kilojoules for every two electrons — Section 5.8's fall, run backwards. It takes two photosystems because no single reaction centre could be both a strong enough oxidant to take electrons from water and a strong enough reductant to reach NADP+ with any margin, and the red drop and the enhancement effect are the measurements that showed there were two. Photosystem II's real product is the hole it leaves behind, which is strong enough to oxidise water; the manganese cluster accumulates four of those before releasing one oxygen molecule. Cyclic flow round photosystem I makes ATP and neither NADPH nor oxygen.

Chapter 4's battery, charged by light

Notice what has not happened yet. Electrons have been lifted twice, water has been split, oxygen has gone, NADPH has been made — and not one molecule of ATP. What the last section actually built, besides the NADPH, was a difference in proton concentration across a sealed membrane. This section spends it, and it does so by a mechanism that is one of the two or three best ideas in biology.

Isolated thylakoids in strong light take the lumen down to about pH 5 against a stroma near pH 8: three units, and the case this section works through. Section 2.4 established that one pH unit is a tenfold difference in hydrogen ion concentration, so three units is a thousandfold. Reading it as "three" rather than "a thousand" gets the ratio wrong by two and a half orders of magnitude and, once the logarithm in the next paragraph has taken the edge off, the energy wrong by a factor of six. A working leaf probably holds its lumen less acid than that, nearer pH 6 and about two units below the stroma, though how acid it gets in the light is still argued between laboratories that measure it in different ways; everything below scales with the answer.

What is a thousandfold difference worth? Section 5.2 gave the arithmetic: RT times the natural logarithm of the ratio, which comes to one value of RT ln 10 for each factor of ten. That section quoted 5.9 kilojoules per mole per tenfold, at body temperature. A leaf is not at body temperature; at 25 °C the same expression gives 5.7. So three decades of pH is about 17 kilojoules per mole of protons, and the two a working leaf probably holds are worth about 11. The only thing that changed from Chapter 5's number is the temperature it was evaluated at.

But Section 4.7 was insistent that a gradient of a charged thing has two terms, and that the electrical one is often as large as the concentration one. Here it is almost absent, and the reason is structural rather than chemical. As protons pile up in the lumen the charge would build too, and it does not, because the thylakoid membrane lets magnesium and chloride ions through: they move to cancel it almost as fast as it appears. So in isolated thylakoids the proton-motive force is very nearly pure pH. How much voltage a working leaf keeps is the lumen's argument again, seen from the other side: one school of measurement finds a real share of it, which would let the lumen stay less acid, and another finds that share an artefact of the method. In a mitochondrion the inner membrane does not permit that, and much of the force there is voltage. Same mechanism, two different splits, and the thing that decides which is the permeability rules of Section 4.3 settling a question about energy.

Now the machine. ATP synthase spans the thylakoid membrane with a ring of subunits in the oily core and a knob on the stromal face. Protons falling from the lumen can only get back to the stroma through the ring, and getting through turns it; the turning ring turns a shaft; the shaft runs up into the knob and distorts its three catalytic sites in turn, and each site, as it is squeezed, releases the ATP it has just assembled. The chemistry is done by mechanical force applied to a protein — which is as literal an example of Section 5.4's transport and mechanical work as this book contains, with the direction reversed.

The ring's size sets the exchange rate, and it has been measured: in spinach chloroplasts the ring has fourteen subunits, and one complete revolution makes three ATP, so about 4.7 protons are spent per ATP. A mitochondrial ring is smaller — eight subunits in cattle — which works out at about 2.7, and why the two differ is a good question that Chapter 7 is better placed to ask.

Which side the knob is on is not an aesthetic matter. It faces the stroma, so the ATP appears in the stroma, which is where the Calvin cycle is waiting for it. Had the same enzyme been built the other way round, every molecule of ATP would have been made inside a sealed sac with nothing in it to spend the ATP on. The orientation is the whole of the delivery system.

Chemiosmosis is the name for this, and photophosphorylation is the name for doing it with light. Now compare it against the rule Section 5.4 laid down, because the comparison repays a moment. That section was categorical: two reactions are coupled only when they share a chemical intermediate, and heat released nearby is no help. Here the shared intermediate is not a molecule at all. It is a difference in concentration across a membrane, and the two machines that share it — the electron chain that charges it and the synthase that spends it — never touch and need not even be near one another. That is a genuine widening of the rule rather than an exception to it: what coupling requires is a shared something that both reactions act on, so that their free energy changes add. A gradient qualifies. Section 4.7 called it a battery; this is the battery with a motor bolted to it.

Peter Mitchell proposed all of this in 1961, and most of the field disbelieved it for about ten years. Everyone was looking for a phosphorylated intermediate, by analogy with every other case of coupling then known, and Mitchell was saying there wasn't one — that the intermediate was a gradient across a membrane, and that an intact closed compartment was therefore part of the mechanism rather than part of the packaging. He got the Nobel Prize in 1978.

The experiment that made the case is this section's best, and it belongs to a chloroplast. In 1966 André Jagendorf and Ernest Uribe soaked isolated thylakoids in a buffer at pH 4, in the dark, until the inside had equilibrated at pH 4. Then they tipped them into a buffer at pH 8 containing ADP and phosphate — still in the dark. For a few seconds the lumen was at 4 and the stroma side at 8, a gradient of four units made by hand, and in those few seconds the thylakoids made ATP. No light. No electron transport. No phosphorylated intermediate anywhere. The gradient alone was enough, which is exactly what Mitchell had claimed and what everyone looking for an intermediate had denied.

The prediction that runs the other way is just as sharp. If the gradient is the intermediate, then anything that lets protons across the membrane by some other route should uncouple the two halves completely. Such a molecule is an uncoupler: a small acid that is soluble in the membrane both with its proton and without it, so it picks one up in the lumen, crosses, and drops it in the stroma. Add one and three things happen at once, and the pattern is diagnostic. The gradient collapses. ATP output falls to nothing. And electron transport speeds up, because the back-pressure that was slowing it is gone. All the energy leaves as heat. Dinitrophenol is the classic example; it does the same to a mitochondrion, and Chapter 7 has its history.

The light reactions do not make ATP directly; they make a proton gradient, and ATP synthase makes ATP from that. Three things acidify the lumen — splitting water, the cytochrome complex pumping, and the reduction of NADP+ — and only the middle one is a pump. Three pH units, the isolated-thylakoid case, is worth about 17 kilojoules for every mole of protons that falls back, and the two a working leaf probably holds about 11; in isolated thylakoids little of the force is voltage, because the membrane lets other ions move to cancel it. The synthase's knob faces the stroma, so the ATP appears where it is needed. The acid-bath experiment made ATP with no light at all, which is what proved the gradient is the intermediate.

Building a sugar out of air

The stroma now has ATP and NADPH, and neither will keep. Section 5.3 established that ATP is a currency and not a store, spent and remade about once a minute; NADPH is the same kind of thing. So whatever the stroma does with them, it has to do now, which is one more reason the two halves of the chloroplast are a few tens of nanometres apart rather than in different organelles.

Start with what is wrong with carbon dioxide, because it has two problems and they are usually run together. The first is that it is the most oxidised carbon there is: both of its bonds go to oxygen, and Section 2.2's electronegativity is what that costs — oxygen pulls hardest of the common elements, so every electron in the molecule is already held about as tightly as an electron in a carbon compound can be, at the bottom of Section 5.8's ladder. Turning it into a sugar means pushing every one of them back up, and that is what the NADPH is for. The second problem is separate and is Section 5.5's: carbon dioxide is also stable. It is small, symmetrical, unreactive, and it does not attack things. Even with all the reducing power in the world to hand, nothing happens until a molecule reaches the transition state, and for something this unreactive that activation energy is high.

The solution to the second problem is not to reduce carbon dioxide at all, but to attach it to something that is already reactive and reduce that. This is carbon fixation, and it is the step that puts carbon into the living world. The acceptor is RuBP, a five-carbon sugar with a phosphate at each end, and the enzyme is rubisco, which Section 6.7 is entirely about. Five carbons plus one makes six, and here is the observation the whole cycle was worked out from: the six-carbon compound never leaves the enzyme: within a fraction of a second the active site adds water to it and cuts it in two, into two three-carbon molecules of 3-⁠phosphoglycerate. Calvin's group never found it among their labelled compounds, and catching it took stopping the enzyme with acid about twelve thousandths of a second after it had started. Off the enzyme, half of it is still intact after an hour; it is the active site that is quick. So the first product to leave the enzyme, the first stable product in the usual phrase, has three carbons, not six, and that fact is the reason most plants are called C3 plants.

It was found with radioactive carbon and a piece of glassware. Melvin Calvin, Andrew Benson and James Bassham grew algae in a thin flat flask — everyone called it the lollipop — lit from both sides, fed them carbonate labelled with 14C, and after a measured interval dropped the whole culture into hot methanol, which kills everything instantly. Spreading the labelled compounds out by two-dimensional paper chromatography and laying the paper on photographic film showed which compounds held the label. The longer the interval, the more spots. At a few seconds there was essentially one, and it was 3-⁠phosphoglycerate. Calvin received the Nobel Prize in 1961; Benson, who did much of the work, did not, and said so for the rest of his long life.

From there the Calvin cycle runs in three phases, and it is worth doing the bookkeeping for three carbon dioxides at once, because one produces fractions of molecules and confuses everybody.

Carboxylation. Three RuBP take three carbon dioxides and become six molecules of 3-⁠phosphoglycerate. Fifteen carbons plus three makes eighteen; six threes are eighteen. No ATP, no NADPH — rubisco needs neither.

Reduction. Each 3-⁠phosphoglycerate is first phosphorylated using an ATP, and then reduced using an NADPH, becoming G3P. The order is Section 5.4's trick exactly: the phosphate is attached first, which makes the molecule less stable and therefore readier to react, and only then is it reduced. Six ATP and six NADPH for the six molecules, and this is the step where the electrons that came from water finally arrive on carbon. It is also a pair of steps Chapter 7 will meet running the other way, on its way to taking the sugar apart.

Regeneration. Six G3P have been made, and here is where most of the cycle's reactions are: five of them are shuffled through a dozen steps — three-carbon, four-carbon, five-carbon, six-carbon and seven-carbon intermediates, swapping fragments — back into three RuBP, at a cost of three more ATP. The sixth G3P leaves.

Treat that as an order of magnitude and not a measurement — the standard values and the values inside a stroma are not the same numbers, and Section 5.3 spent a page on why — but the shape of the answer is right, and it is a long way better than most machines.

Why a cycle at all, rather than a line? Because the acceptor is consumed by the reaction it makes possible. A chloroplast that fixed carbon without rebuilding RuBP would fix as much carbon as it had RuBP and then stop, and how much RuBP it had would be the only question worth asking. Rebuilding the acceptor is what turns a stock into a rate: a small pool goes round indefinitely and processes an unlimited amount of carbon, which is Section 5.8's third shape of pathway and the reason it listed cyclic ones separately. A third of the cycle's ATP, and five of every six sugars it makes, go on rebuilding the acceptor, and that is not overhead. It is what the cycle is.

The old name for this half of photosynthesis is the dark reactions, and it is wrong twice over. It does not happen in the dark, and it does not merely stop when the ATP runs out — it is actively switched off. Three things change in the stroma when the light comes on, and all three point the same way. The protons leaving the stroma for the lumen raise the stromal pH from about 7 to about 8. Magnesium ions move out of the lumen into the stroma to balance the charge those protons took with them. And ferredoxin, which Section 6.4 left holding electrons, reduces a small protein called thioredoxin, which in turn reduces a regulatory disulfide bridge on four of the cycle's enzymes and switches them on: the two phosphatases of the regeneration phase, the kinase that finishes it by rebuilding RuBP, and the enzyme that does the reduction. The first two changes are what switch rubisco on, as the margin note explains, and the two phosphatases also work far better at pH 8 with magnesium present than at pH 7 without — which is Section 5.6's argument about an enzyme's conditions, applied to a whole compartment rather than to one protein. The thioredoxin switch is a separate mechanism: Section 5.7's covalent modification, with a disulfide bridge in place of a phosphate. In the dark thioredoxin is re-oxidised and its four enzymes go off again. The light does not merely supply this cycle. It turns it on.

Rubisco has an extra requirement that is easy to misread. Before it will work at all, a carbon dioxide molecule has to attach itself to a particular lysine side chain in the active site, and a magnesium ion has to bind to the result. That carbon dioxide is not the substrate and is never fixed; it is part of the enzyme's switched-on state. Rubisco needs a carbon dioxide that it will not use in order to use the one it will. Getting a leaf's rubisco into that state takes minutes, with the help of a second enzyme that clears sugar phosphates out of sites not yet switched on, and it is the main reason a shaded leaf moved into full sun takes several minutes to reach its best rate; the stomata, slower still to open, account for most of the rest.

What leaves is G3P, and what a plant does with it is worth a paragraph because it is where the chemistry meets the gardening. Most of it is exported from the chloroplast through a transporter in the envelope that takes one phosphate in for every triose phosphate it lets out, so the stroma never loses its phosphate. In the cytosol it becomes sucrose, and sucrose is the photosynthate the plant actually ships — down to the roots, out to the fruit, up to the growing tips. Whatever is made faster than it can be shipped is built into starch and stored in the stroma, in the grains Section 3.5 mentioned, and drawn down again through the night. A leaf that has run out of starch by four in the morning is a leaf that will grow more slowly that day.

One thing remains unbalanced, and it is the loose end Section 6.4 left. The cycle wants nine ATP for every six NADPH: a ratio of three to two. Linear electron flow does not deliver that ratio. Work it through with the numbers from the last two sections — two waters split give four electrons and two NADPH, and put twelve protons into the lumen, four from the water and about eight from the cytochrome complex; twelve protons at about 4.7 per ATP is about 2.6 ATP, where three were wanted. (The third of Section 6.5's sources, the proton taken out of the stroma for each NADPH, is conventionally left out of that sum and would make it slightly less unfavourable.) The shortfall is modest and it is real, and it is what cyclic electron flow is for: photosystem I going round on its own pumps protons and makes ATP without touching NADP+, and a plant adjusts how much of it runs according to what it needs. The exact figures in that calculation are all still argued over, each of them a separate measurement, so treat the conclusion as firm and the arithmetic as approximate. Figure 6.3 is the cycle with the ledger attached, and starving it of one input at a time is the way to see which phase depends on which.

Carbon dioxide is both fully oxidised and unreactive, so it is not reduced directly: it is attached to a five-carbon acceptor, and the enzyme cuts the six-carbon product into two three-carbon molecules before letting it go. The cycle then reduces those with ATP and NADPH, and sends five of every six sugars it makes, with the other third of its ATP, back into rebuilding the acceptor, which is what makes it a cycle rather than a line. Nine ATP and six NADPH per three carbon dioxides; eighteen and twelve per glucose. It is not the dark reactions: light raises the stroma's pH and moves magnesium into it, which switches rubisco on, and reduces thioredoxin, which switches on four more of the cycle's enzymes.

One active site, two gases

One enzyme has now done the load-bearing step three times without being looked at. Rubisco — ribulose bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase, and the acronym is a mercy — attaches carbon dioxide to the acceptor, and essentially all the carbon in every living thing came through it. It is also slow, and at full tilt it takes the wrong gas about once a second, and there is more of it on Earth than of any other protein. Those three facts are usually presented as a paradox. They are one fact.

Take the abundance first, because it is the measurement that keeps the rest honest. In the leaf of a C3 plant, rubisco is commonly a third to a half of all the soluble protein, and it carries a comparable share of the plant's invested nitrogen. Globally the best recent estimate puts the total mass of it at about 0.7 gigatonnes — roughly ninety kilograms for every person alive, which is more than most of them weigh, and more than there is of any other protein anywhere. Nothing is that abundant by accident.

Then the speed. Section 5.6 gave the range of turnover numbers across biology as a few per second at one end and a million at the other, with carbonic anhydrase near the top — which is why a red cell can load and unload carbon dioxide in the fraction of a second it spends in a capillary. Rubisco is near the bottom: about three carbon dioxides a second when it is saturated, and averaged over a season in the field, far less than that. Now read the two measurements together with Section 5.6's definition of a maximum rate, which is how much enzyme there is multiplied by how fast each molecule cycles. A plant cannot have the second, so it buys the first. Ninety kilograms per person is what three turns a second costs.

And now the mistake, which is the section's subject. The active site will take an oxygen molecule in place of a carbon dioxide, attach it to the acceptor instead, and produce something useless. To see why it can, look at what the enzyme has to do to work at all. Carbon dioxide is inert; to get it to react, rubisco holds RuBP in a rearranged, strongly electron-rich form that will attack a molecule as unreactive as carbon dioxide. A form reactive enough to do that is also reactive enough to attack oxygen — and the two gases are alike in exactly the ways a binding site finds hardest to read. Both are small. Both are uncharged. Neither has a hydrogen to bond to, and neither has a strong dipole. There is not much for a pocket to grip that would distinguish them. The enzyme does discriminate, and by a good deal, but it does so imperfectly and the discrimination costs it speed.

How often does it go wrong? The arithmetic is worth doing in four steps, because each step changes the answer by a lot and the intermediate numbers are where the intuition lives.

What follows is photorespiration, and it has that name because it consumes oxygen and gives off carbon dioxide in the light, which from outside looks like respiration. The oxygenation gives one 3-⁠phosphoglycerate, which is fine, and one molecule of 2-⁠phosphoglycolate, which is not: two carbons, no use to the cycle, and an inhibitor of enzymes the cycle itself needs, so it cannot simply be left where it fell. Getting rid of it takes a route through three organelles.

It starts in the chloroplast, where the phosphate is taken off. The glycolate then crosses into a peroxisome — and this is the answer to a question Section 5.8's sorting exercise raised without settling, which is why a cell keeps a compartment for reactions that make hydrogen peroxide. One of the salvage steps hands hydrogen straight to oxygen and makes peroxide, and a catalase in the same compartment destroys it before it can go anywhere. Doing the step in the cytosol would not be an option. The product goes on to a mitochondrion, where two of the two-carbon molecules are combined into one three-carbon one — and in that step a carbon is released as carbon dioxide and a nitrogen as ammonia. Both have to be recaptured, which costs more. What comes back to the chloroplast, several steps later, is one 3-⁠phosphoglycerate.

Count the carbon. Two oxygenations produce two glycolates, four carbons in total, and three of those four come back into the cycle. So the salvage is not a failure: it recovers three carbons in four, where doing nothing would lose all four and leave an inhibitor behind as well. But it costs ATP, it costs reducing power, and the carbon that is released has to be fixed again from scratch. Added up over a day, at 25 °C in ordinary air, photorespiration costs a C3 plant on the order of a fifth to a third of the carbon it could otherwise have fixed. Hot and dry, it costs considerably more, for reasons Section 6.8 takes up.

Now the question the section has been building to, and it is a question about evolution rather than about chemistry. Why has this not been fixed?

The first answer is that the atmosphere changed underneath it. Carbon fixation of this kind is very old — on the order of three billion years — and it arose in air that held a great deal of carbon dioxide and essentially no free oxygen. In that world there was nothing for rubisco to confuse, and no selection whatever on telling the two apart. The oxygen came later, and it came from this chapter: the cyanobacteria of Section 3.5, running the water-splitting reaction of Section 6.4, put it there over a couple of billion years, and one of their descendants is the chloroplast doing it now. Rubisco has a problem created by its own success, and the reason selection could not have prepared for it is in Darwin's argument as Section 1.7 states it: individuals whose inherited traits suit their environment leave more descendants. The environment meant there is the one they are living in, never the one their descendants will face, so selection cannot improve a property that is not yet costing anything.

The second answer is that improving it may not be free. Compare the rubiscos of many different organisms and a pattern appears: the ones that discriminate best between the two gases are the slowest, and the fastest discriminate worst. If that trade-off is a real constraint of the chemistry, then there is no version available that is both fast and selective, and every organism is sitting somewhere on the same curve, placed by its own conditions — a cyanobacterium in water at one end, a plant in dry air at the other. How tight the trade-off really is has been reopened by recent work, and it is not settled, so the honest statement is that there is evidence of a constraint and argument about its strength.

The third answer is that photorespiration may not be pure loss. There is evidence that it protects a leaf whose light is plentiful and whose carbon dioxide is not, by consuming ATP and reducing power when there is too little carbon dioxide to consume them — without which, as Section 6.3 described, the light reactions back up and an excited chlorophyll with nowhere to send its energy starts damaging the membrane. Plants engineered to lack the salvage route do badly in ordinary air. This argument is real and it is also the weakest of the three, and it should not be allowed to soften the first two: the cost is large and it is measured.

Which is why calling rubisco a bad enzyme is not quite right, and the evidence that it is not is the same evidence that it has a problem. A plant that was managing comfortably would not spend a third to a half of its leaf protein, and a comparable share of its nitrogen, on one slow enzyme. The abundance is the compensation, and Figure 6.4 lets you take the compensation away and watch what happens.

Rubisco is slow, abundant and imperfectly selective, and the three are one fact: a plant compensates for about three turns a second by building an enormous amount of the enzyme — a third to a half of a leaf's soluble protein, and about 0.7 gigatonnes worldwide. It takes oxygen instead of carbon dioxide roughly once in every three or four turns, because the two gases are alike in the ways a binding site can read and because the form of the acceptor that will attack one will attack the other. The salvage recovers three carbons in four and costs a fifth to a third of a C3 plant's potential gain. It has not been improved away because the oxygen arrived after the enzyme did, put there by photosynthesis itself, and because speed and selectivity appear to trade against one another.

Two answers to the same problem

Put Section 6.2's trouble and Section 6.7's together and the problem states itself. A leaf must open its stomata to admit carbon dioxide, and while they are open it loses water far faster than it gains carbon. It can close them, and then two things happen inside: rubisco keeps consuming carbon dioxide so the internal concentration falls, and the light reactions keep making oxygen so the internal concentration of that rises. Falling carbon dioxide and rising oxygen is precisely the condition that makes rubisco take the wrong gas. The defence against drying is the same action that causes photorespiration, which is why this is a problem and not merely a cost.

Heat makes every part of it worse, and by three separate routes, which is why hot climates are where the answers are found. A hot plant must close its stomata more of the time to keep its water. As water warms, carbon dioxide becomes less soluble in it faster than oxygen does, so the dissolved ratio the last section computed shifts against the plant. And rubisco's own preference for carbon dioxide falls as it warms. Three effects, all the same way.

Most plants live with it. A C3 plant — named because rubisco's first product has three carbons — is the arrangement described in every section so far, and it covers most species, including nearly every tree in a temperate forest, and wheat, and rice. In cool, damp, moderately lit places it is the cheapest design there is, because it does nothing extra at all. Two groups of plants do something extra, and the striking thing is that the two solutions are the same chemistry separated along two different axes.

A C4 plant separates the two steps in space. The trick is to fix the carbon dioxide twice, with a different enzyme doing it first. In a mesophyll cell, PEP carboxylase takes phosphoenolpyruvate — the compound at the top of Section 5.3's ladder of phosphate donors — and attaches a carbon to it, giving a four-carbon acid. Two things about that enzyme matter and they are both consequences of one fact: it does not use carbon dioxide at all. It uses bicarbonate, the charged ion that carbon dioxide becomes in water, which Section 2.4's blood buffer already introduced. An oxygen molecule resembles bicarbonate in nothing — bicarbonate is charged, oxygen is not — so this enzyme cannot make rubisco's mistake. And bicarbonate is the more plentiful of the two: at the cell's pH there is about ten times as much of it as of dissolved carbon dioxide, and carbonic anhydrase, the fast enzyme of Section 5.6, keeps it topped up, so this enzyme goes on working at levels of carbon dioxide at which rubisco slows badly.

The four-carbon acid then travels, through the plasmodesmata of Section 3.7, into a bundle-sheath cell — the ring of cells around a leaf vein, which in a C4 plant is large, thick-walled and packed with chloroplasts. There the acid is decarboxylated, releasing carbon dioxide right beside rubisco, which is kept in the bundle sheath and nowhere else. The bundle-sheath walls are built to leak slowly, so the carbon dioxide accumulates to something like ten times the concentration in the air. At that concentration the arithmetic of Section 6.7 turns round: oxygenation almost stops. The three-carbon remainder returns to the mesophyll and is phosphorylated back into PEP, and that is where the cost sits — two extra ATP for every carbon dioxide delivered. Check that against Section 6.6's ledger rather than taking it as small: nine ATP for three carbon dioxides is three per carbon, so adding two takes it to five. A C4 plant spends two-thirds more ATP per carbon fixed than a C3 plant does.

It is a pump. A C4 plant spends ATP to concentrate a substrate, exactly as Section 4.6's pump spends ATP to concentrate an ion, and for the same reason: to make a downstream machine work in conditions it could not otherwise work in. Whether it is worth two extra ATP depends entirely on the climate, which is why C4 plants are the grasses of hot open places — maize, sugarcane, sorghum, millet, and the crabgrass that takes over a lawn in August — and why they lose to C3 plants in cool, dim, wet conditions where photorespiration would have been slight and the two ATP buy nothing. The best evidence that it is a real answer to a real problem is that it has been arrived at independently more than sixty times in the flowering plants alone. About three per cent of land plant species are C4, and they account for something like a quarter of all photosynthesis on land.

CAM — crassulacean acid metabolism, named after the stonecrop family it was first described in — separates the same two steps in time. The stomata open at night, when the air is cool and damp and the water lost per carbon gained is a fraction of what it would be at noon. PEP carboxylase takes the carbon in as bicarbonate and makes a four-carbon acid exactly as in a C4 plant, and the acid is pumped into the vacuole of Section 3.8 and held there until morning. By day the stomata are shut. The acid comes back out of the vacuole, is decarboxylated, and rubisco works on the released carbon dioxide behind a closed door, at a concentration high enough that oxygen is no competition. The plant is measurably sour at dawn and not at dusk, which anybody can test on a leaf of a jade plant or a pineapple.

The cost of CAM is the vacuole. However large it is, that is the night's ration, and the plant cannot fix more carbon in a day than it could store acid in a night — which is why CAM plants are slow-growing and why so many of them are succulent, since a big vacuole is the point. Cacti, agaves, pineapples and a large fraction of the orchids do it; a number of plants switch between CAM and ordinary C3 behaviour as the season dries, which is about as clear a demonstration as one could want that it is a response to a problem.

Two cautions about that table. The water figures are the least reliable numbers in the chapter — different sources measure them on different species under different conditions and disagree by a factor of two or three, and the ranges above are wide because the disagreement is real. And the last row is not a ranking. C3 is not a failed C4: under the conditions most of the world's plants actually live in, doing nothing extra is the better answer, which is why most plants do nothing extra. There is even a prediction in it. The C4 advantage comes from concentrating a scarce gas, so it should shrink as the air's carbon dioxide rises — and the modelling says it should disappear somewhere near 700 parts per million.

Which is where this chapter hands on, and the handover is short because Chapter 5 already wrote it. Section 5.8 said that metabolism is long because the fall has to be taken in parcels the size of one ATP, and that a cell holds very little of each carrier and recycles it constantly. This chapter is the other end of that fall. Light has pushed electrons from water, which holds them more tightly than almost anything, up onto carbon, which holds them loosely — 2870 kilojoules of it per mole of sugar, paid for in photons and banked in bonds. Chapter 7 is what happens when a cell lets them go again: the same electrons, falling back to the same oxygen, down a chain in a membrane, charging a proton gradient and turning the same kind of rotary machine Section 6.5 described. The mechanism will look familiar because it is the one in Section 6.5, running on a different membrane in the organelle beside this one. Between them, the carbon goes round and the energy goes through.

A leaf cannot admit carbon dioxide without losing water, and closing the pore lowers the carbon dioxide inside while raising the oxygen — which is exactly what makes rubisco go wrong. Heat makes all three parts of that worse. A C4 plant answers it in space: PEP carboxylase, which takes bicarbonate and so cannot confuse oxygen for it, fixes carbon in one cell, and the four-carbon acid releases it around rubisco in another, at two extra ATP per carbon delivered — two-thirds more than a C3 plant spends. A CAM plant answers it in time, taking carbon in at night and storing it in the vacuole as acid. Neither is better than C3 in the abstract: each wins in a climate, and most plants live where doing nothing extra is cheapest.

Key ideas

  • Nearly all the energy in the living world enters through photosynthesis. Light can drive chemistry where heat cannot, because a photon is absorbed whole and delivered to one electron rather than shared out, and because sunlight arrives from a surface at 5800 kelvin and so is not at the leaf's temperature.
  • A mole of red photons at 680 nm carries 176 kilojoules, about seventy times the thermal energy a leaf holds per mole at 25 °C. Absorbing one moves an electron into an orbital where it is held less tightly, which makes the molecule a far better electron donor than it was — that, and nothing vaguer, is what "energised" means.
  • Making a glucose from carbon dioxide and water costs about 2870 kilojoules per mole: Section 5.8's number with its sign reversed. The oxygen released comes from the water, not from the carbon dioxide, which van Niel argued from sulfur bacteria and a heavy-oxygen label confirmed.
  • A chloroplast keeps three watery spaces apart. The thylakoid membrane is a separate sealed system inside the stroma, and the seal is what lets a gradient be held; without it every reaction of the light reactions would still run and pay for nothing.
  • Chlorophyll absorbs blue and red strongly and green weakly, because a ring of alternating bonds has a gap small enough for a visible photon to cross. A leaf looks green because green is the band it absorbs least — it still absorbs about three-quarters of it — so the colour is a leftover and not a purpose.
  • An action spectrum measured on a living alga matches chlorophyll's absorption spectrum, which is what identified the pigment; where the two disagree is the evidence that the accessory pigments pass their light on to it.
  • A few hundred antenna pigments feed one reaction centre, because a single chlorophyll is struck far too rarely to keep one busy. An excited chlorophyll can pass its excitation on, do the photochemistry, fluoresce, or fall to heat; carotenoids clean up when the light outruns the chemistry.
  • The light reactions move electrons from water to NADP+, a climb of 1.14 volts costing about 220 kilojoules per two electrons. Two photosystems are needed because no one reaction centre could be both a strong enough oxidant to take electrons from water and a strong enough reductant to reach NADP+; the red drop and the enhancement effect are the measurements that showed there were two.
  • Photosystem II's useful product is the hole it leaves behind, at about +1.2 volts the most powerful oxidant in biology. A manganese cluster accumulates four of those before releasing one oxygen molecule, which is why flashing light gives oxygen with a period of four.
  • Three things acidify the thylakoid lumen — splitting water, the cytochrome complex pumping, and NADP+ reduction taking a proton from the stroma — and only the middle one is a pump. Three pH units, the isolated-thylakoid case, is about 17 kilojoules per mole of protons at 25 °C, and the two a working leaf probably holds about 11; in isolated thylakoids little of the force is voltage, because the membrane lets other ions cancel it.
  • ATP synthase makes ATP from that gradient, with its knob facing the stroma so the ATP appears where the Calvin cycle is. The gradient is the shared intermediate, which widens Section 5.4's rule about coupling rather than breaking it; the acid-bath experiment made ATP in the dark and proved it.
  • Carbon dioxide is both fully oxidised and unreactive, so it is not reduced directly but attached to a five-carbon acceptor. The enzyme cuts the six-carbon product in two before letting it go, which is why the first stable product has three carbons.
  • Nine ATP and six NADPH per three carbon dioxides; eighteen and twelve per glucose. A third of the ATP, and five of every six sugars made, go on rebuilding the acceptor. It is a cycle because the acceptor is consumed by the reaction it enables.
  • They are not the dark reactions. Light raises the stroma's pH and moves magnesium into it, which switches rubisco on, and reduces thioredoxin, which switches on four more of the cycle's enzymes.
  • Rubisco is slow, abundant and imperfectly selective, and those are one fact: about three turns a second is compensated for by building a third to a half of a leaf's soluble protein out of it, some 0.7 gigatonnes worldwide. It takes oxygen roughly once in three or four turns, and the salvage recovers three carbons in four at a cost of a fifth to a third of a C3 plant's potential gain.
  • That confusion is not a design fault but a consequence of an atmosphere that changed after the enzyme evolved — and the oxygen was put there by photosynthesis itself. Speed and selectivity also appear to trade against one another, so there may be no better version to reach.
  • A C4 plant concentrates carbon dioxide around rubisco by fixing it first with PEP carboxylase, which takes bicarbonate and so cannot mistake oxygen for it, at two extra ATP per carbon. A CAM plant does the same thing at night and stores the carbon in its vacuole as acid. Each wins in a climate, and most plants are C3 because doing nothing extra is usually cheapest.

Glossary

Check your understanding

A cell cannot get work out of heat, because it is all at one temperature. Why can a leaf get work out of light?

  • Because light carries much more energy than heat does, so there is more of it to work with.
  • Because a photon is absorbed whole and its energy goes to a single electron, and because sunlight comes from a surface far hotter than the leaf, so the leaf does have a temperature difference to work across.
  • Because light is a form of potential energy and heat is a form of kinetic energy.
  • Because chlorophyll converts light into chemical energy directly, without any intermediate form.

The answer that light simply carries more energy is the one worth arguing with, because the quantities are right and the reasoning is not: a sufficiently hot object would supply the same energy per mole and still be useless, since it would supply it shared out among everything. What matters is that the energy is not shared out — it arrives whole, in one event, and lands on one electron. And Section 5.1's rule is about a system at a single temperature, which a sunlit leaf is not: the sun is the hot reservoir. Calling light potential energy and heat kinetic gets the physics wrong in a tempting way, and a direct conversion into chemical energy describes a step that does not exist, since what chlorophyll produces is an excited state and then a separated charge.

Which statement about a leaf's colour is correct?

  • Chlorophyll absorbs green light most strongly, and the leaf appears green for that reason.
  • A leaf reflects essentially all the green light that falls on it, which is why none of it is used.
  • Green is the band chlorophyll absorbs least; a leaf still absorbs about three-quarters of the green falling on it, and the modest remainder that escapes is what makes it look green.
  • Leaves are green because green light damages chloroplasts, so plants evolved to avoid absorbing it.

Saying chlorophyll absorbs green most strongly is the reversal, and it is the commonest error about the subject: a pigment's colour is what it does not absorb. Saying a leaf reflects the green is the subtler error and the more interesting one — a leaf is not a mirror. Light entering a leaf is scattered between cells and air spaces and passes the pigments many times, so most of even the least-absorbed band is eventually caught. Saying plants evolved to avoid green because it damages them puts a purpose where there is only a consequence. Selection acted on what chlorophyll does with blue and red photons; nothing selected for the leftover, and why chlorophyll's gap falls where it does is still an open question.

Dark-adapted chloroplasts are given a series of single, very brief flashes of light, and the oxygen released after each flash is measured. Almost none appears after the first two flashes, a burst appears after the third, and further bursts after the seventh and the eleventh. What does this show?

  • That only one flash in four is bright enough to excite a reaction centre.
  • That the oxygen-evolving complex accumulates oxidising equivalents, holding its count between flashes, and releases one oxygen molecule only once four electrons have been removed.
  • That three-quarters of the oxygen produced is re-absorbed by the chloroplast before it can be measured.
  • That photosystem I and photosystem II take turns, so only every other flash contributes.

Splitting two waters is a four-electron reaction, and a photon removes one electron at a time. Something has to store the count in between, and the manganese cluster is what does it — which is why the oxygen comes in bursts with a period of four rather than in quarters after every flash. The idea that only one flash in four is bright enough would predict a period of four for the wrong reason, and cannot explain why the first burst is on the third flash rather than the fourth, which is a clue in itself: a dark-adapted cluster is mostly already one step along. The peaks blur as the flashes go on, because individual clusters miss or double-count and the population drifts out of step.

Isolated thylakoids are soaked in a pH 4 buffer in the dark, then transferred to a pH 8 buffer containing ADP and phosphate, still in the dark. They make ATP. What does the experiment establish?

  • That light is not required for photosynthesis, only for the carbon reactions.
  • That acidic conditions activate ATP synthase, which then works on whatever substrates are present.
  • That a proton gradient across the membrane is by itself sufficient to drive ATP synthesis, so it is the intermediate between electron transport and ATP and no phosphorylated intermediate is needed.
  • That ATP synthase can run in either direction depending on the conditions.

The point of the experiment is what is absent: no light, no electron transport, and therefore no opportunity for any phosphorylated intermediate to have been made. All that was supplied was a difference in proton concentration, made by hand, and ATP followed. That is precisely Mitchell's claim, made five years earlier and still widely disbelieved, because everyone was looking for the intermediate by analogy with the couplings of Section 5.4. Reading the result as acid switching the synthase on is a real misreading — it treats the low pH as a signal rather than as a store — and the test against it is that the ATP appears as the gradient dissipates and stops when it is gone. That the synthase can run in either direction is true, and is not what this experiment shows.

PEP carboxylase, the enzyme that fixes carbon first in a C4 plant, never produces the useless two-carbon compound that rubisco does. What is the main reason?

  • It works at a much higher temperature, at which oxygen is far less soluble.
  • Its substrate is bicarbonate, which is a charged ion, and oxygen resembles it in nothing — so there is no confusion for the active site to make.
  • It is kept in the bundle-sheath cells, where the oxygen concentration is very low.
  • It is much faster than rubisco, so oxygen molecules do not have time to enter the site.

Rubisco's difficulty is that carbon dioxide and oxygen are both small, uncharged and hard for a pocket to tell apart. Bicarbonate is charged, and that single difference is enough: nothing about an oxygen molecule resembles it. Placing the enzyme in the bundle sheath has the anatomy backwards — PEP carboxylase is in the mesophyll and rubisco is in the bundle sheath, which is the whole geometry of the mechanism. Crediting its speed confuses rate with selectivity: a faster enzyme meeting the same two molecules would make the same mistake more often per second, not less often per turn. The answer about working hotter is doubly wrong, since a C4 plant's advantage is greatest in the heat and warming the water shifts the dissolved ratio against carbon dioxide.
