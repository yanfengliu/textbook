## Must fix before this ships

### 1. Chapter 3 §3.2 — the motor-versus-diffusion claim contradicts the table three paragraphs above it

> "Eukaryotic cells move material about on tracks, hauled by motor proteins at about a micrometre a second — Section 3.6 — which beats diffusion over any distance greater than a few tens of micrometres"

Do the arithmetic the section just taught. The table uses D = 1,000 µm²/s and t = x²/2D. At x = 30 µm, diffusion takes 0.45 s and a 1 µm/s motor takes 30 s. Diffusion is about seventy times faster. The two are equal at x = 2 mm, not at "a few tens of micrometres" — the claim is out by roughly two orders of magnitude, and it is out against the chapter's own numbers, on the same screen, in a section whose whole method is that the reader can check the arithmetic.

Why it matters: it is the one place in either chapter where a diligent reader who does what the chapter asks is punished for it. It is also load-bearing — `sav-adaptations` names motor transport as one of the three escapes, and §3.6 repeats the comparison (correctly, over an axon, where motors take days and diffusion takes years).

What I would do: the sentence is repairable without losing the point, because the point is right for the cargo motors actually carry. A vesicle is not oxygen: its diffusion constant in cytoplasm is of order 0.1–1 µm²/s, a thousandfold smaller than the table's figure, which puts the crossover at a few micrometres. Say that — "a vesicle wanders a thousand times more slowly than an oxygen molecule, so motors win from a few micrometres up" — and add the second reason, which is stronger and needs no numbers at all: diffusion has no destination. It delivers everywhere at once and nowhere in particular, and a cell that needs a mitochondrion *here* cannot get one by waiting.

Confidence: high. This is arithmetic, and I did it twice.

### 2. Chapter 3 §3.5 — mitochondria do not divide with bacterial division machinery

> "Both divide by pinching in two, using proteins descended from the bacterial division machinery"

For chloroplasts this is right: they constrict with an FtsZ ring inherited from the cyanobacterial ancestor (now nuclear-encoded). For mitochondria it is wrong in every organism the reader is likely to care about. Animals, fungi and plants lost mitochondrial FtsZ; mitochondrial fission there is driven by Drp1/Dnm1, a dynamin-related protein of host eukaryotic ancestry. Mitochondrial FtsZ survives only in scattered protists and algae.

Why it matters more than one clause usually would: this sits inside the chapter's best argument, the one whose explicit subject is *which observations discriminate* between endosymbiosis and infolding. Offered as discriminating evidence, this one points the other way for mitochondria — the machinery is the host's. A section that teaches a reader to audit evidence should not have an unaudited item in its own list. Note also that `organelle-genomes` and `endosymbiosis-evidence` are written against this list, and `fig-symbiont`'s alt text scores "division by fission" as an observation.

What I would do: keep the fact and drop the mechanism, or split it. "Neither can be built from scratch: every mitochondrion comes from a mitochondrion" is the evidence, and it is untouched. If you want the machinery, it is a better paragraph told honestly — the chloroplast kept the bacterial ring, the mitochondrion in your cells is pinched by a host protein instead, and the symbiont has been so thoroughly absorbed that even its division is now run from outside.

Confidence: high on the biology. Judgement call on how much prose to spend fixing it.

### 5. Chapter 3 leans on "osmosis" three times and the book has never defined it

> §3.3 "so water floods in by osmosis and the cell would burst within seconds"
> §3.7 "Water enters by osmosis, the contents press outwards, and the wall pushes back"
> §3.8 "the cell pumps solutes, so water follows by osmosis and the vacuole swells"

Plus twice inside glossary definitions (`cell-wall`, `turgor`) and once in the key ideas ("a peptidoglycan wall that resists osmotic pressure"). There is no `osmosis` entry in chapter 1's, chapter 2's or chapter 3's glossary, no `tb-term`, and no inline gloss anywhere.

Each of the three is a load-bearing step, not decoration: it is the whole reason a bacterium needs a wall, the whole reason a plant stands up, and the whole mechanism by which the central vacuole solves §3.2's problem. The word is doing the work and the reader has not been given it. `npm run check` cannot see this, because an unmarked word is not a term.

What I would do: chapter 4 owns osmosis properly, so do not teach it here — but give it six words at first use in §3.3 ("osmosis, the movement of water towards the saltier side — Chapter 4"), and mark it as a chapter-3 term so the popover works in all three places. Alternatively a margin note in §3.3, which is where the idea first has to carry weight.

Confidence: high.

### 7. Chapter 2 §2.4 — the buffer runs the wrong way across the page

> "Push hydrogen ions in from the left and the reaction runs to the left, consuming them and releasing carbon dioxide"

The equation printed two lines above is `CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻`. H⁺ is on the **right**. Adding it pushes the equilibrium left, which is what the rest of the sentence correctly says — so the sentence contradicts itself as well as the equation, since something entering from the left would drive the reaction right.

Small, and certain. Fix: "Push hydrogen ions in from the right…", or drop the direction and write "Add hydrogen ions and the reaction runs to the left".

Confidence: high.

### 8. Chapter 3 §3.1 — de Duve was not in Brussels in the 1950s

> "Working this way in Brussels in the 1950s, Christian de Duve kept finding a set of digestive enzymes…"

The lysosome work was done at the Catholic University of Louvain, in Leuven. De Duve's laboratory did not move to Brussels until the medical faculty relocated to Woluwe in the 1970s, well after the lysosome and after his second lab at Rockefeller opened in 1962.

Why it matters at all: everything else in this passage is exact — the fractionation, the prediction from biochemistry before the photograph, the 1974 prize shared with Claude and Palade, the Jamieson three-minute pulse on guinea-pig pancreas four sections later. This is the only detail in a historically meticulous chapter that is wrong, and it is the kind of thing a reader checks.

What I would do: "at Louvain", or drop the city.

Confidence: good but not certain — I am working from memory, not a source, so verify before editing.

## Would improve

### 9. Three definitions in one paragraph, in both chapters, in the same shape

Chapter 2 §2.7 puts all four levels of protein structure in a single ~200-word paragraph with bold run-ins: *The primary structure is… The secondary structure is… The tertiary structure is… The quaternary structure exists only…*. Chapter 3 §3.7 does the same with the junctions: *A tight junction is… A desmosome is… A gap junction is…*, one paragraph, preceded by the announcement "three kinds of junction do three different jobs, and the distinctions are worth getting exactly right."

These are the two flattest passages in the two chapters, and they are flat the same way: a list with the bullets filed off, a paragraph that enumerates where chapter 1 would have argued. Compare chapter 1 §1.4, which has the same job to do for cell theory and gives the three claims an `<ol>` after arguing its way there through Hooke, Leeuwenhoek, Schleiden, Schwann and Virchow.

Both also open with throat-clearing. "A polypeptide is not yet a protein. It becomes one by folding, and biologists describe the fold in four levels" announces a structure instead of using it; "the distinctions are worth getting exactly right" tells the reader the next paragraph is important rather than making it so.

What I would do: in §2.7, break the four levels into two paragraphs organised by *what holds them* rather than by their numbers — backbone hydrogen bonds make the local patterns, side chains make the whole shape — because that is the argument the section is actually making and the numbering is only nomenclature. In §3.7, give the three junctions three short paragraphs, each opening on the observation rather than the definition (a sheet that holds urine; a skin that blisters; a heart that beats as one), which is exactly the move the sort activity below already makes and makes well.

Judgement call, but I am confident these are the weakest two paragraphs.

### 10. Chapter 3 §3.6 states the filament table and then paraphrases every row

The section says "they are told apart most simply by diameter", gives a three-row table with diameter, subunit and jobs, and then spends three paragraphs restating each row with the same facts in the same order. Chapter 1's organelle table is not paraphrased afterwards, and does not need to be.

The paragraphs are not wasted — each has something the table cannot hold (the crawling leading edge, keratin blistering, dynamic instability) — but they read as duty until they get there. Cut the overlap and open each on the thing the table could not say.

### 11. Chapter 2 §2.3 says carbon dioxide "barely interacts with anything"; chapter 2 §2.4 has it acidifying the ocean

> §2.3: "as a whole the molecule is neutral and dull, and it is a gas that barely interacts with anything"
> §2.4: "every cell makes carbon dioxide, which becomes an acid in water" … "the ocean has taken up roughly a quarter of the carbon dioxide humans have released"

The point §2.3 is making — that CO₂'s bond polarities cancel, so the molecule is nonpolar — is right and well made, and "bend the molecule straight in your imagination and you lose every property in this section at once" is a lovely sentence. But "barely interacts with anything" is not what nonpolar means, and CO₂ is about seventy times more soluble in water than oxygen and reacts with it. A reader who takes §2.3 at its word will not believe §2.4.

Fix: "so the molecule as a whole is uncharged, and no water molecule can get a grip on either end of it" keeps the contrast and costs nothing.

### 12. The key idea on buffers gives back what the section carefully won

> `<tb-key>` "A buffer does not stop acid being added. It stops the acid being felt, until it is used up."

The section itself is excellent on this, and its figcaption is exactly right: "a buffer does not stop acid arriving, it postpones the consequences until it runs out." The key idea then sharpens "postpones" into "stops… being felt", which is the very misconception the section was built to prevent — the buffered curve is shallow, not flat, and the pH moves continuously along the buffering region.

A key idea is the sentence a reader carries away. This one is the only place in either chapter where the summary is less accurate than the prose it summarises. "It slows the fall, until it is used up" costs one word.

### 13. Chapter 2 §2.7 — "most human proteins hold their shape up to about 40 °C"

> "Most human proteins hold their shape up to about 40 °C and begin to fail not far above it, which is part of why a high fever is dangerous"

Measured thermal stability across the human proteome puts the median melting point nearer 50 °C, and the danger of a fever at 40–41 °C is not mainly bulk denaturation — the heat-shock response is a stress response, not a rescue from general unfolding. The hedge "part of why" is doing real work and saves this from being simply false, but the number invites the reader to think a fever cooks them.

The egg-white sentence beside it is exact (60–65 °C) and does the teaching job on its own. I would either raise the number or drop the fever and keep "much of why boiling sterilises a soup", which is unimpeachable.

Judgement call; confident about the proteome number, less sure how much you want to say about fevers in a chemistry chapter.

### 14. Figure 2.7 arrives a whole section before the prose asks for it

`fig-polymer` sits in §2.6, after the condensation and hydrolysis paragraph. The only citation is in §2.7: "Amino acids join by condensation, exactly as in Figure 2.7". `npm run check` is satisfied, because the figure is cited *somewhere*.

So the reader meets a large interactive figure in §2.6 with no invitation, and is invited to it a section later, by which time they have scrolled past it. Chapter 1 cites every figure in the paragraph immediately before or immediately after it, without exception, and so does the rest of chapter 2.

Fix: one sentence at the end of §2.6's condensation paragraph — the figure has a monomer switch and running it forwards and backwards is the point, so say so there. (Chapter 3 has a milder case: `fig-secretion` sits in §3.4 and is cited two paragraphs later at "Figure 3.4 traces the whole route and lets you break it". That one still lands in the same section and reads fine; I mention it only so the pattern is visible.)

### 15. Chapter 2 §2.8 is an appendix, not a section

It is the only section in either chapter with no figure and no key idea, it carries two objectives against the chapter's average of four, and it is where the chapter ends. The material is right and "Chapter 5 spends that currency; Chapter 8 reads the archive" is a fine last line, but a chapter that has built eight sections of momentum stops on its quietest one.

Options, cheapest first: give it a key idea, so the reader leaves on a beat; or reach for what is already earned and make the section's argument the one it half-states — that the pairing rule is §2.2's weak-bond arithmetic solving a problem that has no other solution, strong enough in aggregate to survive a lifetime and weak enough for an enzyme to open at body temperature. That paragraph is already there and is the best thing in the section; it could be the section.

### 16. Chapter 2 uses "organic" twice without defining it, in the chapter that exists to kill the misconception

"In water and in every organic molecule" (table, §2.1) and "organic chemistry would be dull" (§2.5). The word arrives with no gloss, and it is the one word in this chapter that carries a live folk meaning — *organic* as *alive*, or *natural*. §2.5 is the natural home for the correction and the story is short and good (Wöhler making urea from ammonium cyanate in 1828, and organic chemistry becoming the chemistry of carbon rather than the chemistry of life). The chapter's own opening move — "Nothing in the frame is alive. Every piece of it is ordinary chemistry" — is the same point made about the wrong noun.

### 17. Prerequisites that send a struggling reader sideways

Two edges look wrong for the queue's purpose, which is to work on a missing foundation rather than on something built on top of the failure:

- `denaturation` ← `blood-ph` (ch2). Denaturation by pH needs the pH scale and what charge does to a side chain — `ph-scale`. `blood-ph` is specifically "the range blood is held in, the buffer pair, and what the lungs and kidneys contribute", so a reader who fumbles a denaturation question is sent to practise renal bicarbonate handling.
- `magnification-resolution` ← `resolution-limits` (ch3 ← ch1). Chapter 1's `resolution-limits` is "say which instrument can resolve a given structure, and why an ordinary light microscope stops near 200 nm" — which is chapter 3's `abbe-limit`, the objective two steps *downstream*. So the graph makes the simple distinction (magnification is not resolution) depend on the derived fact, and a reader who fails `abbe-limit` is walked back through `magnification-resolution` to something that tests `abbe-limit` again.

Fix: `denaturation` ← `['protein-levels', 'ph-scale']`; `magnification-resolution` ← `['cell-scale']` and `abbe-limit` ← `['magnification-resolution', 'resolution-limits']`.

Also, `isomers` (ch2) promises "distinguish structural, cis-trans and mirror-image isomers" and the prose teaches all three cases but never uses the phrase *structural isomer*. An item written to the objective can ask for a name the chapter never gave.

## Verdict

**Chapter 2 — ship with the listed fixes.** It is at chapter 1's standard on substance: every number I could check is right, the argument from bond strength to weak-bond specificity to protein folding is genuinely a book's argument rather than a syllabus, and §2.2 and §2.3 are as good as anything in chapter 1. What it needs before it ships is small and mostly local — the reversed equilibrium direction in §2.4, the untaught strandedness behind `dna-vs-rna`, the "In preparation" card, and the two prerequisites pointed at the wrong foundation. What would lift it from good to chapter 1's *voice* is narrower still: the four-levels paragraph rewritten as an argument, §2.8 given a beat to end on, and the CO₂ sentence squared with §2.4. It cannot actually ship until it has an `items.js`, which is the gate's business, not mine.

**Chapter 3 — ship with the listed fixes, and it is the stronger chapter of the two.** §3.1's insistence that the instrument came before the biology, §3.2's willingness to make the reader do arithmetic, and above all §3.5's refusal to tell endosymbiosis as a settled story are the best writing in the book so far, and §3.3's "One old claim should be dropped" is the sentence I would point at to explain what this book is for. Two things hold it back and both are fixable in an afternoon. The first is that two of its claims are wrong in the places it is proudest of — the diffusion crossover contradicts its own table, and the mitochondrial division machinery is host-derived — and a chapter that teaches evidence-auditing has to survive being audited. The second is the seam: chapter 3 does not know chapter 2 exists, uses none of its words, and hangs none of its objectives on it, which costs the book its continuity and costs the study queue every edge it should have between the chemistry and the cell. Fix those, give it an `items.js` and its four missing figures, and it is the best chapter in the book.
