### 1. §7.6 (`yield`) — the fraction in "2.5 ATP per NADH" is put down to leak; it is in the proton counts, and the measured 2.5 is already corrected for leak

Location: `index.html` line 300: "The measurement is lower than the machinery predicts, and the difference is not error. It is leak." Line 302: "**The ATP made per NADH is not a stoichiometry. It is an efficiency**, and an efficiency is not a whole number ... A chemical equation can have integer coefficients; a leaky machine cannot." The same cause is repeated in the key, line 332 ("those two conversion figures are efficiencies rather than stoichiometries, because the membrane leaks and because moving the ATP out costs a proton"), key idea line 463 ("efficiencies and not stoichiometries, because the membrane leaks"), check q3's explanation, line 511 ("they are efficiencies rather than stoichiometries, because the membrane leaks"), and the bank's header, `items.js` lines 24–25 ("because ATP per carrier is an efficiency and not a stoichiometry").

What is wrong: line 298 has just derived the fraction from the machinery: ten protons per NADH, divided by 3.7 per ATP delivered to the cytosol, is 2.7. That is a stoichiometry — a ratio of two proton counts that do not divide evenly — and it has no leak in it. A perfectly tight membrane with an eight-subunit ring would still give 2.7, so "a leaky machine cannot [have whole numbers]" puts the cause of the fraction in the wrong place. Nor is the measured 2.5 "the machinery minus leak". Hinkle's group corrected their values for leak before reporting them, by measuring respiration against membrane potential; their "corrected values were close to 1, 0.5, and 1 at the three coupling sites", which sum to 2.5. Hinkle's 2005 review says "The fractional values result from the coupling ratios of proton transport", and that variability of coupling "is generally not significant". The small gap left between 2.7 and 2.5 is not pinned on any one cause: Watt et al. call 2.7 and 1.6 "close to experimental values of 2.5 and 1.5", and Ferguson lists leak as one of several reasons a given measurement might come in lower. Leak itself is real and large in a living cell — about a fifth of the oxygen a resting animal's mitochondria use pays for it (Rolfe & Brown), and coupling efficiency is "approximately 70% in hepatocytes and up to 90% in other cells" (Brand & Nicholls) — but it is a loss the cell takes on top of the ratio, not the reason the ratio is a fraction. The claim is the section's bolded sentence, is repeated in the key, a key idea and a check, and is graded in a dozen item fields (finding 46). The objective itself, `p-o-ratio` line 297, is already framed as proton counts and is right.

Evidence: Hinkle, Kumar, Resetar & Harris, *Biochemistry* 30:3576 (1991), PMID 2012815; Hinkle, *Biochim. Biophys. Acta* 1706:1 (2005), PMID 15620362 ("Values of about 2.5 with NADH-linked substrates and 1.5 with succinate are consistent with most reports"); Watt, Montgomery, Runswick, Leslie & Walker, *PNAS* 107:16823 (2010), PMC2947889 (the ring of eight, 2.7 protons per ATP at the synthase, 3.7 delivered); Ferguson, *PNAS* 107:16755 (2010), PMC2947903 ("The actual values might in any particular circumstance be lower than 2.7 and 1.6 for several reasons, including proton leakage or because the protonmotive force might be simultaneously driving more than one process"); Brand & Nicholls, *Biochem. J.* 435:297 (2011), PMC3076726; Rolfe & Brown, *Physiol. Rev.* 77:731 (1997). OpenStax 7.4 says the yield varies because the protons pumped vary between species, and does not blame leak.

Fix, line 300: "Now measure it instead of calculating it, which is what Peter Hinkle and others did over three decades of careful work, correcting for the protons that leak back: the answer is about 2.5 ATP per NADH and about 1.5 per FADH₂. That is close to the machinery's 2.7 and 1.6, and the fraction is the machinery's own: ten protons out and 3.7 back in do not divide evenly." Line 302: "**The ATP made per NADH is a ratio of two proton counts**, and nothing obliges it to be a whole number. What a living cell actually gets is less again, because the inner membrane is not perfectly tight: in a resting body about a fifth of the oxygen the mitochondria use pays for protons that came back through the membrane instead of the synthase, and that share is not the same in a heart as in a liver, or in one mitochondrion at two different rates." Key (line 332) and key idea (line 463): "...and those two conversion figures are not whole numbers, because the proton counts do not divide evenly; a living cell gets somewhat less, because the membrane leaks." q3's explanation: "...the measured values are about 2.5 and 1.5, which is what the proton counts predict." Header lines 24–25: "because the protons per ATP do not divide evenly into the protons per carrier".

Severity: **error** (the section's bolded claim puts the cause of the fraction in the wrong place, and it is graded in a check, the key, a key idea and a dozen item fields).

Touches: objectives `p-o-ratio` (statement right as it stands), `why-not-38`, `yield-arithmetic`; check q3; items per finding 46.

### 2. §7.6 (`yield`) — "the faster the chain runs, the steeper the gradient and the more leaks back" runs backwards for the case §7.8 teaches

Location: `index.html` line 300: "the faster the chain runs, the steeper the gradient and the more leaks back." Repeated in four items (finding 47).

What is wrong: §7.8 (line 413) teaches respiratory control: the chain runs faster because ATP is being spent, the synthase lets protons back in, and the gradient falls. In that ordinary, demand-driven case a faster chain runs on a *lower* gradient, and because the leak rises steeply with voltage, leak takes a *smaller* share of the flow. Its share is largest when the cell spends little ATP and the gradient stands high; with no ATP being made at all it is the whole of the respiration. "Faster, steeper, more leak" is true only when the chain is pushed faster by extra fuel at unchanged demand, which the sentence does not say and the chapter never describes.

Evidence: Brand & Nicholls 2011 (above): "Since the proton leak rate is voltage-dependent"; adding "a small amount of ADP, allowing the ATP synthase to function, pmf to drop and electron transport to accelerate"; the coupling efficiency "varies with ATP demand (it drops to zero in state 4o)". The chapter's own §7.8 on respiratory control.

Fix: "...and some protons find their way back across without turning anything. The leak grows with the gradient, so it takes its largest share when the cell is spending little ATP and the gradient stands high, and its smallest when the cell is working hard."

Severity: **error** (backwards for the case the chapter itself teaches, and graded). Touches: `p-o-ratio`, `respiratory-control`; items per finding 47.

### 3. §7.6 (`yield`) — "The old figures were not measurements at all"

### 4. §7.6 (`yield`) — the cell's own efficiency, 56 per cent, is thrown out on a false ground

Location: `index.html` line 326: "Quote that one, because both figures are standard-state figures and the comparison is therefore a fair one. It is tempting to use the cell's own 50 kilojoules an ATP from Section 5.3 instead, which gives 1600 kilojoules and 56 per cent, but that prices the product at the cell's concentrations while pricing the fuel at nobody's, and mixing the two is how a good many surprising efficiencies get published." Line 328: "The other 66 per cent leaves as heat". Key, line 332: "About a third of the fuel's free energy is captured"; key idea line 464: "About a third of glucose's free energy is captured as ATP."

What is wrong: the warning against mixing is right; the conclusion drawn from it is not, because the fuel at the cell's own concentrations is worth almost exactly the standard 2870 kilojoules, so the 56 per cent mixes nothing. At 37 °C, RT is 2.577 kJ/mol, and ΔG = ΔG°′ + RT ln([CO₂]⁶ / ([glucose][O₂]⁶)). At 5 mM glucose, carbon dioxide at 0.06 atm and oxygen at 0.05 atm, the correction is +16 kJ, so the fuel is worth about 2854 kJ/mol. At a tissue's lower oxygen (0.005 atm) and 0.1 mM glucose it is about 2808. Either way 1600/2850 is 56 per cent, the figure the chapter computes and then discards. Both figures are fair: 34 per cent at standard prices on both sides, about 56 at the cell's prices on both sides. Only a mix of the two would be unfair. Line 328's "The other 66 per cent" and the key's "About a third" then give the standard-price figure as if it were what a cell does.

Evidence: the arithmetic above, mine, from the chapter's own 2870 and 50 kJ (Section 5.3) and the concentrations stated; the exact oxygen and glucose inside a working cell vary, which moves the fuel's value by tens of kilojoules and the efficiency by about one point. OpenStax 7.4 gives "about 34 percent" and says it is what living systems extract, which is the same standard-price figure presented as the living one.

Fix, line 326: "...about 34 per cent. That compares two standard-state figures, and it is a fair comparison. The cell's own price of an ATP from Section 5.3, about 50 kilojoules, gives 1600 kilojoules, and at the cell's own concentrations of glucose, oxygen and carbon dioxide the fuel is worth about 2850, hardly different from the standard figure: about 56 per cent. That is fair too. What is not fair is to price the ATP at the cell's concentrations and the fuel at standard ones, and that mixing is how a good many surprising efficiencies get published. Either way a cell is doing better than a petrol engine, which manages about a quarter." Line 328: "The rest — two-thirds at standard prices, under half at the cell's own — leaves as heat". Key (line 332) and key idea (line 464): "About a third of the fuel's free energy is captured at standard prices, and more than half at the cell's own; the rest is the heat you are making now."

Severity: **error** (the chapter rejects the in-cell figure for a reason that is false, and grades the rejection, finding 49). Touches: `respiration-efficiency`.

### 5. §7.6 (`yield`) — the malate–aspartate shuttle is not reversible, and running one way is not what the glycerol 3-phosphate shuttle buys

Location: `index.html` line 307, the malate–aspartate shuttle: "worth the full 2.5. It is reversible, so it only runs when the matrix has empty carriers to take them." Line 308, the glycerol 3-phosphate shuttle: "It costs one ATP per NADH, and it buys speed: it runs in one direction only and does not stop when the matrix is loaded."

What is wrong: the malate–aspartate shuttle runs one way, inwards. Its aspartate–glutamate carrier swaps aspartate out for glutamate plus a proton in, and the membrane potential drives it. That is why it can load a matrix whose NADH/NAD⁺ ratio stands well above the cytosol's. Borst's 2020 review: "The present consensus is that the MAS is irreversible." So both shuttles run in one direction, and the contrast the chapter draws between them cannot be reversibility. What the glycerol 3-phosphate shuttle buys is independence from the matrix's own carriers: it hands the electrons to ubiquinone from the membrane's outer face, past complex I, however reduced the matrix is. The malate–aspartate shuttle is also not quite free. Its driving proton is one of the ten a matrix NADH pumps, so a shuttled NADH is worth about nine-tenths of a matrix one; Mookerjee et al. have it "driven by re-entry of 2 of the 20 subsequently translocated H⁺" per glucose. "The full 2.5" is the usual textbook simplification and is defensible; "reversible, so it only runs when the matrix has empty carriers" is not.

Evidence: Borst, *IUBMB Life* 72:2241 (2020), PMC7693074; LaNoue & Tischler, *J. Biol. Chem.* 249:7522 (1974), the carrier's electrogenic exchange; Mookerjee, Gerencser, Nicholls & Brand, *J. Biol. Chem.* 292:7189 (2017), PMC5409486, which also gives the glycerol 3-phosphate route as six protons per NADH, matching the chapter's 1.5.

Fix, line 307: "...so the electrons arrive on a matrix NAD⁺ and are worth almost the full 2.5. It is driven one way, inwards, because the aspartate leaving the matrix is swapped for glutamate and a proton coming in. That proton is its only cost, a tenth of what the NADH is worth, and it lets the shuttle load a matrix whose carriers are already more reduced than the cytosol's." Line 308: "...and are worth 1.5. It costs one ATP per NADH, and what it buys is a route that does not depend on the matrix's own carriers: it hands the electrons straight to ubiquinone, however loaded the matrix is."

Severity: **error** (a false mechanism, graded in `i-shuttle-cost-2`'s rubric, finding 50). Touches: `shuttle-cost`.

### 6. §7.7 (`anaerobic`) — "fermentation produces no ATP at all" is stated of every fermentation, and is true of the two the chapter describes

Location: `index.html` line 342: "**fermentation produces no ATP at all.** The two ATP a fermenting cell gets per glucose are glycolysis's". Also: the key, line 379, and key idea line 465 ("it yields no ATP of its own"); glossary `fermentation`, `glossary.js` line 59 ("It yields no ATP of its own: the two from glycolysis are the whole of the return."); objective `fermentation-purpose`, `objectives.js` line 334 ("explain what is wrong with describing it as a way of producing energy"); `FIGURES.md` line 105, which makes it one of the five sentences "the whole chapter turns on"; `items.js` header lines 20–21. The section defines fermentation as "any pathway that regenerates NAD⁺ by handing the electrons of NADH to an organic molecule the cell has made itself", and line 344 says there are "more than a dozen fermentations in nature".

What is wrong: for lactate and alcohol fermentation, the step after glycolysis makes no ATP, and the sentence is right about them. But the chapter states it of fermentation as it defines it, and many fermentations make ATP after glycolysis. The mixed-acid and butyric fermentations of bacteria turn acetyl-CoA into acetyl phosphate and then into acetate, or butyryl-CoA into butyrate, with a kinase that makes an ATP (Thauer, Jungermann & Decker, Table 3, "Reactions coupled with SLP"). Some lactic acid bacteria also add to their gradient by exporting lactate together with protons (ten Brink & Konings). A reader who learns "fermentation produces no ATP at all" as a definition will be wrong about most bacterial fermentations. The point underneath — fermentation is for regenerating NAD⁺, and in the familiar two the step itself is a loss — stands and is worth keeping. It needs only its scope.

Evidence: Thauer, Jungermann & Decker, *Bacteriol. Rev.* 41:100 (1977), PMC413997, Table 3 (acetate kinase, butyrate kinase); ten Brink & Konings, *J. Bacteriol.* 152:682 (1982), PMC221516 ("the carrier-mediated efflux of lactate supplies a significant quantity of metabolic energy"). OpenStax 7.5 gives the same definition ("Processes that use an organic molecule to regenerate NAD⁺ from NADH are collectively referred to as fermentation") and describes only the lactic acid and alcohol fermentations; it makes no universal claim about ATP.

Fix, line 342: "...and it is worth being blunt about what follows: **in the lactate and alcohol fermentations, the step after glycolysis makes no ATP at all.** The two ATP a fermenting muscle or yeast gets per glucose are glycolysis's... (Some bacterial fermentations squeeze another ATP or so out after glycolysis; none comes anywhere near what respiration makes.)" Key (line 379), key idea (line 465) and glossary (line 59): "in the lactate and alcohol fermentations it yields no ATP of its own". Objective (line 334): "Say what fermentation is for, and explain why, in the lactate and alcohol fermentations, the step after glycolysis makes no ATP of its own." `FIGURES.md` line 105: "that in the lactate and alcohol fermentations the fermentation step yields no ATP of its own". Header lines 20–21: "...and in the lactate and alcohol fermentations the fermentation step itself makes none."

Severity: **error** (a universal set in bold, in the key, a key idea, the glossary, an objective and `FIGURES.md`'s list of load-bearing sentences, and true only of the two fermentations the chapter names).

Touches: objectives `fermentation-purpose` (statement), `two-fermentations`, `anaerobic-respiration`; Figure 7.4's brief (see the figure notes); items per finding 51.

### 7. §7.7 (`anaerobic`) — a yeast can take its ethanol back

Location: `index.html` line 348: "For every glucose, two carbons leave the cell as gas and four as alcohol, and neither can be got back. The yeast is poisoning its own medium, and eventually itself". Margin note, line 350: "A yeast in a barrel has nowhere to send anything. Lactate is what you make when somebody else will clear it up; ethanol is what you make when nobody will." Key, line 379: "a yeast makes ethanol and cannot take it back".

What is wrong: baker's and brewer's yeast make ethanol while the sugar lasts and then consume it. When the glucose runs out they switch on a second alcohol dehydrogenase, Adh2, which runs the other way, and respire the ethanol through acetaldehyde and acetyl-CoA. This is the diauxic shift, and the make, accumulate and consume strategy is the standard account of why *Saccharomyces* ferments sugar in the presence of oxygen at all: Thomson et al. resurrected the ancestral enzyme and showed the duplication that made Adh2 a consumer ("Yeast later consumes the accumulated ethanol, exploiting Adh2"). The yeast needs oxygen to respire it, so in a sealed barrel it cannot; but "neither can be got back" and "nobody will [clear it up]" are false of the yeast itself. The carbon dioxide is lost; the ethanol is not. The contrast the margin note draws survives with one clause changed: lactate is cleared by other organs, and ethanol is cleared later by the yeast that made it.

Evidence: Thomson, Gaucher, Burgan, De Kee, Li, Aris & Benner, *Nat. Genet.* 37:630 (2005), "Resurrecting ancestral alcohol dehydrogenases from yeast". The chapter's own line 346 notes that lactate "can be turned back into pyruvate later"; ethanol cannot be turned back into pyruvate, because pyruvate decarboxylase's step is irreversible, and that is the true form of the contrast.

Fix, line 348: "...two carbons leave the cell as gas and four as alcohol. The gas is gone for good. The alcohol the yeast can use again, but only later and only with oxygen: when the sugar runs out it switches on a second alcohol dehydrogenase that runs the other way and respires the ethanol it made. Until then it is poisoning its own medium, and eventually itself: ..." Margin note, line 350: "...A yeast in a barrel has nowhere to send anything, so its ethanol stays in its own medium until the yeast itself comes back for it, once the sugar is gone and if there is oxygen then. Lactate is what you make when somebody else will clear it up; ethanol is what you make when you will have to clear it up yourself." Key, line 379: "a yeast makes ethanol, which cannot be turned back into pyruvate".

Severity: **error** (false of the organism the section is about, and graded in `i-two-fermentations-3`, finding 52). Touches: `two-fermentations`.

### 8. §7.7 (`anaerobic`) — "The machinery is the same", and the methanogens' 15 kilojoules is "close to the least any organism has been shown to get by on"

Location: `index.html` line 364: "Anaerobic respiration is respiration in the full sense ... The machinery is the same. Only the terminal acceptor differs, and since the yield is the size of the fall, an acceptor that sits higher up gives less." Line 377: "sulfate reducers living on 19 kilojoules a pair. Methanogens in a cow's rumen, a rice paddy and a landfill are living on 15, which is close to the least any organism has been shown to get by on". The key, line 379: "the same machinery ending on nitrate, sulfate or carbon dioxide, and yielding less only because those sit higher on the ladder"; key idea line 467: "the same chain, gradient and synthase ... and it yields less only because those acceptors sit higher on the ladder". Glossary `terminal-electron-acceptor`, line 47 ("get less because those sit higher up"), and `anaerobic-respiration`, line 62 ("the machinery is the same as the aerobic kind, and the yield is lower only because the acceptor sits higher on the ladder"). `FIGURES.md` line 213 ("which is the simplification §7.7 makes in words ('the machinery is the same')") and its readout for sulfate and carbon dioxide ("these organisms live on other fuels than NADH and on much smaller gradients made by other machinery").

What is wrong: three things, one after another.
- **Same kind, not same machinery.** Anaerobic respiration uses the same *kind* of machinery — a chain in a membrane, a gradient, an ATP synthase — but rarely the same complexes. *E. coli*, which the section names, has no complex III at all; its nitrate chain runs from an NADH dehydrogenase through quinone to a nitrate reductase that moves charge by a quinone loop. Methanogens pump sodium with a methyl-transferase, and those without cytochromes use flavin-based electron bifurcation in the cytoplasm (Thauer et al. 2008). "The same chain, gradient and synthase" is not true of any of the organisms the section names.
- **Not "only" the acceptor.** The yield depends on the donor as well as the acceptor, and most of these organisms do not start from NADH. Most methanogens and many sulfate reducers take their electrons from hydrogen, at −0.41 volts. From hydrogen to carbon dioxide and methane is about 33 kJ per pair under standard conditions, and to sulfate about 38, roughly twice the table's 15 and 19 (Thauer, Jungermann & Decker 1977, Table 5; Thauer et al. 2008: "Most methanogenic archaea can reduce CO₂ with H₂ to methane"). Line 377 itself says the 19 and 15 are priced "exactly as in Section 7.4's table", from NADH, and then says the organisms are "living on" them.
- **"Close to the least".** The least energy any organism has been shown to live on is set per reaction, not per pair: about 20 kJ per mole of reaction, the price of moving a single ion across the membrane, a third of an ATP (Schink 1997). Syntrophic fermenters live near it. A per-pair figure priced from a donor the organism does not use cannot be compared with that, so the clause should go.

The `FIGURES.md` readout's "much smaller gradients" has the same fault. The gradient need not be smaller. What is small is the number of ions moved per reaction.

Evidence: Thauer, Kaster, Seedorf, Buckel & Hedderich, *Nat. Rev. Microbiol.* 6:579 (2008), PMID 18587410; Thauer, Jungermann & Decker 1977 (above), Table 5 (H⁺/H₂ −0.414 V, CO₂/CH₄ −0.244, sulfate to sulfide about −0.217 as the eight-electron mean); Schink, *Microbiol. Mol. Biol. Rev.* 61:262 (1997), on the minimum energy quantum. OpenStax 7.5 has methanogens reducing carbon dioxide "to oxidize NADH", which is the same simplification (see the disagreement table).

Fix, line 364: "The machinery is the same kind — a chain in a membrane, a gradient and an ATP synthase — though the complexes themselves differ from one organism to the next. What is new here is the terminal acceptor, and since the yield is the size of the fall, an acceptor that sits higher up gives less from the same donor." Line 377: "...sulfate reducers living on 19 kilojoules a pair, priced from NADH as the table is. Methanogens in a cow's rumen, a rice paddy and a landfill get 15 on the same pricing. Most of them, and many sulfate reducers, take their electrons from hydrogen instead, which starts a little lower and roughly doubles both figures. Their waste product is the second most important greenhouse gas." Key (line 379), key idea (line 467) and the two glossary entries: "the same kind of machinery ending on nitrate, sulfate or carbon dioxide, and yielding less from the same donor because those sit higher on the ladder". `FIGURES.md` line 213: "(the machinery is the same kind)"; the readout: "these organisms take their electrons from fuels other than NADH, mostly hydrogen, and move only a few ions per reaction".

Severity: **error** (three false clauses in the section's defining sentence, its key, a key idea, two glossary entries and the figure brief). Eleven of the fourteen disagreements go the chapter's way (one of them, glycogen, the bank's rather than the prose's). ## Verdict

**Do not ship the prose until findings 1–14 are applied.** Most of the fourteen errors are single sentences. Two need a paragraph rewritten. The first is §7.6's account of why the ATP per NADH is not a whole number (lines 298–302, above all line 300's "It is leak" and line 302's "not a stoichiometry.
