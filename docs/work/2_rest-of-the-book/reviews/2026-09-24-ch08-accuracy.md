### 1. §8.4 (`fork`) and check q4 — "strands running the same way ... would give two continuous strands" is false: forks leave an origin in both directions

Location: `index.html` line 192, last two sentences: "It follows from the two facts, and changing either one would remove it: strands running the same way, or a polymerase able to add at either end, would each give two continuous strands. Figure 8.3 lets you make both changes." Line 460, q4's explanation: "The lagging strand follows from two facts, and removing either removes it: parallel strands, or a polymerase that could add at either end, would both give two continuous strands." The same claim is on the page twice more: Figure 8.3's alt text (line 194), "with either one the fragments disappear", and its caption (line 195), "change either of the two rules ... and watch the pieces disappear".

What is wrong: the chapter itself says (line 190) that two forks leave every origin in opposite directions. If both templates ran the same way, the two new strands at the fork travelling one way would both have their 3′ ends pointing into it and both be continuous; but at the fork leaving the same origin the other way, both 3′ ends would point away and both strands would have to be made in pieces. Parallel strands move the lagging strand from one fork to the other; they do not remove it. Only a polymerase able to add at either end removes it everywhere. This is the item writer's prose point 1, and it is right. It reaches the figure too (notes at the end).

Evidence: the geometry of a bidirectional origin, as drawn in Alberts et al., *Molecular Biology of the Cell*, 6th ed., ch. 5 (the replication bubble with two forks, each with one leading and one lagging strand) and Kornberg & Baker, *DNA Replication*, 2nd ed., ch. 1; the argument needs nothing past the chapter's own two facts plus line 190.

Fix, line 192: "It is not a quirk of the machinery. It follows from the two facts. A polymerase able to add at either end would make both new strands continuously at every fork. Strands running the same way would only move the problem: at the fork travelling one way both new strands would grow towards it, but at the fork leaving the same origin in the other direction both would have to be made in pieces. Figure 8.3 lets you make both changes." Line 460: "The lagging strand follows from two facts. A polymerase that could add at either end would make both strands continuously; parallel strands would not, because the fork leaving the origin the other way would then have both its new strands made in pieces." Alt text line 194, the clause "and with either one the fragments disappear": "and with the second the fragments disappear at every fork, while with the first they disappear from one fork only to double at the fork leaving the origin the other way" — or whatever the built figure actually shows, which the figure pass must confirm. Severity: **error**. Touches: `lagging-strand` (prose, q4), Figure 8.3's alt text and caption, `FIGURES.md` lines 202, 218 and 236.

### 2. §8.5 (`fidelity`) — xeroderma pigmentosum needs two broken copies, not "a broken copy"

Location: `index.html` line 258: "People who inherit a broken copy of any of several genes of this second route have xeroderma pigmentosum."

What is wrong: xeroderma pigmentosum is autosomal recessive. A person with one broken copy of an XP gene and one working copy is a carrier with normal repair and normal skin; the disease needs both copies broken. The sentence says one is enough, and eight lines later the Lynch syndrome card (line 286, "inherited a faulty copy of a mismatch repair gene") correctly uses the same words for a condition where one copy is enough, so a reader will take the two as the same kind of inheritance.

Evidence: Kraemer & DiGiovanna, "Xeroderma Pigmentosum", *GeneReviews* (University of Washington, updated 2022): autosomal recessive; heterozygotes are asymptomatic. OpenStax 14.6 says only that XP patients have "a defect in the nucleotide excision repair enzymes", which is not wrong.

Fix: "People who inherit broken copies of any one of several genes of this second route, one from each parent, have xeroderma pigmentosum." Severity: **error** (false, and set beside a true sentence that uses the same words for dominant inheritance). Touches: `excision-repair`, `repair-choice`; the item side is checked in the items batch.

### 14. §8.6 line 299 — histones package any sequence, but not "equally well"

### 15. §8.6 line 299 — "Almost any change to them, it seems, is fatal" overstates what conservation shows

Location: `index.html` line 299, last sentence. `items.js` `i-packing-compared-3`, rubric point 6 (line 1721): "so almost any change seems to be fatal".

What is wrong: that H4 barely differs between a pea and a cow shows that changes have been removed by selection, not that they kill. Where it has been tested, many single changes are survivable. ### 18. `i-pairing-geometry-2`, explanation — the rare form offers cytosine's pattern, not "the pattern it normally offers adenine"

### 23. `i-origins-arithmetic-1` — the 12-minute option's number does not follow from its own reasoning

Location: `items.js` lines 1059–1060: "About 12 minutes, since each of the two parent strands is copied by a pair of forks of its own." Why: "Multiplies the forks. One origin gives two forks, and each fork copies both parent strands as it goes."

What is wrong: the error the option names does not produce its number. With a pair of forks for each parent strand, each fork still covers 3 million nucleotides of its strand and the answer stays at 50 minutes; with four forks sharing the circle it is 25 minutes, which is another option's number. Twelve minutes needs a second error on top (each fork also counting at double speed). So a reader who makes the stated mistake lands on a different option, and this one is ruled out by arithmetic rather than by the idea it is meant to test. ### 24. `i-proofreading-3`, rubric — proofreading can still reach a mismatch a nucleotide or two in

### 34. `i-c-value-paradox-1`, key — "the two plants have similar numbers of genes", where the chapter's table says the onion's number is not reliably known

### 36. `i-primer-needed-2` — two distractors a reader can discard without knowing which strand needs primers (the item writer's weak item)

## Notes kept for the figure pass

Read against the briefs in `FIGURES.md` at `cb447d7` and against the captions and alt texts on the page. Line numbers are `FIGURES.md`'s unless they say `index.html`. Each note is something to look for on the stage, not a finding yet.

- **8.1 `helix-lab`.** The brief draws "two backbone rails 2 nm apart" (line 116; the alt text at `index.html` line 106 says the same) and gives `pairWidthNm` "C1′ to C1′" (line 140). The helix is 2 nm across at its phosphates. The C1′ atoms across a Watson–Crick pair are only about 1.05 nm apart (from memory of standard structural tables). A bench that draws the rails 2 nm apart and each pair C1′ to C1′ will show every right pair about half as wide as the gap it sits in. The stage should either measure both to the same atoms or say which atoms the rails are. The alt text says a rare form "refuses its usual partner". If finding 11 is taken, the bench could also show the partner a rare form accepts: guanine for a rare thymine, thymine for a rare guanine.
- **8.2 `meselson-stahl`.** The brief (line 160) is right about heating, including the point most accounts miss: heated conservative DNA at generation 1 also gives heavy and light single strands in equal amounts. So heating separates the dispersive scheme from the other two, and not the semiconservative scheme from the conservative. Check that the built stage and its words say the same. Check too that single strands band at their own higher densities, as the brief says, with heated hybrid DNA giving two bands 0.015 g/cm³ apart, and that light, hybrid and heavy duplexes sit at 1.710, 1.717 and 1.724.
- **8.3 `replication-fork`.** Finding 1: the rule *Strands run the same way* (line 202) and `fragmentsStarted` "0 under either hypothetical rule" (line 218) are right only for a fork moving one way. At the fork leaving the origin the other way, both new strands would be made in fragments. If the stage shows one fork, it must say so and say what the other fork would do. If it shows a bubble, `fragmentsStarted` must not fall to 0 under that rule. Line 236's "watches it vanish" needs the same care, and so do the alt text and caption (`index.html` lines 194–195). Finding 5: chromosome 1 given "its real share of the cell's origins" (line 197) gets about 1,200 to 2,000 by the chapter's own numbers, so the stage will print thousands beside the prose's "hundreds" (`index.html` line 204). Line 236's "about a month" for chromosome 1 from one origin is right: 249 million pairs at two forks of 50 a second is about 29 days.
- **8.4 `chromosome-end`.** With the brief's defaults (line 245: 10,000 pairs at birth, 50 lost a division, a stop at about fifty divisions), the cell stops with 7,500 pairs left, three-quarters of where it began. The brief already requires the header to say the threshold was chosen. The stage should also say why a cell stops with so much left, which `index.html` line 323 explains: the shortest telomeres, not the average one, stop it. Otherwise the stop looks arbitrary.

## Verdict

**Ship after the five errors are fixed: findings 1 and 2 in the prose, and 16, 25 and 33 in the bank.**

Finding 1 matters most. The prose, q4's explanation, and Figure 8.3's alt text and caption all say that parallel strands would remove the lagging strand. They would only move it to the other fork, and two items build the claim in (finding 16). It is also in Figure 8.3's brief (the rule *Strands run the same way*), so it must be settled before that figure is built or checked. Finding 2: the chapter says one broken copy causes xeroderma pigmentosum, which needs two. The bank's other two errors each state something false in an explanation: that both daughters inherit an uncorrected mismatch (25), and that telomerase is the only way to keep an end (33).

The nine weak findings should go in the same pass. Two are in the chapter's material: the objective that asks for rates the chapter does not give (3), and the item graded on something the prose never teaches, that a base's rare form pairs with the wrong partner (11). Seven are in the bank: 23, 32, 34 and 36–39, the last four being the item writer's four weak items with the fixes adjudicated above. The 25 small findings are wording and can go in with them.

Beyond these, the chapter is careful and often better than its reference. Every disagreement with OpenStax goes its way, its arithmetic checks, and its cross-references say what the sections they name say.
